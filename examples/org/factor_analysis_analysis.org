#+begin_abstract
We fit exploratory Factor Analysis (Spearman 1904; Thurstone 1947) by
principal axis factoring, with an optional Kaiser-normalized varimax
rotation, using =datamunge='s C++ =stats::FactorAnalysis=, and report it
as a literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed
chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and figure in
this document. Unlike PCA -- which rotates the axes of /total/ variance
and keeps every component -- factor analysis posits a generative
latent-variable model, \(x = \mu + \Lambda f + \varepsilon\), that splits
each variable's variance into a /communality/ (shared, explained by a
handful of common factors) and a /uniqueness/ (variable-specific), and
models only the off-diagonal covariance. We verify the method on data
drawn from a /known/ two-factor model over six variables: it recovers the
true loadings to a maximum error of 0.0503 and its estimated
communalities correlate with the true ones at 0.9833. On a second dataset
whose two latent factors are correlated (\(\rho=0.6\)), the unrotated
solution collapses into an uninterpretable "general factor" that loads on
all six variables (loadings 0.82, 0.76, 0.74, 0.57, 0.56, 0.56); a
varimax rotation -- which provably leaves the model fit and communalities
unchanged -- restores interpretable simple structure, raising a
simple-structure index from 0.8393 to 0.8552. We then show the factor
model's defining property directly: reconstructing the observed
correlation matrix from two factors leaves an off-diagonal residual RMS
of just 0.0103, versus 0.0948 for a rank-2 PCA of the same data -- because
factor analysis, by design, spends its parameters on the /off/-diagonal
(shared) covariance and banishes variable-specific noise to a separate
diagonal \(\Psi\), while PCA must fit both at once. Finally, iris exposes
factor analysis's central practical constraint, one PCA never faces: with
only four variables, a two-factor model is over-parameterized
(identifiability degrees of freedom \(-1\)) and produces a Heywood case
(a communality of 1.0143, an impossible value clamped to zero
uniqueness) that never converges, while a one-factor model is identified
and converges in 22 iterations. We derive the factor model, the
communality/uniqueness split, the principal-axis-factoring iteration,
rotational indeterminacy and varimax, the regression factor scores, and
the identifiability count, tying each to the corresponding library call.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

This project's [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and
[[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA]] reports both answer a
descriptive question -- /what are the directions of greatest variance?/ --
by rotating the axes of the data and keeping as many as one likes. Factor
analysis, despite its superficial resemblance (it too eigendecomposes a
\(p \times p\) matrix and talks about "loadings"), answers a
fundamentally different, /generative/ question: /is there a small set of
unobserved common factors that could have produced the correlations we
see?/ It posits an explicit statistical model,

\[
x = \mu + \Lambda f + \varepsilon,
\]

in which each observed variable is a linear combination of a few latent
common factors \(f\) plus its own private noise \(\varepsilon\), and it
estimates that model rather than merely re-describing the data. The
consequence is a genuine conceptual split absent from PCA: each variable's
variance is partitioned into a /communality/ (the part the common factors
explain) and a /uniqueness/ (the part specific to that variable alone),
and only the shared part is modeled. Where PCA's first component is the
single axis of most variance -- however that variance is distributed --
factor analysis deliberately ignores each variable's private variance and
fits only what variables have in /common/.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.5.9) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives the factor model, the communality/uniqueness decomposition,
the principal-axis-factoring iteration =stats::FactorAnalysis= uses, the
rotational indeterminacy that makes rotation both necessary and harmless,
varimax, the regression factor scores, and the identifiability count that
governs how many factors a dataset can support -- each tied to the
specific method that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by
chunk. §4 reports the recovery of a known two-factor structure, a loadings
figure, the general-factor-versus-simple-structure rotation experiment,
the factor-analysis-versus-PCA correlation-reproduction comparison, and
iris's identifiability limits. §5 discusses when factor analysis is the
right tool and why small datasets so often defeat it. §6 concludes.

* Background: Factor Analysis

** Setup and notation

\(n\) observations \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^p\). For the
synthetic studies, \(p=6\) with \(n=500\); for iris, \(p=4\) with
\(n=150\) (=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=,
=Species= withheld exactly as in every companion report). Factor analysis
uses no response variable; the factors are latent and unobserved. By
default =FactorAnalysisOptions::standardize= is true, so the model is fit
on the correlation matrix -- every variable rescaled to unit variance,
making loadings, communalities and uniquenesses directly comparable
across variables.

** The factor model

The generative model is

\[
x = \mu + \Lambda f + \varepsilon, \qquad
f \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_k), \quad
\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Psi), \quad
\Psi = \operatorname{diag}(\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_p),
\]

with \(f\) and \(\varepsilon\) independent. Here \(\Lambda \in
\mathbb{R}^{p \times k}\) is the /loading matrix/ (=loadings()=, entry
\((j, c)\) is variable \(j\)'s loading on factor \(c\)), \(f\) is the
vector of \(k\) common factors shared across all \(p\) variables, and
\(\varepsilon\) is the /unique/ (variable-specific) noise, with a
/diagonal/ covariance \(\Psi\) -- the crucial structural assumption that
the only thing linking different variables is the common factors.
Because \(f\) and \(\varepsilon\) are independent, the model implies a
covariance of exactly

\[
\Sigma = \Lambda \Lambda^\top + \Psi .
\]

The single most important consequence hides in the off-diagonal:
\(\Psi\) is diagonal, so for \(a \neq b\),
\(\Sigma_{ab} = (\Lambda \Lambda^\top)_{ab}\) -- /every/ covariance
between distinct variables is carried by the common factors alone, and
none by the uniquenesses. Factor analysis is, at heart, a model of the
off-diagonal correlations; §4.4 measures exactly this.

** Communality and uniqueness

On the correlation scale (\(\Sigma_{jj} = 1\)), the diagonal of
\(\Sigma = \Lambda\Lambda^\top + \Psi\) reads

\[
1 = \underbrace{\sum_{c=1}^{k} \Lambda_{jc}^2}_{h_j^2 \;=\; \text{communality}}
    \;+\; \underbrace{\psi_j}_{\text{uniqueness}} .
\]

The /communality/ \(h_j^2\) (=communalities()=) is the fraction of
variable \(j\)'s variance explained by the common factors -- the sum of
its squared loadings -- and the /uniqueness/ \(\psi_j\)
(=uniquenesses()=) is the remainder, variance private to that variable.
This split is the whole point of factor analysis and has no counterpart
in PCA, where every scrap of variance, shared or private, is folded into
the components indiscriminately. A variable with a low communality is one
the common factors barely touch.

** Principal axis factoring

=FactorAnalysis= estimates \(\Lambda\) by /principal axis factoring/
(PAF), an iterative eigen-method. Rearranging the model covariance gives
\(\Lambda\Lambda^\top = \Sigma - \Psi\): the loadings diagonalize the
correlation matrix /with its diagonal replaced by the communalities/ (the
"reduced correlation matrix"), since replacing the 1's on the diagonal by
\(h_j^2 = 1 - \psi_j\) removes exactly the unique variances. But the
communalities are themselves unknown, so PAF alternates:

1. Replace the diagonal of the correlation matrix \(R\) with the current
   communalities to form the reduced matrix \(R^\ast\).
2. Eigendecompose \(R^\ast = \sum_c \lambda_c a_c a_c^\top\)
   (via =linalg::jacobi_eigen=, the same symmetric solver the
   [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA report]] uses) and read off the
   loadings from the top \(k\) eigenpairs,
   \(\Lambda_{\cdot c} = \sqrt{\lambda_c}\, a_c\).
3. Refresh each communality as the loadings' row sum of squares,
   \(h_j^2 \leftarrow \sum_c \Lambda_{jc}^2\).

iterating until the largest per-variable communality change drops below
=tol= (=iterations()= / =converged()= report the outcome). The starting
communalities are the /squared multiple correlations/,
\(h_j^2 = 1 - 1/(R^{-1})_{jj}\), the standard PAF initializer, computed
from a symmetric inverse of \(R\). Should a communality try to exceed 1 --
a /Heywood case/, corresponding to a negative uniqueness, which is
statistically impossible -- it is clamped, and §4.5 shows a real dataset
where this happens.

** Rotational indeterminacy and varimax

The factor model is only identified /up to an orthogonal rotation/. For
any \(k \times k\) orthogonal \(T\) (\(TT^\top = I\)), replacing
\(\Lambda\) by \(\Lambda T\) leaves the model covariance untouched,

\[
(\Lambda T)(\Lambda T)^\top = \Lambda T T^\top \Lambda^\top =
\Lambda\Lambda^\top ,
\]

and leaves every communality unchanged (row norms are rotation-invariant).
So infinitely many loading matrices fit /equally well/: rotation changes
nothing about the fit and everything about interpretability. This is not
a defect to be hidden but a freedom to be spent, and =FactorAnalysisOptions::rotation=
spends it on /simple structure/ -- loadings pushed toward either 0 or
\(\pm\)large, so each variable loads on as few factors as possible.
Varimax (Kaiser 1958) does this by choosing \(T\) to /maximize the
variance of the squared loadings/ within each factor,

\[
\max_{T} \; \sum_{c=1}^{k} \left[
\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p} \Lambda_{jc}^4 \;-\; \Big(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p} \Lambda_{jc}^2\Big)^2
\right],
\]

solved by sweeping pairwise planar rotations of the factor columns to
convergence, with Kaiser row-normalization so high- and low-communality
variables influence the rotation equally. §4.3 shows a case where this
transforms an uninterpretable fit into an obvious one.

** Factor scores

Because the factors are latent, an observation's factor values \(f\)
cannot be computed directly, only /estimated/. =scores()= uses the
Thomson /regression/ estimate, the conditional expectation of \(f\) given
the data under the model,

\[
\hat{f} = \Lambda^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu),
\]

so for the whole (standardized) data matrix \(Z\) the scores are
\(F = Z\,\Sigma^{-1}\Lambda\) -- one row of \(k\) estimated factor values
per observation, the factor-analytic analogue of PCA's scores, though
(unlike PCA's) an /estimate/ rather than an exact projection.

** How many factors can a dataset support?

Factor analysis cannot extract as many factors as PCA can components.
Counting parameters against the \(p(p+1)/2\) distinct entries of \(\Sigma\),
the model's identifiability degrees of freedom are

\[
\mathrm{df} = \frac{(p - k)^2 - (p + k)}{2},
\]

and a fit is only well-determined when \(\mathrm{df} \geq 0\). For
\(p=6, k=2\) this is \(4\) (comfortable); for \(p=4, k=2\) it is \(-1\)
(over-parameterized). This single inequality is why the six-variable
synthetic studies below behave and the four-variable iris fit in §4.5
does not -- a constraint PCA, which never tries to separate common from
unique variance, simply does not have.

** What we will report

Given the fits, we report the recovery of a known two-factor loading
structure, a loadings figure, the general-factor-versus-simple-structure
effect of rotation on correlated-factor data, the factor-model-versus-PCA
correlation-reproduction comparison, and iris's identifiability limits.

* Data and Methods

** The synthetic two-factor data

To check the method against a known ground truth -- impossible with real
data, where the true loadings are unobservable -- we generate \(n=500\)
observations of six variables from an explicit two-factor model. Two
independent standard-normal factors \(f_A, f_B\) drive the variables
through a block loading pattern: variables 1-3 load only on \(f_A\)
(loadings 0.85, 0.80, 0.75), variables 4-6 only on \(f_B\) (0.70, 0.65,
0.60), and each variable gets independent standard-normal noise scaled so
its total variance is 1. The true communalities are therefore the squared
loadings (0.7225, 0.64, 0.5625, 0.49, 0.4225, 0.36), and the true
loadings are withheld from the fit and used only afterward to score
recovery.

** The correlated-factor variant

The same block loadings, but with the two latent factors /correlated/
(\(\rho=0.6\); \(f_B = \rho f_A + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,g\)), so that every
variable becomes positively correlated with every other. This is the
setting -- ubiquitous in real psychometric data -- where an unrotated
solution produces a dominant, hard-to-interpret "general" factor, and
where rotation earns its keep; it is fit separately (seed 321) purely to
demonstrate rotation.

** The iris data

=datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns Fisher's 150 flowers measured on
four features, with =Species= withheld from fitting. With only \(p=4\)
variables, iris is deliberately included as the /hard/ case: §2.7's count
shows it cannot identify two factors, and §4.5 reports what happens when
one asks anyway.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::FactorAnalysis= for the model
(and =stats::PCA= for §4.4's comparison), =linalg::DenseMatrix= for the
correlation-reproduction arithmetic, and =plot::RPlot= for every figure.
No external statistics package is used.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** Includes, usings, and three scoring helpers

Three helpers used only to /score/ the fits after the fact: a Pearson
correlation, a "simple-structure index" (the mean fraction of each
variable's communality captured by its single dominant factor, in
\([1/k, 1]\); larger is simpler), and the off-diagonal residual RMS
between a correlation matrix and a low-rank reconstruction.

// Pearson correlation of two equal-length vectors.
static double pearson(const std::vector<double>& a, const std::vector<double>& b) {
    const std::size_t n = a.size();
    double ma = 0.0, mb = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) { ma += a[i]; mb += b[i]; }
    ma /= n; mb /= n;
    double num = 0.0, da = 0.0, db = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        const double xa = a[i] - ma, xb = b[i] - mb;
        num += xa * xb; da += xa * xa; db += xb * xb;
    }
    return num / std::sqrt(da * db);
}

// Mean fraction of each variable's communality captured by its single dominant factor -- a simple
// "simple structure" index in [1/k, 1]; larger means cleaner separation across factors.
static double simple_structure_index(const datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double>& L) {
    const std::size_t p = L.rows(), k = L.cols();
    double acc = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t j = 0; j < p; ++j) {
        double h2 = 0.0, mx = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t c = 0; c < k; ++c) { const double q = L(j, c) * L(j, c); h2 += q; mx = std::max(mx, q); }
        if (h2 > 0.0) acc += mx / h2;
    }
    return acc / static_cast<double>(p);
}

// RMS of the off-diagonal residuals of a correlation matrix R minus a rank-k reconstruction M.
static double offdiag_rms(const datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double>& R,
                          const datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double>& M) {
    const std::size_t p = R.rows();
    double ss = 0.0; std::size_t cnt = 0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < p; ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < p; ++j)
            if (i != j) { const double d = R(i, j) - M(i, j); ss += d * d; ++cnt; }
    return std::sqrt(ss / static_cast<double>(cnt));
}

*** Factor analysis versus PCA: reproducing the correlations

#+name: fa-vs-pca
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Factor analysis vs. PCA: reproducing the correlation matrix (synthetic) ===\n";
    {
        // Observed correlation matrix of the synthetic variables.
        const std::size_t p = 6;
        datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double> R(p, p, 0.0);
        std::vector<double> mean(p, 0.0), sd(p, 0.0);
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < p; ++j) {
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) mean[j] += cols[j][i];
            mean[j] /= n;
            double ss = 0.0;
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) ss += (cols[j][i] - mean[j]) * (cols[j][i] - mean[j]);
            sd[j] = std::sqrt(ss / (n - 1));
        }
        for (std::size_t a = 0; a < p; ++a)
            for (std::size_t b = 0; b < p; ++b) {
                double s = 0.0;
                for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
                    s += ((cols[a][i] - mean[a]) / sd[a]) * ((cols[b][i] - mean[b]) / sd[b]);
                R(a, b) = s / (n - 1);
            }

        // FA reconstruction: Lambda Lambda^T (off-diagonal common-variance model).
        datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double> FAhat(p, p, 0.0);
        for (std::size_t a = 0; a < p; ++a)
            for (std::size_t b = 0; b < p; ++b) {
                double s = 0.0;
                for (std::size_t c = 0; c < fa.num_factors(); ++c) s += fa.loadings()(a, c) * fa.loadings()(b, c);
                FAhat(a, b) = s;
            }

        // PCA rank-2 reconstruction of R: sum_{c<2} lambda_c v_c v_c^T.
        PCA pca(synth, vnames, PCAOptions{true, true});  // correlation-matrix PCA
        datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double> PChat(p, p, 0.0);
        for (std::size_t a = 0; a < p; ++a)
            for (std::size_t b = 0; b < p; ++b) {
                double s = 0.0;
                for (std::size_t c = 0; c < 2; ++c)
                    s += pca.explained_variance()[c] * pca.component_loadings(c)[a] * pca.component_loadings(c)[b];
                PChat(a, b) = s;
            }

        std::cout << "off-diagonal residual RMS, FA (2 factors):     " << offdiag_rms(R, FAhat) << "\n";
        std::cout << "off-diagonal residual RMS, PCA (2 components): " << offdiag_rms(R, PChat) << "\n";
        double sum_comm = 0.0;
        for (double c : fa.communalities()) sum_comm += c;
        std::cout << "sum of FA communalities: " << sum_comm << " of " << p << " total variance ("
                  << 100.0 * sum_comm / p << "% common, rest is variable-specific uniqueness)\n";
    }
#+end_src

#+end_example

On the correlated-factor data, the /unrotated/ solution is exactly the
uninterpretable case §2.5 warns about: its first factor is a "general"
factor that loads substantially and positively on /all six/ variables
(0.82, 0.76, 0.74, 0.57, 0.56, 0.56), because with correlated latent
factors every variable shares variance with every other, and the leading
eigenvector of the reduced correlation matrix picks up that common thread
first. Read literally, it says "there is one big thing all six variables
measure" -- true, but useless for distinguishing the two underlying
groups. Varimax rotates the same fit -- changing neither the communalities
nor a single reproduced correlation, per §2.5 -- into interpretable simple
structure: v1-v3 now load high on Factor 1 and low on Factor 2, v4-v6 the
reverse. The simple-structure index rises from 0.8393 to 0.8552; the
improvement is real but bounded, because varimax is an /orthogonal/
rotation and the true factors here are correlated, so no orthogonal
solution can separate them perfectly -- an honest limitation that motivates
the oblique rotations (not implemented here) used when factors are
believed correlated.

** Factor analysis versus PCA: reproducing the correlations

#+begin_example
off-diagonal residual RMS, FA (2 factors):     0.0103
off-diagonal residual RMS, PCA (2 components): 0.0948
sum of FA communalities: 3.3933 of 6 total variance (56.5550% common, rest is variable-specific uniqueness)
#+end_example

This is §2.2's defining property made numerical. Reconstructing the
observed correlation matrix's /off-diagonal/ entries from two factors
(\(\Lambda\Lambda^\top\)) leaves an RMS residual of just 0.0103; doing the
same from the first two /principal components/ of the same data leaves
0.0948 -- roughly nine times worse, from the same number of latent
dimensions. The reason is structural, not incidental: factor analysis
assigns each variable a private uniqueness \(\psi_j\) that absorbs its
diagonal (self-)variance, freeing the two factors to spend all their
representational power on the /shared/, off-diagonal covariance that the
data actually has. PCA has no uniqueness term, so its two components must
simultaneously fit each variable's private variance /and/ the shared
covariance, and with only two dimensions to spend it does the latter
worse. The last line quantifies what factor analysis chose to model: of
the six units of total variance, 3.3933 (56.55%) is common and the rest is
variable-specific -- the split PCA never draws.

#+begin_example

#+end_example

Asking for two factors from four variables is exactly the case §2.7's
degrees-of-freedom count forbids (\(\mathrm{df} = -1\)), and the fit
misbehaves accordingly: it never converges (still moving at 1000
iterations), and =Petal.Length= produces a /Heywood case/ -- an estimated
communality of 1.0143, which is impossible (a variable cannot have more
than 100% of its variance explained), driving its uniqueness to a clamped
0.0000. This is not a bug in the solver but a real statistical signal
that the model is over-parameterized: there simply are not enough distinct
correlations among four variables to pin down two factors' worth of
loadings /and/ four uniquenesses. PCA, by contrast, would happily return
four orthogonal components for these same four variables -- because it
never attempts the harder task of separating common from unique variance,
and so never runs out of identifying information.

* Discussion

**Factor analysis and PCA look alike and answer opposite questions.**
Both eigendecompose a \(p \times p\) matrix and report "loadings," which
invites conflation, but §4.4 pins down the difference operationally: PCA
maximizes /total/ variance captured and reconstructs the whole covariance
(diagonal included) as well as \(k\) dimensions allow, whereas factor
analysis models only the /shared/ off-diagonal covariance and hands each
variable's private variance to a separate uniqueness term. On the same
data with the same two dimensions, that made factor analysis nine times
better at reproducing the off-diagonal correlations (0.0103 vs 0.0948) --
not because it is "better" in general, but because it was solving the
problem those off-diagonals actually pose. Use PCA to compress or
de-noise; use factor analysis when you believe a few unobserved causes
generated your correlations and you want to estimate them.

**Rotation is a free lunch that must be ordered explicitly.** §2.5's
algebra shows any orthogonal rotation of the loadings fits identically,
and §4.3 shows why that matters: the raw PAF solution on correlated-factor
data was a technically-correct but uninterpretable general factor, and
varimax converted it -- at zero cost to the fit -- into the two-group
structure a human can read. The catch, visible in the same section's
bounded simple-structure gain (0.8393 to 0.8552), is that varimax is
orthogonal: when the underlying factors are themselves correlated, no
orthogonal rotation fully separates them, and an oblique rotation (which
this implementation does not provide) would be the honest next step.

**Identifiability, not sample size, is factor analysis's binding
constraint.** The iris fits (§4.5) failed not for lack of data -- 150
observations is plenty -- but because four variables cannot algebraically
determine two factors' loadings plus four uniquenesses, exactly as §2.7's
\(\mathrm{df} = -1\) predicted, surfacing as non-convergence and a
Heywood case. This is the practical lesson most likely to bite a real
user: factor analysis wants /many/ variables per factor (a common rule of
thumb is at least three to five indicators per factor), and applying it to
a handful of columns -- however many rows -- asks it to do something the
model structure forbids. The synthetic studies used six variables for two
factors precisely to stay on the right side of this line.

* Conclusion

Fitting =datamunge='s C++ =stats::FactorAnalysis= by principal axis
factoring recovers a known two-factor loading structure to a maximum
error of 0.0503 with communalities correlated at 0.9833 against ground
truth, confirming the estimator on data whose true parameters are known.
A varimax rotation converts an uninterpretable general-factor solution
into simple structure without touching the fit, illustrating both the
freedom rotation provides and -- in its bounded improvement on
correlated-factor data -- the limit of orthogonal rotation. The
factor model reproduces the observed off-diagonal correlations about nine
times better than a rank-matched PCA (0.0103 vs 0.0948), a direct,
quantitative demonstration of the communality/uniqueness split that
distinguishes factor analysis from every variance-maximizing method in
this project. And iris demonstrates the method's central practical
limit -- a Heywood case and non-convergence at two factors from four
variables, resolving to a clean single "size" factor -- exactly as the
identifiability count predicts. As with its companions, this entire report
is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example factor_analysis_analysis=, which re-executes §3.5.9's
assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above -- from the
=datamunge= source tree.
