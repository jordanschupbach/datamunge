#+begin_abstract
Evolutionary algorithms improve a population by letting the fittest individuals reproduce more often --
but *how much* more? *Fitness-proportionate* (roulette-wheel) selection is the classic answer: give each
individual a slice of a roulette wheel sized by its fitness, and spin. The probability of being chosen is
exactly the individual's share of the total fitness. This report drives =datamunge='s
=algorithms::roulette_wheel_sample=, verified to reproduce the fitness distribution over hundreds of
thousands of draws, and shows the empirical frequencies matching the theory.
#+end_abstract

* Slicing the wheel by fitness

Give individual \(i\) with fitness \(f_i\ge0\) a slot on a wheel whose size is proportional to \(f_i\).
Spinning the wheel selects \(i\) with probability

\[ p_i=\frac{f_i}{\sum_j f_j}. \]

Concretely, form the cumulative sums \(F_i=\sum_{j\le i}f_j\), draw \(u\) uniformly in \([0,\sum f)\), and
return the first \(i\) with \(F_i>u\) -- a binary search, so each draw is \(O(\log n)\). The *selection
pressure* is set entirely by the spread of fitnesses: when they are similar the wheel is nearly uniform
and selection is weak; when one individual dominates it is chosen almost every spin. That direct
proportionality is roulette-wheel selection's appeal and its weakness -- a single super-fit individual can
take over the population prematurely, and the scheme misbehaves if fitnesses can be negative (they must be
shifted to non-negative first) or if they are all nearly equal (selection becomes random drift). Scaling
or ranking the fitnesses tempers this, but the bare roulette wheel remains the textbook definition of
fitness-proportionate reproduction.

* Algorithm

#+begin_example
ROULETTE-WHEEL-SAMPLE(fitness, n):
  cumulative F[i] = f[0] + ... + f[i];  total = F[last]
  repeat n times:
      u = uniform(0, total)
      select the first i with F[i] > u        # binary search
#+end_example

* Worked example: frequencies match the fitness shares

We select many times from a small population and compare the empirical selection frequency of each
individual with its fitness share.

#+name: rw-run
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    const std::vector<double> fit{1, 2, 3, 4, 5};
    double total = 0; for (double f : fit) total += f;
    const int N = 300000;

    const auto pick = roulette_wheel_sample(fit, N, 1);
    std::vector<int> count(fit.size(), 0);
    for (auto i : pick) ++count[i];

    std::cout << "=== Roulette-wheel selection ===\n";
    std::cout << " individual  fitness  share   empirical\n";
    std::vector<double> ids, shares, freqs;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < fit.size(); ++i) {
        const double share = fit[i] / total, freq = static_cast<double>(count[i]) / N;
        std::printf("     %zu        %.0f     %.3f    %.3f\n", i, fit[i], share, freq);
        ids.push_back(static_cast<double>(i) + 1); shares.push_back(share); freqs.push_back(freq);
    }

    auto plot = RPlot::create();
    plot.bars(ids, freqs, "emp", RGB{37, 99, 235}, 0.5);
    plot.points(ids, shares, "share", RGB{220, 40, 40}, 8.0);
    plot.legend({"empirical frequency", "fitness share (theory)"}, {{37, 99, 235}, {220, 40, 40}});
    plot.title("Roulette-wheel selection frequency matches the fitness share")
        .x_label("individual").y_label("selection probability").background({250, 250, 252});
    plot.save_svg("build/org/roulette_wheel.svg");
    std::cout << "\nwrote roulette_wheel.svg\n";
#+end_src

Every red theory dot sits atop its blue empirical bar: the wheel's slot sizes translate exactly into

way to reward fitness -- and the reason practitioners temper it with scaling or ranking when a single
dominant individual would otherwise spin its way to taking over the whole population.
