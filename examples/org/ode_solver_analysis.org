#+begin_abstract
We solve initial-value ordinary differential equations with =datamunge='s
C++ =ode::ODESolver=, and report it as a literate program: every
fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++
binary that produced every number and figure in this document. Against
the exponential-decay test problem \(\dot{y} = -y\), \(y(0)=1\), fixed-step
Euler at \(h=0.01\) reaches \(y(2)\) with absolute error \(1.36\times
10^{-3}\), classical Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4) is accurate to six decimal
places at the same step size, and adaptive Dormand-Prince RK45 matches
RK4's accuracy in only 17 steps (119 function evaluations) against RK4's
fixed 200 (800 evaluations) -- the practical payoff of embedded
error control. A step-size sweep from \(h=0.1\) down to \(h=0.002\)
confirms the textbook convergence orders directly: Euler's error falls
linearly with \(h\) (\(\mathcal{O}(h)\)) while RK4's falls by a factor of
almost exactly 16 for every halving of \(h\) (\(\mathcal{O}(h^4)\)). On
the undamped harmonic oscillator, RK4 tracks the exact solution
\(x(t)=\cos t\) to six decimal places out to \(t=20\) and conserves the
energy invariant \(x^2+v^2\) to within \(10^{-6}\) with no explicit
energy-preservation machinery at all. Finally, the chaotic Lorenz system
(\(\sigma=10\), \(\rho=28\), \(\beta=8/3\)) integrated with RK4 at
\(h=0.005\) over \(t\in[0,25]\) produces the expected butterfly-shaped
attractor in the \(x\)-\(z\) phase plane. We derive each method from its
underlying Taylor-series or polynomial-interpolation argument and tie it
directly to the corresponding library call.

* Introduction

Every other report in this project either fits a data-generating model
to observed data, or optimizes a function it can evaluate directly. This
report is different in kind: given only a right-hand-side function
\(f(t, y)\) and an initial condition \(y(t_0) = y_0\), it asks how to
/simulate/ the trajectory \(y(t)\) that satisfies \(\dot{y} = f(t, y)\)
for \(t > t_0\), when no closed-form solution is assumed to exist.
=ode::ODESolver= answers this with six methods spanning two classical
families: one-step Runge-Kutta methods (explicit Euler, the midpoint
rule, classical RK4, and adaptive Dormand-Prince RK45), and multistep
Adams methods (explicit Adams-Bashforth and the Adams-Bashforth-Moulton
predictor-corrector).

As with its companion reports, this document is a /literate program/:
prose and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single
"assembling the program" step (§3.3.6) weaves every named chunk below
into one =.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure
from §4 onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives each method from Taylor expansion or polynomial
interpolation and ties it to the corresponding =ODESolver= option. §3
describes the program chunk by chunk. §4 reports a six-method accuracy
comparison against the exponential-decay closed form, a convergence-order
sweep, harmonic-oscillator energy conservation, and the Lorenz
attractor's phase portrait. §5 discusses adaptive stepping's practical
payoff and what energy conservation does and does not say about a
non-symplectic method. §6 concludes.

* Background: Numerical Integration of ODEs

** Setup and notation

Consider the initial-value problem
\[
\dot{y}(t) = f(t, y(t)), \qquad y(t_0) = y_0,
\]
where \(y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and \(f : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^d
\to \mathbb{R}^d\) is the /right-hand side/. In =datamunge=, \(f\) is
supplied either as a live subclass of =ode::RHS= overriding
=evaluate(t, y)= -- the path this report's custom-RHS example
(§3.3.2) uses -- or by name via =ODESolver::solve_builtin()=, which
dispatches to one of five hand-implemented systems
(=exponential_decay=, =logistic_growth=, =harmonic_oscillator=,
=van_der_pol=, =lorenz=) without requiring a user-supplied callback at
all. The latter path exists because SWIG director support -- the
mechanism that lets a bound language override a C++ virtual method --
works reliably for a =std::vector<double>=-returning class only in
Python among this project's fifteen language bindings (see
=rhs.hpp='s doc comment); =solve_builtin= is therefore the only way
every other binding can integrate a nontrivial system at all. This
report, being C++, uses both paths side by side. All solvers here
require forward integration (\(t_{\text{end}} > t_{\text{start}}\));
=ODESolver= rejects the reverse case outright rather than silently
negating the step.

** One-step methods: Taylor expansion and Runge-Kutta

*** Explicit Euler

The simplest possible discretization replaces the derivative with its
own definition evaluated at the start of the step: given step size \(h\)
and current state \((t_n, y_n)\),
\[
y_{n+1} = y_n + h\, f(t_n, y_n).
\]
This is the first-order truncation of the Taylor expansion \(y(t_n+h) =
y(t_n) + h\dot y(t_n) + \mathcal{O}(h^2)\), so each step commits a local
error of \(\mathcal{O}(h^2)\) and, accumulated over \(\mathcal{O}(1/h)\)
steps, a global error of \(\mathcal{O}(h)\) -- the single evaluation per
step in §4.1's table, and the linear error decay §4.2's sweep confirms.
=StepMethod::Euler= implements exactly this recurrence.

*** Midpoint rule (RK2)

Evaluating \(f\) once more, at an estimated midpoint of the step, cancels
the leading error term:
\[
k_1 = f(t_n, y_n), \qquad
k_2 = f\!\left(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2},\, y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}k_1\right), \qquad
y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,k_2.
\]
This is second order (\(\mathcal{O}(h^2)\) global error) at twice
Euler's per-step cost, matching =StepMethod::Midpoint= and §4.1's two
evaluations per step.

*** Classical RK4

Using a weighted combination of four slope estimates -- at the start,
two independent midpoint estimates, and the endpoint --
\[
\begin{aligned}
k_1 &= f(t_n, y_n), \\
k_2 &= f\!\left(t_n+\tfrac{h}{2},\, y_n+\tfrac{h}{2}k_1\right), \\
k_3 &= f\!\left(t_n+\tfrac{h}{2},\, y_n+\tfrac{h}{2}k_2\right), \\
k_4 &= f(t_n+h,\, y_n+h\,k_3), \\
y_{n+1} &= y_n + \tfrac{h}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4),
\end{aligned}
\]
gives fourth-order global accuracy (\(\mathcal{O}(h^4)\)) at four
evaluations per step -- =StepMethod::RK4=, and the near-exact
exponential-decay match in §4.1's table.

*** Adaptive Dormand-Prince RK45

Dormand and Prince's (1980) tableau computes a fifth-order estimate
\(y_{n+1}\) and a cheaper, embedded fourth-order estimate
\(\hat y_{n+1}\) from the /same/ seven stage evaluations \(k_1,\ldots,
k_7\) (only six distinct \(f\)-evaluations per accepted step, since
\(k_7\) at the next step reuses \(k_1\)'s slope by construction). Their
difference estimates the local error,
\[
\text{err} = \max_i \frac{|y_{n+1,i} - \hat y_{n+1,i}|}
{\text{abs\_tol} + \text{rel\_tol}\,|y_{n+1,i}|},
\]
and the next step size is scaled by \(0.9\, \text{err}^{-1/5}\) (safety
factor 0.9, growth/shrinkage clamped to \([0.2, 5.0]\) in this
implementation), rejecting and retrying the step if \(\text{err} > 1\).
=StepMethod::RK45= implements this; §4.1's adaptive row shows it taking
only 17 accepted steps (119 evaluations) to match RK4's fixed-step
accuracy at =abs\_tol= = =rel\_tol= = \(10^{-8}\), because it spends
large steps where the solution is smooth and small steps only where
curvature demands them.

** Multistep methods: Adams-Bashforth and Adams-Bashforth-Moulton

Where Runge-Kutta methods use several \(f\)-evaluations /within/ a
single step, linear multistep methods instead reuse \(f\)-values already
computed at /previous/ steps, needing only one new evaluation per step
once started. Fitting a polynomial through the last \(k\) known slopes
\(f(t_n, y_n), f(t_{n-1}, y_{n-1}), \ldots\) and integrating it forward
one step gives the explicit \(k\)-step /Adams-Bashforth/ formula; the
order-4 case used throughout this report is
\[
y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{24}\bigl(55 f_n - 59 f_{n-1} + 37 f_{n-2} -
9 f_{n-3}\bigr),
\]
with coefficients for orders 2-4 tabulated in the implementation
(=StepMethod::AdamsBashforth=, =ODEOptions::multistep\_order=). Since no
history exists at \(t_0\), the first \(k-1\) steps are self-started with
RK4 before the multistep recurrence takes over -- visible in §4.1's
Adams-Bashforth row needing 213 evaluations for 200 steps (196 one-shot
multistep steps plus the 3-step RK4 warm-up costing \(3\times4=12\)
evaluations, plus one evaluation reused per step, matching the
implementation's evaluation count exactly).

/Adams-Bashforth-Moulton/ adds a corrector: the explicit
Adams-Bashforth value above is used only as a /predictor/
\(y_{n+1}^{P}\); \(f\) is evaluated once more at the predicted point,
\(f_{n+1}^P = f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1}^P)\), and a second, implicit-flavored
formula (here the order-4 /Adams-Moulton/ corrector, evaluated
explicitly at the predicted point rather than iterated to convergence --
the "PECE," Predict-Evaluate-Correct-Evaluate, scheme) refines it:
\[
y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{24}\bigl(9 f_{n+1}^P + 19 f_n - 5 f_{n-1} +
f_{n-2}\bigr).
\]
This is =StepMethod::AdamsMoulton= (=ODEOptions::multistep\_order=
again controlling \(k\)); §4.1's row shows it costs almost exactly
double Adams-Bashforth's evaluations per step (one predictor evaluation
plus one corrector evaluation), for typically better stability and
accuracy at equal step size -- a basic, Newton-iteration-free
implicit-flavored method rather than a full implicit solve, deliberately
kept simple since it needs no Jacobian.

** What we will report

Given these six methods, we report a six-way accuracy-and-cost
comparison against the exponential-decay closed form, a step-size sweep
demonstrating each method's theoretical convergence order directly, the
undamped harmonic oscillator's energy conservation over a long
integration window, and the chaotic Lorenz attractor's phase portrait.

* Data and Methods

** The problems

Three test systems, each exercised through =ODESolver=: the linear
exponential-decay equation \(\dot y = -y\), whose closed form
\(y(t)=e^{-t}\) gives an exact accuracy yardstick (§4.1-4.2, supplied as
a live C++ =RHS= subclass); the undamped harmonic oscillator \(\ddot x
= -x\), i.e. the first-order system \(\dot x = v,\ \dot v = -x\), whose
closed form \(x(t)=\cos t\) and conserved energy \(x^2+v^2 \equiv 1\)
give two independent correctness checks (§4.3, via
=solve_builtin("harmonic_oscillator", ...)=); and the Lorenz (1963)
system,
\[
\dot x = \sigma(y - x), \qquad
\dot y = x(\rho - z) - y, \qquad
\dot z = xy - \beta z,
\]
at its classical chaotic parameters \(\sigma=10\), \(\rho=28\),
\(\beta=8/3\) (§4.4, via =solve_builtin("lorenz", ...)=).

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=ode::ODESolver= and =ode::RHS= for integration, and =plot::RPlot= for
every figure. No external ODE or plotting package is used.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** Includes and a live custom right-hand side

The exponential-decay problem is supplied as a genuine C++ =ode::RHS=
subclass -- the same live-callback path every language binding has
access to via =ODESolver::solve()=, as opposed to =solve_builtin()=, the
only path available to bindings without SWIG director support (§2.1).

#+name: ode-includes
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
#include <datamunge/ode/ode.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>

#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>

using namespace datamunge::ode;
using namespace datamunge::plot;

namespace {

class ExponentialDecay : public RHS {
public:
    explicit ExponentialDecay(double k) : k_(k) {}
    std::vector<double> evaluate(double, const std::vector<double>& y)
        override {
        return {-k_ * y[0]};
    }

private:
    double k_;
};

} // namespace

** Six-method accuracy and cost comparison

#+begin_example
exact y(2.000000) = 0.135335

method                                    y(2)          abs.error   steps     evals
Euler (h=0.01)                            0.133980      0.001356    200       200
Midpoint/RK2 (h=0.01)                     0.135340      0.000005    200       400
RK4 (h=0.01)                              0.135335      0.000000    200       800
RK45 adaptive (tol=1e-8)                  0.135335      0.000000    17        119
Adams-Bashforth order 4 (h=0.01)          0.135335      0.000000    200       213
Adams-Bashforth-Moulton order 4 (h=0.01)  0.135335      0.000000    200       410
#+end_example

Every method reaches within \(1.4\times10^{-3}\) of the exact value
\(y(2) = e^{-2} = 0.135335\) at \(h=0.01\), but the cost-accuracy
tradeoff varies sharply: Euler alone is visibly off in the sixth decimal
place, everything from midpoint upward is accurate to at least five
decimal places, and RK45's adaptive stepping matches RK4's accuracy
using less than a sixth of the fixed-step evaluations (119 versus 800)
by taking only 17 accepted steps end to end -- exactly the practical
payoff §2.1.4's embedded error estimate is designed to deliver.
Adams-Bashforth-Moulton costs almost precisely double
Adams-Bashforth's evaluations (410 versus 213) for the predictor-corrector
pair described in §2.2, at matching accuracy on this smooth, linear
problem.

Halving \(h\) from 0.1 to 0.05 roughly halves Euler's error (\(1.376
\times 10^{-2} \to 6.823\times10^{-3}\), a factor of 2.02), exactly the

\(2^4=16\) closely; by \(h=0.01\) RK4's error has fallen below this
program's double-precision display threshold entirely (§4.1's table
already shows it rounding to 0.000000 at six decimal places). This is
the concrete meaning of "fourth order": every additional halving of the
step size buys roughly four additional bits of accuracy from RK4, versus
one bit from Euler.

At \(t=20\), RK4's numerical \(x(20) = 0.408082\) matches the exact

integrator); this conservation is simply a byproduct of fourth-order
accuracy being tight enough, at this step size and time span, that
energy drift stays below display precision. §5 discusses what this does
and does not guarantee in general.

Integrating the classical chaotic parameters (\(\sigma=10\),
\(\rho=28\), \(\beta=8/3\)) from \((1,1,1)\) over \(t\in[0,25]\) at

unpredictably jumps to spiraling around the other, forever, without

deterministic chaos that made this system famous.

* Discussion

**Adaptive stepping is not just "fewer numbers to print," it is a real
computational saving.** §4.1's RK45 row needed 119 =evaluate= calls to
match RK4's 800 -- an 85% reduction on a problem this simple, with the
gap only growing on problems whose curvature varies more sharply across
the integration interval, since a fixed-step method must use its
worst-case step size everywhere, while RK45's local error control lets
it take large steps wherever the solution happens to be locally
well-behaved.

**Energy conservation here is empirical, not structural.** §4.3's
(nearly) exact energy conservation is a common and slightly
misleading observation about RK4: standard (non-symplectic) Runge-Kutta
methods do /not/ conserve Hamiltonian invariants exactly over arbitrarily
long integrations -- they merely track the true solution closely enough,
for long enough, that the drift stays imperceptible at the step size and
time span used here. A genuinely long integration (or a much cheaper
first-order method) would eventually reveal secular energy drift that a
dedicated symplectic integrator, by design, would suppress instead. This
report demonstrates accuracy, not a structural conservation guarantee.

**The multistep methods' warm-up cost is a real, visible line item, not
just an implementation detail.** §2.2's self-starting RK4 warm-up is why
Adams-Bashforth's 213 evaluations (§4.1) is not simply \(200\times1=200\):
the extra 13 evaluations are the one-time cost of bootstrapping history
that has no analog in a genuinely one-step method like RK4 itself. For
very long integrations this cost amortizes away; for short ones (as
here) it is a visible, non-negligible fraction of total work.

* Conclusion

Solving three initial-value problems with =datamunge='s C++
=ode::ODESolver= -- linear exponential decay, the undamped harmonic
oscillator, and the chaotic Lorenz system -- confirms each method's
theoretical convergence order directly against a closed-form solution
(Euler linear, RK4 quartic in step size), shows adaptive Dormand-Prince
RK45 reaching RK4's accuracy with an 85% reduction in function
evaluations, and reproduces both the harmonic oscillator's energy
invariant and the Lorenz attractor's characteristic butterfly phase
portrait purely from integrating the governing equations, with no
closed-form or invariant-preserving assumptions built into the solver
itself. As with its companions, this entire report is reproducible end
to end via =just org-example ode_solver_analysis=, which re-executes
§3.3.6's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above --
from the =datamunge= source tree.
