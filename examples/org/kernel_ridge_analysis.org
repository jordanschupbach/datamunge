#+begin_abstract
We implement Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) in =datamunge='s C++
=stats::KernelRidgeRegression= and report it as a literate program: every
fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++
binary that produced every number and figure in this document. KRR is
ridge regression performed in a (possibly infinite-dimensional) kernel
feature space: it minimizes squared error plus a penalty on the fitted
function's roughness, which by the representer theorem has the closed-form
dual solution alpha = (K + lambda I)^{-1} y, so a prediction is a
kernel-weighted combination of the training responses. It thus unifies
three ideas this project treats separately -- the "kernel trick" of the
[[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA]] report, the ridge penalty
of the [[file:elastic_net_iris_analysis.org][regularized-regression]]
report, and the predictive machinery of
[[file:gaussian_process_regression_iris_analysis.org][Gaussian process
regression]] (whose posterior mean KRR reproduces exactly). We fit it to
120 noisy samples of a sine wave: with both the ridge strength lambda and
the RBF bandwidth gamma chosen automatically by an exact, refit-free
leave-one-out cross-validation, KRR recovers the true function to an RMSE
of 0.0195, at a leave-one-out R^2 of 0.9596 whose 0.1513 error is
essentially the 0.15 noise floor. A bias-variance study then turns the
regularization dial explicitly: holding a deliberately narrow bandwidth
fixed and sweeping lambda across six orders of magnitude, the model's
effective degrees of freedom -- the continuous complexity measure
tr[K(K+lambda I)^{-1}] -- fall monotonically from 21.6 to 4.6, while the
error against the true function traces the classic U (0.0603 when
under-regularized, 0.0298 at the optimum, 0.2026 when over-regularized).
The RBF kernel's leave-one-out R^2 of 0.9596 dwarfs a linear kernel's
0.6236 on this nonlinear target -- and we confirm the theory's promise
that a linear kernel /is/ ordinary ridge regression, reproducing a
closed-form ridge fit to a maximum discrepancy of 0.0000. Finally, on
iris, KRR recovers a mildly nonlinear petal-length-versus-width
relationship at a leave-one-out R^2 of 0.9516, edging the 0.9260 of its
own linear-kernel (ridge) special case. We derive the RKHS penalty, the
representer theorem, the dual solution and its hat matrix, effective
degrees of freedom, the closed-form leave-one-out identity, and the
linear-kernel/ridge equivalence, tying each to the corresponding library
call.

* Introduction

This project already contains several ways to fit a curve. Ordinary least
squares and its [[file:elastic_net_iris_analysis.org][ridge/lasso
variants]] fit /linear/ functions of the predictors; Nadaraya-Watson
[[file:kernel_regression_iris_analysis.org][kernel regression]] fits a
nonlinear curve as a local weighted average; and
[[file:gaussian_process_regression_iris_analysis.org][Gaussian process
regression]] fits a nonlinear curve with Bayesian uncertainty. Kernel
Ridge Regression sits at the intersection of the first and the third: it
is /exactly/ ridge regression -- the same squared-error-plus-quadratic-
penalty recipe -- but carried out in the rich, nonlinear feature space of
a kernel, so it fits smooth nonlinear functions while keeping ridge's
clean closed-form linear-algebra solution and its single, interpretable
regularization knob.

The mechanism is the "kernel trick" the
[[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA report]] introduces:
although the feature space may be infinite-dimensional, the fit depends on
the data only through inner products, which a kernel function supplies
directly. The representer theorem then guarantees the solution is a finite
combination of kernels centered at the training points, reducing the
whole problem to solving one \(n \times n\) linear system. The result is a
method that is at once flexible (it can fit any smooth function given the
right kernel) and controlled (a single penalty \(\lambda\) governs how
much it is allowed to bend), with the bias-variance tradeoff laid bare in
a way few methods make so explicit.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.4.10) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives the RKHS penalty, the representer theorem, the dual solution
and its hat matrix, the effective-degrees-of-freedom complexity measure,
the closed-form leave-one-out cross-validation =KernelRidgeRegression=
uses to tune itself, and the linear-kernel/ridge equivalence -- each tied
to the specific method that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk
by chunk. §4 reports the recovery of a known nonlinear function, an
explicit bias-variance regularization study, a comparison across kernels,
the ridge-regression identity, and a real-data iris fit. §5 discusses when
KRR is the right tool and how it relates to its neighbors. §6 concludes.

* Background: Kernel Ridge Regression

** Setup and notation

We observe \(n\) pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) with predictors \(x_i \in
\mathbb{R}^p\) and a numeric response \(y_i\). =KernelRidgeRegression=
takes them through the same formula interface as the other regressors
(=KernelRidgeRegression(data, "y ~ x")=), standardizes the predictors by
default, and centers the response (the penalty below does not shrink the
intercept, so the response mean is added back after fitting).

** Penalized least squares in a function space

Ordinary ridge regression fits the best /linear/ function under an
\(\ell_2\) penalty on its coefficients. KRR generalizes "linear function"
to "any function in a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\(\mathcal{H}\)," penalizing the function's RKHS norm \(\lVert f
\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\) -- a measure of its roughness. It solves

\[
\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - f(x_i)\bigr)^2
   \; + \; \lambda \, \lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^2 ,
\]

with \(\lambda \ge 0\) (=KernelRidgeRegressionOptions::lambda=) trading
data fidelity against smoothness. The RKHS is defined implicitly by a
/kernel/ \(k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle\), an inner
product of feature maps \(\phi\) we never form explicitly.
=KernelRidgeRegressionOptions::kernel= selects among

\[
k_{\text{rbf}}(x, x') = e^{-\gamma \lVert x - x' \rVert^2}, \quad
k_{\text{lin}}(x, x') = x^\top x', \quad
k_{\text{poly}}(x, x') = (\gamma\, x^\top x' + c_0)^{d},
\]

the RBF (squared-exponential) kernel corresponding to an infinite-
dimensional \(\phi\) -- which is what lets KRR represent arbitrary smooth
functions.

** The representer theorem and the dual solution

The objective ranges over an infinite-dimensional function space, yet its
minimizer is finite-dimensional. The /representer theorem/ states that the
solution must lie in the span of the kernels centered at the training
points,

\[
f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \, k(x, x_i) ,
\]

so only the \(n\) dual coefficients \(\alpha\) (=dual_coefficients()=) need
be found. Substituting this form collapses the objective to \(\lVert y -
K\alpha \rVert^2 + \lambda\,\alpha^\top K \alpha\), where \(K_{ij} = k(x_i,
x_j)\) is the \(n \times n\) Gram matrix, and minimizing over \(\alpha\)
gives the closed form

\[
\alpha = (K + \lambda I)^{-1} y ,
\]

a single symmetric linear system. =KernelRidgeRegression::fit= solves it
through an eigendecomposition of \(K\) (=linalg::jacobi_eigen=, as in the
[[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and
[[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA]] reports), which also
yields the cross-validation and complexity quantities below for free. A
prediction at a new point is then \(f(x_\ast) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(x_\ast,
x_i)\) plus the response mean.

** The hat matrix and effective degrees of freedom

The fitted training values are \(\hat{y} = K\alpha = K(K + \lambda
I)^{-1} y = H y\), a /linear smoother/ with hat matrix \(H = K(K + \lambda
I)^{-1}\). Diagonalizing \(K = U D U^\top\) gives \(H = U \operatorname{diag}
\!\bigl(d_i/(d_i + \lambda)\bigr) U^\top\), so its trace -- the /effective
degrees of freedom/ (=effective_degrees_of_freedom()=) --

\[
\mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \operatorname{tr}(H) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}
   \frac{d_i}{d_i + \lambda}
\]

is a continuous count of how many parameters the fit effectively spends.
As \(\lambda \to 0\) each ratio approaches 1 and \(\mathrm{df} \to
\operatorname{rank}(K)\) (the model interpolates); as \(\lambda \to
\infty\) every ratio approaches 0 and \(\mathrm{df} \to 0\) (the model
collapses toward a constant). §4.2 dials \(\lambda\) and watches this
number fall, making the abstract notion of "model complexity" a concrete,
measured quantity.

** Leave-one-out cross-validation in closed form

Because KRR is a linear smoother, its leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation
error needs no refitting. The standard identity for linear smoothers gives
the \(i\)-th LOO residual directly from the full fit,

\[
y_i - \hat{f}^{(-i)}(x_i) = \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{1 - H_{ii}} ,
\]

so a single fit yields the exact error of \(n\) leave-one-out fits.
=KernelRidgeRegression= uses this both to /report/ honest performance
(=fitted_values()=, =r_squared()=, =rmse()= are all leave-one-out, not
resubstitution) and to /select/ \(\lambda\) and the RBF bandwidth
\(\gamma\): when either option is left negative it is chosen by minimizing
this LOO error over an internal grid (a cheap \(\lambda\) sweep in the
fixed eigenbasis of \(K\), repeated per candidate \(\gamma\)). §4.2's
cross-validation curve is exactly this criterion plotted against
\(\lambda\).

** The linear kernel is ordinary ridge regression

With \(k(x, x') = x^\top x'\) the Gram matrix is \(K = X X^\top\), and a
standard push-through identity gives

\[
K(K + \lambda I)^{-1} y = X (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y ,
\]

whose right-hand side is precisely the prediction of ordinary ridge
regression with penalty \(\lambda\). So linear-kernel KRR /is/ ridge
regression, computed the dual way through the \(n \times n\) Gram matrix
instead of the \(p \times p\) normal equations -- a genuine, checkable
identity that §4.4 confirms to within double-precision round-off, and the
regression analogue of the [[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA
report's]] linear-kernel-equals-PCA check.

** What we will report

Given the fits, we report the recovery of a known nonlinear function
against its ground truth, an explicit bias-variance study of the
regularizer (effective degrees of freedom and error versus \(\lambda\),
with under-, well-, and over-regularized fits), a comparison of the RBF,
linear, and polynomial kernels, the linear-kernel/ridge identity, and a
real-data iris fit.

* Data and Methods

** The synthetic nonlinear target

To check recovery against a known ground truth -- impossible with real
data, where the true regression function is unobservable -- we draw
\(n = 120\) points with \(x \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\) and \(y = \sin(2\pi
x) + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.15^2)\). The
true function \(\sin(2\pi x)\) is withheld from every fit and used only
afterward, to measure how closely the fitted curve matches it (an RMSE on
a dense grid). A sine over a full period is markedly nonlinear, so it
cleanly separates a genuinely nonlinear method from a linear one.

** The iris data

=datamunge::datasets::iris()= supplies Fisher's 150 flowers; we regress
=Petal.Length= on =Petal.Width=, a single-predictor problem that is mostly
but not perfectly linear, so it exercises KRR on real data and lets its
RBF and linear (ridge) forms be compared directly.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::KernelRidgeRegression= for the
model, and =plot::RPlot= for every figure. No external statistics package
is used.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** Includes and a ground-truth error helper

One helper, used only to /score/ a fit after the fact: the RMSE of its
predictions, on a dense grid, against the known true function
\(\sin(2\pi x)\) -- never an input to the model.

#include <string>
#include <vector>

using datamunge::dstruct::DataFrame;
using datamunge::stats::KernelRidgeRegression;
using datamunge::stats::KernelRidgeRegressionOptions;
using namespace datamunge::plot;

static constexpr double kTwoPi = 6.283185307179586;

// RMSE of a model's predictions on a dense grid against the known true function sin(2*pi*x).
static double truth_rmse(const KernelRidgeRegression& m, std::size_t grid = 400) {
    std::vector<double> gx(grid);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < grid; ++i) gx[i] = static_cast<double>(i) / static_cast<double>(grid - 1);
    DataFrame g;
    g.add_column("x", gx);
    const auto pred = m.predict(g);
    double ss = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < grid; ++i) {
        const double d = pred[i] - std::sin(kTwoPi * gx[i]);
        ss += d * d;
    }
    return std::sqrt(ss / static_cast<double>(grid));
}

    std::cout << "=== A nonlinear target: y = sin(2*pi*x) + noise ===\n";
    const std::size_t n = 120;
    std::mt19937_64 rng(20240815);
    std::uniform_real_distribution<double> ux(0.0, 1.0);
    std::normal_distribution<double> noise(0.0, 0.15);

    synth.add_column("y", y);

*** The linear-kernel/ridge identity

#+name: krr-linear-is-ridge
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Identity: KRR with a linear kernel IS ridge regression ===\n";
    {
        const double lam = 1.0;
        KernelRidgeRegression klin(synth, "y ~ x", KernelRidgeRegressionOptions{"linear", lam});
        const auto krr_pred = klin.predict(synth);
        // Primal ridge on the standardized single predictor, closed form.
        double mean = 0.0;
        for (double v : x) mean += v;
        mean /= n;
        double var = 0.0;
        for (double v : x) var += (v - mean) * (v - mean);
        const double sd = std::sqrt(var / n);  // population sd, matching the class
        double ymean = 0.0;
        for (double v : y) ymean += v;
        ymean /= n;
        double sxx = 0.0, sxy = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
            const double xs = (x[i] - mean) / sd;
            sxx += xs * xs;
            sxy += xs * (y[i] - ymean);
        }
        const double beta = sxy / (sxx + lam);
        double max_abs_diff = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
            const double ridge_pred = ymean + ((x[i] - mean) / sd) * beta;
            max_abs_diff = std::max(max_abs_diff, std::abs(ridge_pred - krr_pred[i]));
        }
        std::cout << "max |KRR-linear prediction - closed-form ridge prediction| (lambda=1): " << max_abs_diff << "\n";
    }

With both hyperparameters chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation, KRR
lands on \(\lambda = 0.0041\) and \(\gamma = 0.1924\), spending only 5.16
effective degrees of freedom (of a possible 120) to describe the data. Its
leave-one-out RMSE of 0.1513 is essentially the irreducible noise floor --
the data were generated with noise standard deviation 0.15, so no method
could do materially better on held-out points. The decisive number is the
last one: the fitted curve matches the /true/, noiseless \(\sin(2\pi x)\)
to an RMSE of just 0.0195. KRR did not merely track the noisy points; it
recovered the function beneath them.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt KRR fit closely tracking the true sine through noisy data :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/krr_synth_fit.svg]]

The picture confirms it: the red KRR fit lies almost exactly on the green
true sine, threading through the gray noisy cloud without chasing
individual points -- the visual signature of a well-regularized nonlinear
fit.

** The regularization dial

#+begin_example
lambda         effective dof      LOO RMSE   truth RMSE
1e-05                21.6104        0.1696        0.0603
1e-03                17.5737        0.1632        0.0490
1e-02                15.2085        0.1601        0.0435
1e-01                12.4417        0.1570        0.0308
1e+00                 9.0064        0.1586        0.0298
1e+01                 4.5553        0.2674        0.2026
#+end_example

To expose the bias-variance tradeoff we fix a deliberately /narrow/
bandwidth (\(\gamma = 5\)), so that the RBF kernel is local enough to
interpolate noise unless \(\lambda\) holds it back, and sweep \(\lambda\).
The effective degrees of freedom (§2.4) fall monotonically from 21.6 to
4.6 as \(\lambda\) grows -- a direct, continuous readout of the model
simplifying. The error against the true function traces the classic U:
0.0603 when barely regularized (\(\lambda = 10^{-5}\), where the extra
degrees of freedom are spent fitting noise), improving to a minimum near
0.0298 (\(\lambda \approx 1\)), then rising sharply to 0.2026 when
over-regularized (\(\lambda = 10\), where the fit is too stiff to follow
the sine at all). The leave-one-out RMSE -- computable /without/ the true
function, from §2.5's identity -- has its own minimum in the same region
(0.1570 at \(\lambda = 0.1\)), which is exactly why cross-validation can
find a good \(\lambda\) using only the observed data.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Cross-validation curve with a clear minimum :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/krr_cv_curve.svg]]

Letting the class /select/ \(\lambda\) at this bandwidth traces the CV
curve above (against \(\log_{10}\lambda\), so the log-spaced grid reads
evenly): a flat valley of near-equivalent small-\(\lambda\) fits, a broad
minimum where the selected \(\lambda\) (red) sits, and a steep climb into
the over-regularized regime on the right. This is §2.5's leave-one-out
criterion made visual, and the shape every practitioner hopes for when
tuning a regularizer.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Overfit, well-fit, and underfit curves on the same data :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/krr_regularization.svg]]

The three regimes, drawn: the barely-regularized fit (\(\lambda =
10^{-5}\), red) wiggles to chase noise; the over-regularized fit
(\(\lambda = 10\), blue) is too stiff, flattening the peaks and troughs;
and the well-chosen fit (\(\lambda = 1\), green) recovers the smooth
underlying shape. A single scalar \(\lambda\) moves the model continuously
between these extremes -- the defining virtue of a penalized method.

** Comparing kernels

#+begin_example
kernel               LOO R^2      LOO RMSE   truth RMSE
rbf                   0.9596        0.1513        0.0195
linear                0.6236        0.4614        0.4492
polynomial            0.9504        0.1675        0.0720
#+end_example

The kernel is where the nonlinearity lives, and it matters. The RBF kernel
(leave-one-out \(R^2 = 0.9596\), true-function RMSE 0.0195) far outperforms
the /linear/ kernel (\(R^2 = 0.6236\), true RMSE 0.4492), which -- being
ordinary ridge regression (§4.4) -- can only fit a straight line through a
full sine wave and necessarily fails. The cubic polynomial kernel lands in
between (\(R^2 = 0.9504\), true RMSE 0.0720): flexible enough to bend
through most of the sine, but a globally polynomial basis fits a periodic
function less faithfully than the RBF's local bumps. The lesson mirrors
the [[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA report's]]: "nonlinear
kernel" is not one choice but a modeling decision, and the RBF is the
robust default for smooth functions.

** The linear kernel is ridge regression

#+begin_example
max |KRR-linear prediction - closed-form ridge prediction| (lambda=1): 0.0000
#+end_example

§2.6's identity, confirmed: linear-kernel KRR's predictions match an
independently computed, closed-form ridge regression (with the same
\(\lambda\)) to a maximum discrepancy of 0.0000 across all 120 points. The
two are the same estimator viewed from two sides -- KRR through the \(n
\times n\) Gram matrix \(K(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\), ridge through the \(p
\times p\) normal equations \((X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}\) -- and the
kernel machinery reduces exactly to the familiar linear method when the
kernel is linear, just as it should.

** Real data: iris

#+begin_example

Formula: Petal.Length ~ Petal.Width
Kernel: rbf, observations: 150
lambda: 0.2572 (LOO-CV selected), gamma: 0.6250 (LOO-CV selected)
Effective degrees of freedom: 4.9538 (of 150)
Leave-one-out R^2: 0.9516, RMSE: 0.3870
linear-kernel KRR leave-one-out R^2: 0.9260 (vs RBF 0.9516)
#+end_example

On iris, KRR predicts =Petal.Length= from =Petal.Width= at a leave-one-out
\(R^2\) of 0.9516, spending 4.95 effective degrees of freedom. Its own
linear-kernel (ridge) special case reaches 0.9260 -- close, because the
relationship is /mostly/ linear, but visibly bettered by the RBF fit,
which picks up the mild curvature a straight line must miss.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt KRR fit of iris petal length on petal width :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/krr_iris_fit.svg]]

The fitted curve shows where that 2.5-point \(R^2\) gain comes from: the
relationship flattens slightly at both ends of the petal-width range (the
tight low-width /setosa/ cluster, and a gentle plateau at the high-width
end) and steepens in the middle -- a mild S-shape the RBF kernel captures
and a line cannot. With cross-validation choosing a moderate \(\lambda\)
and \(\gamma\), KRR fits that curvature without overreacting to it, a
concrete example of the method behaving well on data whose true form is
unknown.

* Discussion

**KRR unifies three things this project treats separately.** It is ridge
regression (§4.4 makes the identity exact), it uses the kernel trick (the
same \(K(K+\lambda I)^{-1}\) machinery as the
[[file:kernel_pca_iris_analysis.org][kernel PCA]] and
[[file:gaussian_process_regression_iris_analysis.org][GP]] reports), and
its predictive mean is /identical/ to a Gaussian process's with the same
kernel and \(\lambda\) playing the role of the noise variance. The
practical difference from the GP is what each reports: KRR is a point
estimator returning only the mean curve, while the GP additionally returns
a posterior variance and selects its hyperparameters by marginal
likelihood rather than cross-validation. When only the fit is needed, KRR
is the leaner tool; when calibrated uncertainty matters, the GP earns its
extra cost.

**The effective degrees of freedom make regularization legible.** §4.2's
central number, \(\mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \sum_i d_i/(d_i+\lambda)\), turns
the abstract "amount of regularization" into a concrete count that fell
from 21.6 to 4.6 as \(\lambda\) swept its range. It is what lets one say
precisely /how much/ model a given \(\lambda\) buys, connects the
continuous ridge penalty to the discrete language of "number of
parameters," and -- because it is a byproduct of the same eigendecomposition
that solves the system -- costs nothing extra to report. It is also the
bridge to the closed-form leave-one-out identity (§2.5), the two together
letting KRR both measure its own complexity and tune it without any
external validation set.

**The kernel is the model; the penalty is the control.** §4.3 and §4.2
separate the two knobs cleanly: the /kernel/ (and its bandwidth) decides
what shapes the fit can take -- straight lines, local bumps, global
polynomials -- while \(\lambda\) decides how much of that flexibility is
actually used. Getting a good fit means getting both right, and the
report's two failure directions illustrate it: a linear kernel is the
wrong /shape/ for a sine no matter how \(\lambda\) is set (§4.3), while a
narrow RBF with a bad \(\lambda\) has the right shape but the wrong
/amount/ of it (§4.2). Cross-validation, tuning both at once, is what
navigates between them.

* Conclusion

Implementing Kernel Ridge Regression in =datamunge='s C++
=stats::KernelRidgeRegression= and applying it to 120 noisy samples of a
sine wave, we recover the true underlying function to an RMSE of 0.0195
with a leave-one-out \(R^2\) of 0.9596 -- at the noise floor -- using ridge
and bandwidth parameters chosen automatically by an exact, refit-free
leave-one-out cross-validation. An explicit bias-variance study dials the
regularizer across six orders of magnitude, watching the effective degrees
of freedom fall from 21.6 to 4.6 and the error against the truth trace the
classic U from 0.0603 (overfit) through 0.0298 (optimal) to 0.2026
(underfit). The RBF kernel's \(R^2\) of 0.9596 dwarfs a linear kernel's
0.6236 on this nonlinear target, while that same linear kernel is confirmed
to reproduce ordinary ridge regression to a discrepancy of 0.0000, and on
real iris data KRR edges its own ridge special case (0.9516 versus 0.9260)
by capturing a mild curvature. As with its companions, this entire report
is reproducible end to end via =just org-example kernel_ridge_analysis=,
which re-executes §3.4.10's assembly block -- and therefore every number
and figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
