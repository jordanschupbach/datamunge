#+begin_abstract
We implement two /model-agnostic/ explainable-AI methods -- LIME (Ribeiro,
Singh & Guestrin, 2016) and SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) -- in =datamunge='s
C++ =stats::LimeImageExplainer= and =stats::ShapImageExplainer=, and report
it as a literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled,
executed chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and
figure in this document. Where the
[[file:cnn_analysis.org][companion CNN report's]] layer-wise relevance
propagation was /model-specific/ -- it reached inside the network and used
its weights -- LIME and SHAP treat the model as an opaque black box, learning
what it attends to purely by /perturbing the input and watching the
output/. Both partition the image into a grid of superpixels and toggle
them on (original) or off (a baseline); LIME then fits a proximity-weighted
linear surrogate to the resulting predictions, while SHAP computes exact
/Shapley values/ -- each superpixel's average marginal contribution over all
\(2^{16}\) coalitions of the others, the unique attribution satisfying the
efficiency, symmetry, and dummy axioms of cooperative game theory. Applied
to a CNN that classifies horizontal versus vertical bars at 100% accuracy,
SHAP's attributions sum /exactly/ to the model's score difference
\(f(\text{full}) - f(\text{empty})\) (the efficiency axiom, confirmed to
machine precision), and both methods localize the decision to the bar. The
three explainers agree in an informative way: LIME and SHAP, both
perturbation-based, correlate at up to 0.94 with each other, and both agree
with LRP on /where/ the evidence is while differing in /resolution/ --
superpixel-coarse for LIME and SHAP, pixel-sharp for LRP (a cross-method
correlation near 0.25-0.33). Finally, because they are model-agnostic, the
/same/ LIME and SHAP code explains a completely different classifier -- a
fully-connected network on the flattened pixels -- with no changes, and its
SHAP explanation agrees with the CNN's at 0.74. We derive the local-surrogate
objective, the Shapley value and its axioms, the exact-enumeration
algorithm, and the model-specific-versus-agnostic trade-off, tying each to
the corresponding library call.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The [[file:cnn_analysis.org][CNN report]] ended by opening the black box
with layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP), which traces a prediction to
the input pixels by running a conservation rule /backward through the
network's own layers/. That is powerful but /model-specific/: it needs the
architecture, the weights, and a custom backward pass for every layer type.
A different and complementary style of explanation asks for none of that.
/Model-agnostic/ methods treat the classifier as an opaque function -- give
it an input, read off a prediction -- and learn what it depends on by
/probing/ it: perturb the input in structured ways, watch how the output
moves, and attribute the movement to the parts that caused it. They work on
/any/ model -- a neural network, a random forest, a black-box API -- because
they use nothing but its predictions.

This report implements the two most widely used such methods. /LIME/ fits a
simple, interpretable model (here a linear one) in the local neighborhood
of the instance being explained, using the black box only to label
perturbations. /SHAP/ grounds attribution in cooperative game theory: it
treats the input's parts as players in a game whose payoff is the model's
score, and assigns each part its /Shapley value/, the game-theoretically
unique fair share of the total. For images, both methods work over
/superpixels/ -- small patches toggled on or off -- rather than individual
pixels, which keeps the number of "features" small enough to attribute
meaningfully (and, for exact SHAP, to enumerate).

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.4.5) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp= file,
compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is that
program's literal output.

§2 derives the LIME local-surrogate objective, the Shapley value and its
axioms, the exact enumeration SHAP uses, and how these model-agnostic
methods relate to each other and to the model-specific LRP -- each tied to
the piece of =datamunge= that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk
by chunk. §4 reports the LIME, SHAP, and LRP explanations of a CNN's
predictions side by side, their agreement, and the same methods explaining
a different model. §5 discusses what perturbation-based explanation buys
and what it costs. §6 concludes.

* Background: Perturbation-Based Explanation

** Setup: a black box and its superpixels

We are given a trained classifier only as a /prediction function/ -- in the
library, an =ImagePredict=, a callable mapping an image to the score
(probability) of the class being explained. To explain one image \(x\), we
partition it into a grid of \(P = \) =patch_rows= \(\times\) =patch_cols=
/superpixels/ and represent any /perturbation/ by a binary vector
\(z \in \{0,1\}^P\): superpixel \(p\) is kept at its original pixels when
\(z_p = 1\) and replaced by a =baseline= value (here 0, i.e. removed to a
black background) when \(z_p = 0\). Writing \(h(z)\) for the image this
produces, the black box gives a score \(f(h(z))\) for any coalition of
present superpixels. Everything below is built from queries of that one
function; the explainers never see the model's internals.

** LIME: a local linear surrogate

LIME's premise is that even a globally complex model is /locally/ simple:
near one instance, its behavior can be approximated by an interpretable
model. It draws many random perturbations \(z^{(1)}, \ldots, z^{(N)}\),
labels each with the black box, \(f_s = f(h(z^{(s)}))\), and fits a linear
surrogate \(g(z) = \beta_0 + \sum_p \beta_p z_p\) by /proximity-weighted/
least squares, minimizing

\[
\sum_{s=1}^{N} \pi(z^{(s)})\,\bigl(f_s - g(z^{(s)})\bigr)^2
   \; + \; \lambda \lVert \beta \rVert^2 ,
\]

where the weight \(\pi(z) = \exp(-d(z)^2 / \sigma^2)\) (=lime_kernel_width=
\(\sigma\)) makes perturbations closer to the original image -- fewer
superpixels removed -- count more, so the fit is /local/, and \(\lambda\)
(=lime_l2=) is a small ridge penalty. =LimeImageExplainer= solves the
weighted ridge normal equations directly; each coefficient \(\beta_p\)
(=patch_weights()=) is superpixel \(p\)'s local importance, and
=local_r_squared()= reports how faithfully the surrogate tracks the model
nearby. LIME is fast and general, but it is an /approximation/ -- its
explanation is only as good as the local linear fit, and depends on the
sampling and the kernel width.

** SHAP: Shapley values from game theory

SHAP replaces "fit a local model" with a principled definition of a fair
attribution. Treat the \(P\) superpixels as players in a cooperative game
whose payoff for a coalition \(S \subseteq \{1, \ldots, P\}\) is the model's
score with exactly those superpixels present, \(v(S) = f(h(\mathbf{1}_S))\).
The /Shapley value/ of superpixel \(p\) is its average marginal
contribution over every ordering in which the players could be added,

\[
\phi_p = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{p\}}
   \frac{|S|!\,(P - |S| - 1)!}{P!}\;
   \bigl(v(S \cup \{p\}) - v(S)\bigr) ,
\]

the /unique/ attribution satisfying four axioms: /efficiency/ (the
attributions sum to \(v(N) - v(\varnothing)\), the whole prediction is
accounted for), /symmetry/ (players with identical contributions get equal
credit), /dummy/ (a player that never changes the payoff gets zero), and
/additivity/. =ShapImageExplainer= computes this /exactly/ -- it evaluates
the model on all \(2^P\) coalitions once, caches them, and sums the marginal
contributions -- so the result carries no sampling error and satisfies
efficiency to machine precision (§4.1 confirms it). Exactness costs an
exponential number of model queries, which is why \(P\) is kept small (the
constructor refuses \(P > 20\)); §5 returns to this trade-off.

** One family, and the contrast with LRP

LIME and SHAP are closer than they look: KernelSHAP shows that the Shapley
values are themselves the solution of a particular weighted linear
regression over coalitions -- the same shape as LIME, but with a specific
kernel that makes the coefficients satisfy the game-theoretic axioms rather
than merely fit locally. Here we compute Shapley values by exact
enumeration instead, but the kinship explains why §4 finds LIME and SHAP
agreeing so closely. Both differ from the
[[file:cnn_analysis.org][CNN report's]] LRP along the axis that names this
report: LRP is /model-specific/ (it needs and uses the network's weights and
layer structure, and runs in a single fast backward pass, at pixel
resolution), whereas LIME and SHAP are /model-agnostic/ (they need only the
prediction function, cost many forward queries, and here work at superpixel
resolution). §4.2 shows these are not competitors but complementary views:
they agree on /where/ the model looks and differ in /how/ they get there and
/how finely/ they resolve it.

** What we will report

Given a trained CNN, we report LIME, SHAP, and LRP explanations of the same
predictions side by side, with LIME's local fit quality, SHAP's exact
efficiency, and the cross-method agreement; then we apply the identical LIME
and SHAP code to a different model -- a fully-connected network -- to
demonstrate model-agnosticism.

* Data and Methods

** The model and data

We reuse the [[file:cnn_analysis.org][CNN report's]] task: 12x12 grayscale
images of horizontal and vertical bars at random positions, on which we
train the same convolutional network (conv -> ReLU -> pool -> dense ->
softmax) to 100% training accuracy. We then explain its predictions on two
held-out example images, one of each class. For the model-agnostic
demonstration we additionally train a fully-connected network
(=stats::MLPClassifier=) on the flattened pixels.

** Superpixels and the baseline

Both explainers operate on a 4x4 grid of superpixels (\(P = 16\), so exact
SHAP enumerates \(2^{16} = 65536\) coalitions), with "off" superpixels set
to a baseline of 0 -- black, the images' background. This choice matters:
because the background is already near the baseline, removing a background
superpixel barely changes the image, so it correctly receives almost no
attribution; the evidence concentrates on the superpixels that contain the
bar.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=linalg::DenseMatrix= for images, =stats::CNNClassifier= (with its
=relevance= method for LRP) and =stats::MLPClassifier= for the models,
=stats::LimeImageExplainer= and =stats::ShapImageExplainer= for the
model-agnostic explanations, and =plot::RPlot= for every figure.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** Includes and helpers

Helpers to build and draw: a bar-image generator, a converter from an image
to a one-row pixel DataFrame (so the fully-connected model can score a
single image), a Pearson correlation between two relevance maps (negatives
clamped so all methods compare on the same non-negative support scale), and
the grid/heatmap drawing utilities from the CNN report.

static constexpr std::size_t H = 12, W = 12;

static DenseMatrix<double> make_bar(bool horizontal, std::size_t pos, std::mt19937_64& rng) {
    std::normal_distribution<double> noise(0.0, 0.08);
    DenseMatrix<double> img(H, W, 0.0);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < H; ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < W; ++j) {
            double v = 0.0;
            if (horizontal && (i == pos || i == pos + 1)) v = 1.0;
            if (!horizontal && (j == pos || j == pos + 1)) v = 1.0;
            img(i, j) = std::clamp(v + noise(rng), 0.0, 1.0);
        }
    return img;
}

// One image as a single-row pixel DataFrame (p0..p{HW-1}), for the fully-connected MLP predictor.
static DataFrame image_to_df(const DenseMatrix<double>& img) {
    DataFrame d;
    for (std::size_t p = 0; p < H * W; ++p) d.add_column("p" + std::to_string(p), std::vector<double>{img(p / W, p % W)});
    return d;
}

// Pearson correlation of two relevance maps over their pixels (negatives clamped to 0 so all three
// methods are compared on the same non-negative "support" scale).
static double map_corr(const DenseMatrix<double>& A, const DenseMatrix<double>& B) {
    std::vector<double> a, b;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < H; ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < W; ++j) { a.push_back(std::max(0.0, A(i, j))); b.push_back(std::max(0.0, B(i, j))); }
    const std::size_t n = a.size();
    double ma = 0, mb = 0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) { ma += a[i]; mb += b[i]; }
    ma /= n; mb /= n;
    double num = 0, da = 0, db = 0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) { const double xa = a[i] - ma, xb = b[i] - mb; num += xa * xb; da += xa * xa; db += xb * xb; }
    return (da > 0 && db > 0) ? num / std::sqrt(da * db) : 0.0;
}

template <typename Cmap>
static void draw_grid(RPlot& plot, const DenseMatrix<double>& m, double ox, double oy, Cmap cmap) {
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < m.rows(); ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < m.cols(); ++j) {
            const double x0 = ox + static_cast<double>(j), y0 = oy + static_cast<double>(m.rows() - 1 - i);
            plot.polygon({x0, x0 + 1, x0 + 1, x0}, {y0, y0, y0 + 1, y0 + 1}, cmap(m(i, j)), true);
        }
}
static void bg_box(RPlot& plot, double cw, double ch) {
    const double aspect = 960.0 / 640.0;
    double bw = cw, bh = cw / aspect;
    if (bh < ch) { bh = ch; bw = ch * aspect; }
    const double ox = (bw - cw) / 2.0, oy = (bh - ch) / 2.0;
    plot.polygon({-ox, cw + ox, cw + ox, -ox}, {-oy, -oy, ch + oy, ch + oy}, {250, 250, 252}, true);
}
static DenseMatrix<double> normalize01(const DenseMatrix<double>& R) {
    double mx = 1e-9;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < R.rows(); ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < R.cols(); ++j) mx = std::max(mx, R(i, j));
    DenseMatrix<double> N(R.rows(), R.cols(), 0.0);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < R.rows(); ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < R.cols(); ++j) N(i, j) = std::max(0.0, R(i, j)) / mx;
    return N;
}

    std::cout << "\n=== LIME and SHAP explanations (4x4 superpixels) vs. LRP ===\n";
    std::cout << std::left << std::setw(14) << "example" << std::right << std::setw(12) << "LIME R^2"
              << std::setw(16) << "SHAP efficiency" << std::setw(14) << "corr LRP,LIME" << std::setw(14)

** Three explanations of the same predictions

vertical            0.2589          0.4281        0.3338        0.2561         0.8620

Two facts stand out. First, the "SHAP efficiency" column is the model's
score budget, \(f(\text{full}) - f(\text{empty})\); SHAP's sixteen Shapley
values sum to it /exactly/ (to machine precision, by construction -- the
efficiency axiom of §2.3), so the attribution leaves nothing unexplained.
Second, LIME and SHAP -- both perturbation-based -- agree strikingly with each
other, correlating at 0.9387 on the horizontal example and 0.8620 on the
vertical one: two methods with quite different derivations (a local linear
fit versus a game-theoretic average) land on nearly the same explanation,
which is reassuring evidence that the explanation is a property of the
/model/, not of the method. LIME's local \(R^2\) is moderate (0.38, 0.26):
the surrogate is a /linear/ approximation to a genuinely nonlinear network,
faithful enough to rank the superpixels but not to reproduce the model
exactly -- exactly the caveat §2.2 flagged.

** Where they agree, and where they differ

The cross-method correlations tell a precise story. LIME-versus-SHAP is
high (0.86-0.94) because both are perturbation-based and, as §2.4 noted, are
close kin -- exact Shapley and a weighted local regression are nearly the
same computation here. LRP-versus-LIME and LRP-versus-SHAP are lower
(0.25-0.33), but /not/ because they disagree about the answer: the figure
shows all three highlighting the same bar. The gap is one of /resolution/,
not location -- LRP's pixel-sharp line and the perturbation methods' patch-
wide band overlap only partially pixel-for-pixel, which a correlation
penalizes even though both point at the bar. The honest reading is that
model-specific and model-agnostic explanations here /corroborate/ one
another: three methods, two of them knowing nothing of the network's
insides, independently attribute the decision to the object.

This is the point of "model-agnostic." Not one line of the LIME or SHAP
code changed; we merely handed the explainers a /different/ prediction
function -- a fully-connected network trained on the flattened pixels, which
shares no architecture with the CNN and has no notion of convolution,
pooling, or a backward pass. LIME and SHAP explain it just the same,
highlighting the bar (figure), and the MLP's SHAP map agrees with the CNN's
at 0.7408: two independently trained, structurally unrelated models attend
to the same evidence, and one method uncovers it in both. (The MLP's small
SHAP-efficiency budget, 0.0646, reflects that this particular network's
score for the target class barely moves between the blank and full images --
a quirk of that model that the explanation faithfully reports rather than
hides.) Where LRP would have needed a bespoke backward pass written for the
CNN's every layer, LIME and SHAP needed only the ability to call each model
and read its output.

* Discussion

**Model-agnostic explanation trades knowledge for generality.** LRP, in the
[[file:cnn_analysis.org][CNN report]], used the network's weights to produce
a pixel-sharp attribution in a single fast backward pass -- but only for a
model whose every layer it was written to understand. LIME and SHAP give
that up: knowing nothing but the prediction function, they explain /any/
model, as §4.3's fully-connected network showed with the same code. The
price is paid in queries and in resolution. LIME must sample and fit; exact
SHAP must enumerate \(2^P\) coalitions -- here 65536 forward passes /per
image/, feasible only because \(P = 16\) is small. And both work at
superpixel granularity, coarser than LRP's pixels. Neither style dominates;
the choice is whether you have (and want to exploit) the model's internals.

**Exactness and axioms are what set SHAP apart.** LIME answers "what linear
model fits the black box near here?", a question whose answer depends on the
sampling, the kernel width, and the ridge penalty -- §4.1's moderate \(R^2\)
is a reminder that the local fit is imperfect. SHAP answers a question with
a /unique/ correct answer: the Shapley value is the only attribution
satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity, and computing it
exactly (§2.3) removes every free parameter but the superpixel grid and the
baseline. §4.1's exact efficiency -- the attributions summing to the last
decimal to \(f(\text{full}) - f(\text{empty})\) -- is not a lucky fit but a
theorem. That rigor is why SHAP is often preferred when the stakes are high,
and why the two methods' strong agreement (§4.2) is worth noting: LIME, far
cheaper, here recovers nearly what the principled method certifies.

**Explanations agree because the explanation is real.** The most reassuring
result is the convergence. Three methods derived from three different
principles -- backward relevance conservation (LRP), local linear
surrogacy (LIME), and cooperative-game fairness (SHAP) -- and two independent
models (CNN and MLP) all point at the bar. When explanations from unrelated
methods and unrelated models corroborate one another, the attribution is
much more likely to reflect a genuine property of what the models learned
than an artifact of any one explainer. The flip side, and the reason these
tools matter, is the case this data does not show: when a model is right for
the wrong reason, it is exactly this kind of cross-checking -- does the
evidence fall where it should, do independent methods agree -- that reveals
it.

* Conclusion

Implementing LIME and SHAP in =datamunge='s C++ =stats::LimeImageExplainer=
and =stats::ShapImageExplainer=, we explain a bar-classifying CNN purely by
perturbing its inputs, with no access to its internals. SHAP's exact
Shapley values, computed by enumerating all 65536 superpixel coalitions,
satisfy the efficiency axiom to machine precision, and LIME's proximity-
weighted linear surrogate recovers nearly the same explanation, the two
correlating at up to 0.94. Placed beside the model-specific LRP, all three
localize the decision to the bar, agreeing on where the evidence lies while
differing in resolution -- pixel-sharp for LRP, superpixel-coarse for the
perturbation methods. And because LIME and SHAP need only a prediction
function, the identical code explains a structurally unrelated
fully-connected network, whose SHAP explanation agrees with the CNN's at
0.74 -- two different models, shown to attend to the same evidence by a
method that knows the internals of neither. As with its companions, this
entire report is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example explainable_ai_analysis=, which re-executes §3.4.5's
assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above -- from the
=datamunge= source tree.
