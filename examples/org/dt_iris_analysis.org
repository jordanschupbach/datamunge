#+begin_abstract
We fit a CART-style binary decision tree to Fisher's (1936) iris data using
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::DecisionTreeClassifier=, and report it as a
literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk
of the single C++ binary that produced every number and figure in this
document. A depth-3 tree separates the three species with 97.33% training
accuracy using just 9 nodes (5 leaves), splitting exclusively on petal
measurements -- the tree assigns sepal length and sepal width literally
zero importance, discovering on its own the same fact both companion
reports (LDA and SVM, on this same data) establish only by inspecting
fitted coefficients. A depth sweep shows accuracy and node count both
saturating by depth 5 (100% training accuracy, 17 nodes), and the axis-
aligned rectangular decision boundary this method produces looks
qualitatively different from either companion report's boundary --
straight-line-but-diagonal for LDA, smoothly curved for a radial-kernel
SVM. We derive recursive partitioning, the Gini/entropy impurity measures,
the greedy split-selection rule, and feature importance from first
principles, connect each to the corresponding library call, and close by
comparing all three methods' boundaries and notions of "confidence" on the
same 150 flowers.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The two companion reports in this project --
[[file:lda_iris_analysis.org][=lda_iris_analysis.org=]] and
[[file:svm_iris_analysis.org][=svm_iris_analysis.org=]] -- both fit a
classifier to Fisher's iris data by solving a single global optimization
problem: an eigenproblem for LDA, a convex quadratic program for SVM. This
report's method could not be more different. A decision tree is built by a
sequence of /local/, greedy choices -- at each step, ask the one yes/no
question about one measurement that most cleanly separates whatever
flowers remain -- and never revisits an earlier choice once made. That
simplicity is also decision trees' main selling point: the fitted model is
not a matrix of coefficients or a set of support vectors but a short,
literal list of thresholds a person can read top to bottom, and this
report's own fitted tree, printed in full in §4.2, is exactly that.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.8) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp= file,
compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is that
program's literal output.

§2 derives recursive partitioning, the Gini and entropy impurity measures,
the greedy split rule that picks each node's question, the stopping rules
that keep a tree from growing without bound, and feature importance, each
tied to the specific library call that implements it. §3 describes the
program chunk by chunk. §4 reports the fitted tree itself, its accuracy,
feature importance, a depth sweep, and the decision-region picture this
method is famous for. §5 discusses what a three-way comparison across all
three companion reports' methods says about this dataset. §6 concludes.

* Background: Decision Tree Classification

** Setup and notation

As in the companion reports: $n = 150$ flowers, $p = 4$ measurements
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$, and $y_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, $K = 3$, labeling
one of the three species.

** Recursive partitioning

A decision tree partitions $\mathbb{R}^p$ into axis-aligned rectangular
regions using a sequence of binary questions of the form "is $x_j \leq t$?"
for some feature $j$ and threshold $t$. CART (Classification and
Regression Trees; Breiman et al., 1984) builds this sequence top-down and
/greedily/: starting from the root (every training row), it picks whichever
single question best separates the data currently at that node, splits the
data into the two children the answer implies, and recurses on each child
independently, stopping per the rules in §2.5. This is fundamentally
different from LDA and SVM: neither the Gini/entropy criterion below nor
any later split is chosen by solving one optimization problem over the
whole tree at once -- each split is only ever locally optimal, which is
both why fitting is fast and interpretable and why a greedy tree can, in
principle, land somewhere a global optimizer wouldn't.

** Measuring impurity

A node is "impure" when the training rows that reach it don't all share
one class. Writing $p_k$ for the fraction of a node's rows belonging to
class $k$, =datamunge= implements both classical impurity measures
(=SplitCriterion=):

\[
\text{Gini:} \quad G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^K p_k^2 , \qquad\qquad
\text{Entropy:} \quad H = -\sum_{k=1}^K p_k \log_2 p_k .
\]

Both are zero exactly when a node is /pure/ (every row the same class,
some $p_k = 1$) and largest when classes are evenly mixed; Gini is the
default and is what this report uses throughout. This is exactly
=DecisionTreeClassifier='s private =impurity_of()=, evaluated fresh at
every node considered during fitting.

** Choosing the best split

For a candidate split of a node's $n$ rows into a left child ($n_L$ rows)
and right child ($n_R$ rows), the split's quality is its /weighted impurity
decrease/:

\[
\Delta = I(\text{parent}) - \frac{n_L}{n} I(\text{left}) - \frac{n_R}{n} I(\text{right}) ,
\]

where $I$ is whichever impurity measure from §2.3 is in use. At each node,
CART scans every predictor and every candidate threshold for that
predictor (the midpoints between consecutive sorted training values) and
keeps whichever single (feature, threshold) pair maximizes $\Delta$. §4.2's
fitted tree's very first split -- ~Petal.Length <= 2.45~ -- is exactly the
single question, out of every question CART considered over all four
measurements, that most reduced Gini impurity at the root.

** Stopping rules and the bias/variance tradeoff

Left unconstrained, a tree can keep splitting until every leaf is
perfectly pure, which usually means memorizing the training set rather
than learning a generalizable rule -- the tree analogue of SVM's small-$C$
/versus/ large-$C$ tradeoff in the companion report. =datamunge= exposes
four independent stopping rules (=DecisionTreeClassifierOptions=):
=max_depth= caps how many splits separate any leaf from the root;
=min_samples_split= refuses to split a node with fewer rows than that;
=min_samples_leaf= refuses a split that would leave either child with
fewer rows than that; and =min_impurity_decrease= refuses a split whose
best available $\Delta$ doesn't clear that threshold. §4.4 measures the
first of these directly, sweeping =max_depth= from 1 to 10 and watching
both training accuracy and tree size grow -- and, eventually, stop
growing -- together.

** Feature importance

Every time a predictor is chosen as a node's splitting feature, that
split's $\Delta$ (weighted by how many rows reached that node) is credited
to the predictor. Summing those credits over the whole tree and
normalizing to sum to 1 gives =feature_importance()=: a predictor a tree
never splits on gets importance exactly zero, not merely small -- unlike
LDA's discriminant coefficients or SVM's kernel weights, which are
essentially never exactly zero for an unhelpful predictor, a tree performs
a hard, binary form of feature selection as a side effect of fitting.

** Prediction

Classifying a new row means walking the fitted tree from the root, at each
internal node following the branch its answer to that node's threshold
question implies, until reaching a leaf. That leaf's =class_counts=
(training rows of each class that landed there during fitting) give both
the predicted label -- the majority class at that leaf -- and, in
=predict_detail()='s =probability= field, the /empirical/ class
proportions among those training rows. This is a third distinct notion of
"confidence," alongside LDA's Bayes posterior and SVM's one-vs-one vote
count: a tree's probability for a new point is literally "what fraction of
training flowers that ended up at the same leaf were this species,"
nothing more model-based than that.

** What we will report

Given the fit, we report the tree itself (§4.2, printed exactly as
=print_summary= writes it), training accuracy and a confusion matrix, a
classification plot marking misclassified points directly, feature
importance, a =max_depth= sweep, and -- reduced to two predictors purely
for visualization, the same caveat the companion reports make about their
own 2-D views -- the tree's decision regions, drawn as filled rectangles
rather than the smooth boundaries LDA and SVM produce.

* Data and Methods

** The data

As in both companion reports: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns
Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ --
measured on =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=
(cm), with no missing values.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, the formula-driven
=stats::DecisionTreeClassifier= for every model in this report, and
=plot::RPlot=/=plot::RLayout= for every figure -- including the
classification and decision-region plots, which the classifier itself
knows how to draw. No external plotting or statistics package is used.

    {
        const std::vector<std::size_t> depths =
            {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10};
        std::vector<double> depth_x, accs, node_counts;
        std::cout << "\nDepth sensitivity:\n";
        std::cout << std::setw(8) << "depth" << std::setw(12) << "accuracy"

The entire model is three questions deep and reads like a field guide:

* Discussion

**Three methods, three boundary shapes, one dataset.** Read together with
its companions, this report completes a genuine three-way comparison on
identical data: LDA draws one straight (diagonal) line, informed by a
pooled covariance estimate over all four measurements; a radial-kernel SVM
draws one smooth curve, informed by a kernel function and a handful of
support vectors; a depth-3 tree draws a small number of straight, axis-
aligned cuts, informed by nothing more sophisticated than "which single
threshold on which single measurement helps most, asked repeatedly."
All three land within a point of each other on training accuracy (98.00%,
97.33%, 97.33%) and make the exact same species of error -- confusing a
handful of /versicolor/ and /virginica/ flowers in the one region of
measurement space where they truly overlap -- because that overlap is a
property of the /data/, not of any one method's assumptions.

**Three notions of confidence.** The three reports also now span three
genuinely different answers to "how sure is the model": LDA's Bayes
posterior is a smooth function of distance to each class's fitted
Gaussian; an SVM one-vs-one vote is a coarse count out of very few
pairwise outcomes; a tree's leaf probability is the plain empirical class
mix of whichever training rows happened to land in the same leaf as the
new point. None of these is strictly "more correct" than the others, but
they carry different amounts of information -- a tree's leaf probability
in particular can only ever take values that were achievable given the
training rows that actually reached that specific leaf (here, thirds,
since the relevant leaf held exactly 3 rows), which is a real limitation
compared to LDA's continuously-valued posterior.

**Interpretability versus the other methods.** §4.2's entire fitted model
fits in eight lines and can be followed by hand for any new flower;
neither LDA's coefficient matrix nor SVM's support vectors and kernel
offer anything comparably direct to read, even though both are fully
specified by comparably few numbers. That directness is the tree's real
advantage on a problem this easy -- and per §2.2, also a hint at its
limits: a greedy method that never revisits a choice can, on harder data
where the first split isn't nearly as clean as ~Petal.Length <= 2.45~
turned out to be here, lock in a worse structure than a method that
solves one problem globally.

**Limitations.** Every accuracy figure in this report is a resubstitution
estimate, exactly the same caveat both companion reports carry. Trees
specifically are also known to be high-variance: a small change to the
training data can produce a visibly different sequence of splits even when
the final accuracy is similar, which is precisely the instability that
ensemble methods built on top of many trees exist to address (=datamunge='s
own =RandomForestClassifier= reuses this exact class internally, drawing a
random predictor subset at each split via =DecisionTreeClassifierOptions::
max_features=) -- a natural next report, and out of scope for this one.

* Conclusion

A depth-3 CART decision tree, fit with =datamunge='s C++
=stats::DecisionTreeClassifier=, separates Fisher's three iris species
with 97.33% training accuracy in just 9 nodes, splitting exclusively on
petal measurements and assigning both sepal measurements exactly zero
importance. A depth sweep traces the same underfit-to-saturate curve the
SVM companion report traces with its cost parameter, reaching perfect
training accuracy by depth 5. Read alongside its two companions, this
report completes a three-way comparison of decision-boundary shape and
prediction "confidence" on identical data -- diagonal-linear-and-
probabilistic for LDA, smoothly-curved-and-vote-based for SVM, rectangular-
and-empirical-frequency-based for this tree -- three different roads to
the same 97-98% answer. As with its companions, this entire report is
reproducible end to end via =just org-example dt_iris_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.3.8's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
