#+begin_abstract
Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common divisor is more than two thousand
years old and still one of the fastest things a computer does: it replaces the
larger of two numbers by its remainder modulo the smaller and repeats, and the
last nonzero remainder is the gcd. Its /extended/ form carries a little more
bookkeeping and pays an outsized dividend -- alongside \(g=\gcd(a,b)\) it
returns integers \(x,y\) with \(ax+by=g\), the *Bezout identity*. Those
coefficients are the key to modular arithmetic: when \(a\) and \(m\) are
coprime, \(ax+my=1\) says \(ax\equiv 1\pmod m\), so \(x\) is the *modular
inverse* of \(a\) -- the operation at the heart of RSA key generation, the
Chinese Remainder Theorem, and rational reconstruction. This report implements
both in =datamunge='s C++ =algorithms::extended_gcd= and =modular_inverse= and
presents them as a literate program: every number and the figure below are
produced by a single compiled binary. We give the algorithm, its coefficient
extension, arguments for correctness and its \(O(\log\min(a,b))\) running time,
a worked example computed by hand -- \(\gcd(240,46)=2=240\cdot(-9)+46\cdot47\)
-- and a modular inverse, \(3^{-1}\equiv 4\pmod{11}\).
#+end_abstract

* The Euclidean algorithm

Let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers, not both zero. Their *greatest common divisor*
\(\gcd(a,b)\) is the largest integer dividing both. Euclid's insight is a single
identity: for \(b\neq 0\),
\[
\gcd(a,b) \;=\; \gcd(b,\; a \bmod b),
\]
because any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\) also divides \(a - qb = a\bmod b\)
for the quotient \(q=\lfloor a/b\rfloor\), and vice versa -- the two pairs share
exactly the same set of common divisors. Since \(a\bmod b\) is strictly smaller
than \(b\), iterating drives the second argument down to \(0\); at that point
\(\gcd(g,0)=g\) reads off the answer as the last nonzero remainder. We take
\(\gcd(0,0)=0\) by convention.

=datamunge= exposes =extended_gcd(a, b)= returning an =ExtendedGcd{gcd, x, y}=,
and =modular_inverse(a, m)= built on top of it.

* Tracking the Bezout coefficients

Plain Euclid throws away everything but the final remainder. The extension keeps
just enough history to /reconstruct/ the gcd as a combination of the original
inputs. Alongside each running remainder \(r\) we carry a pair \((s,t)\)
maintaining the invariant
\[
s\cdot a + t\cdot b \;=\; r .
\]
We seed two rows -- \((r,s,t)=(a,1,0)\) and \((b,0,1)\), which obviously satisfy
it -- and every Euclidean step \(r' \leftarrow r_{\text{old}} - q\,r\) is applied
identically to the coefficients: \(s' \leftarrow s_{\text{old}} - q\,s\) and
\(t' \leftarrow t_{\text{old}} - q\,t\). Linearity guarantees the invariant
survives each step, so when the remainder reaches \(0\) the previous row holds
\((g, x, y)\) with \(ax+by=g\).

#+begin_example
EXTENDED-GCD(a, b):
  (old_r, r) <- (a, b)
  (old_s, s) <- (1, 0)          # s*a + t*b == r  is maintained for both rows
  (old_t, t) <- (0, 1)

  while r != 0:
      q <- old_r / r                        # integer (truncating) quotient
      (old_r, r) <- (r, old_r - q*r)        # Euclidean remainder step
      (old_s, s) <- (s, old_s - q*s)        # same step on a's coefficient
      (old_t, t) <- (t, old_t - q*t)        # same step on b's coefficient

  if old_r < 0:                             # normalise sign (negative inputs)
      (old_r, old_s, old_t) <- (-old_r, -old_s, -old_t)

  return gcd = old_r,  x = old_s,  y = old_t   # a*x + b*y == gcd
#+end_example

*Correctness.* The invariant \(s a + t b = r\) holds initially and is preserved
by the identical linear update, so the returned \((x,y)\) satisfy
\(ax+by=\text{old\_r}\); and old_r is the gcd by the Euclidean argument above.
Truncating division can momentarily leave the gcd negative when an input is
negative, so a final sign flip -- which negates both sides of \(ax+by=g\)
and thus preserves it -- forces \(g\ge 0\).

*Complexity.* Each iteration replaces \((r_{\text{old}}, r)\) by \((r, r_{\text{old}}\bmod r)\).
Two successive remainders sum to at most the larger one, which forces the pair
to shrink geometrically; the worst case is consecutive Fibonacci numbers
(Lame's theorem), giving \(O(\log\min(|a|,|b|))\) division steps. Every step
does a constant amount of 64-bit arithmetic, so the whole computation is
logarithmic in the inputs.

* From Bezout to the modular inverse

The *modular inverse* of \(a\) modulo \(m\) is the residue \(x\in[0,m)\) with
\(a x \equiv 1 \pmod m\). It exists exactly when \(\gcd(a,m)=1\): running the
extended algorithm on \((a,m)\) yields \(x,y\) with
\[
a x + m y = \gcd(a,m) = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; a x \equiv 1 \pmod m,
\]
so \(x\) -- reduced into \([0,m)\) -- is the inverse. If \(\gcd(a,m)>1\) no
inverse can exist, since \(ax\) would always share that factor with \(m\) and
never be \(\equiv 1\); =modular_inverse= throws =std::invalid_argument= in that
case, and also when \(m\le 0\). This single routine underlies division in a
prime field, RSA's computation of the private exponent \(d = e^{-1}\bmod
\varphi(n)\), and the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

* A worked example, by hand

Take \(a=240\), \(b=46\). Running the extended algorithm gives the following
trace; each row is a Euclidean step with quotient \(q=\lfloor
\text{old\_r}/r\rfloor\), remainder \(r\), and the coefficients \((s,t)\)
satisfying \(s\cdot 240 + t\cdot 46 = r\):

#+begin_example
  q  |    r  |   s  |    t     |  check: s*240 + t*46
-----+-------+------+----------+----------------------
  -  |  240  |   1  |    0     |  240
  -  |   46  |   0  |    1     |   46
  5  |   10  |   1  |   -5     |  240 - 230 = 10
  4  |    6  |  -4  |   21     |  -960 + 966 =  6
  1  |    4  |   5  |  -26     |  1200 - 1196 = 4
  1  |    2  |  -9  |   47     |  -2160 + 2162 = 2   <- gcd
  2  |    0  |  23  | -120     |  remainder 0: stop
#+end_example

The last nonzero remainder is \(2=\gcd(240,46)\), read off with its
coefficients \(x=-9\), \(y=47\), so
\[
240\cdot(-9) + 46\cdot 47 \;=\; -2160 + 2162 \;=\; 2 .
\]
For a modular inverse, take \(a=3\), \(m=11\): the extended algorithm gives
\(3\cdot 4 + 11\cdot(-1) = 1\), so \(x=4\) and indeed \(3\cdot 4 = 12 \equiv 1
\pmod{11}\) -- the inverse of \(3\) modulo \(11\) is \(4\).

The program below reproduces these two results, cross-checks the Bezout
identity and the gcd (against =std::gcd=) on a hundred thousand random pairs,
and plots the decreasing remainder sequence of \(\gcd(240,46)\).

#+name: ee-run
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "=== Extended Euclidean algorithm ===\n\n";

    // 1) A worked gcd together with its Bezout coefficients.
    const std::int64_t a = 240, b = 46;
    const ExtendedGcd g = extended_gcd(a, b);
    std::cout << "extended_gcd(" << a << ", " << b << "):\n";
    std::cout << "  gcd = " << g.gcd << ",  x = " << g.x << ",  y = " << g.y << "\n";
    std::cout << "  Bezout check: " << a << "*(" << g.x << ") + " << b << "*(" << g.y << ") = "
              << a * g.x + b * g.y << "\n\n";

    // 2) A modular inverse: 3^{-1} mod 11.
    const std::int64_t v = 3, m = 11;
    const std::int64_t inv = modular_inverse(v, m);
    std::cout << "modular_inverse(" << v << ", " << m << ") = " << inv << "\n";
    std::cout << "  check: " << v << " * " << inv << " mod " << m << " = " << (v * inv) % m << "\n\n";

    // 3) Bezout identity a*x + b*y == gcd, and gcd == std::gcd, on many random pairs.
    std::mt19937_64 rng(20240927);
    std::uniform_int_distribution<std::int64_t> dist(-100000, 100000);
    int checked = 0, ok = 0;
    for (int t = 0; t < 100000; ++t) {
        const std::int64_t p = dist(rng), q = dist(rng);
        const ExtendedGcd e = extended_gcd(p, q);
        const bool identity = (p * e.x + q * e.y == e.gcd);
        const bool matches_std = (e.gcd == static_cast<std::int64_t>(std::gcd(p, q)));
        ++checked;
        if (identity && matches_std) ++ok;
    }
    std::cout << "Randomized check over " << checked << " pairs in [-100000, 100000]:\n";
    std::cout << "  a*x + b*y == gcd AND gcd == std::gcd held in " << ok << " / " << checked
              << " cases\n\n";

    // 4) The decreasing remainder sequence of gcd(240, 46), for the figure.
    std::vector<double> step, rem;
    std::int64_t r0 = a, r1 = b, k = 0;
    step.push_back(static_cast<double>(k++));
    rem.push_back(static_cast<double>(r0));
    while (r1 != 0) {
        step.push_back(static_cast<double>(k++));
        rem.push_back(static_cast<double>(r1));
        const std::int64_t r2 = r0 % r1;
        r0 = r1;
        r1 = r2;
    }
    std::cout << "Remainder sequence for extended_gcd(240, 46): ";
    for (auto rv : rem) std::cout << static_cast<std::int64_t>(rv) << " ";
    std::cout << "\n";

    auto plot = RPlot::create();
    plot.points(step, rem, "remainder r_k", {37, 99, 235}, 6.0);
    plot.lines(step, rem, "", {37, 99, 235}, 1.0);
    plot.title("Euclidean remainder sequence for gcd(240, 46)")
        .x_label("step k").y_label("remainder r_k").background({250, 250, 252});
    plot.save_svg("build/org/extended_euclidean_steps.svg");
    std::cout << "\nwrote extended_euclidean_steps.svg\n";
#+end_src

The compiled binary confirms the hand computation exactly:
\(\gcd(240,46)=2\) with \(240\cdot(-9)+46\cdot47=2\), and \(3^{-1}\equiv
4\pmod{11}\). The Bezout identity and agreement with =std::gcd= hold on all one
hundred thousand random pairs -- including negatives and zeros, where the sign
normalisation keeps the gcd non-negative. The remainder sequence
\(240, 46, 10, 6, 4, 2\) collapses to the gcd in only five division steps, a
concrete instance of the logarithmic bound:

The remainders plunge rapidly toward the gcd rather than decreasing by fixed
amounts -- each is a remainder modulo its predecessor, so the sequence contracts
geometrically. That steepness is exactly why Euclid's algorithm, and the
extension that hands us Bezout coefficients and modular inverses for free, runs
in \(O(\log\min(a,b))\) time and remains the workhorse behind modern
public-key cryptography.
