#+begin_abstract
We implement a convolutional neural network (CNN) -- conv -> ReLU ->
max-pool -> flatten -> dense -> softmax -- trained by backpropagation
through its convolution and pooling layers, in =datamunge='s C++
=stats::CNNClassifier=, and report it as a literate program: every
fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++
binary that produced every number and figure in this document. A CNN
adapts a neural network to images by two structural changes a
fully-connected network lacks: a convolution layer applies one small set
of weights at /every/ spatial position (weight sharing), so it learns
local, translation-equivariant feature detectors with far fewer
parameters, and pooling summarizes those detections to grant a degree of
positional tolerance. We demonstrate the machinery on synthetic 12x12
images of horizontal and vertical bars at random positions: the network
classifies them at 100% test accuracy, its final training cross-entropy
falling to 0.0006, and -- inspecting the eight 3x3 filters it learned -- we
find exactly the horizontal- and vertical-edge detectors the task calls
for, discovered from labels alone. The report's central result concerns
/translation invariance/, and is deliberately honest about where it does
and does not come from. Training on bars confined to the top-left of the
image and testing on the same shapes shifted to the bottom-right -- positions
never seen during training -- we find that convolution's weight sharing is
necessary but /not sufficient/: a CNN with ordinary local (2x2) pooling,
whose dense head still depends on where features land, scores 0% on the
shifted test set, no better than a coin flip inverted. Replacing local
pooling with /global/ pooling -- collapsing each feature map to "did this
filter fire anywhere?", discarding position entirely -- lifts test accuracy
to 82.6%, and does so with just 258 parameters, while a 4706-parameter
fully-connected network manages only 17.2%. All three models fit the
training data perfectly; only the globally-pooled convolutional network
generalizes across position. Finally, we look /inside/ the trained network
with /layer-wise relevance propagation/, which traces a prediction back to
the input pixels that supported it: on our bar images 89-92% of the
relevance lands squarely on the bar itself, and a class-contrastive view
shows a horizontal bar carries strong evidence for "horizontal" and
essentially none for "vertical." We derive the convolution, ReLU and
pooling operations, the softmax/cross-entropy loss, backpropagation through
convolution and max-pooling, the weight-sharing and invariance arguments,
and the z+ relevance-propagation rule, tying each to the corresponding
library call.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The [[file:neural_network_analysis.org][feed-forward network report]]
classifies data by flattening it into a vector and learning a fully-
connected map. For images that is wasteful and fragile. Wasteful, because
a fully-connected layer learns a separate weight for every (pixel, unit)
pair, ignoring that an image's meaning is built from /local/ patterns --
edges, corners, textures -- repeated across the frame. Fragile, because
having learned that "a bright patch here means class X," the network knows
nothing about the same patch appearing /there/: shift the object a few
pixels and the learned weights no longer apply.

A convolutional neural network fixes both by changing the architecture to
respect image structure. Its central layer, the /convolution/, slides one
small bank of weights -- a filter -- across every position of the image,
computing the same local operation everywhere. This /weight sharing/ means
a horizontal-edge detector, once learned, is applied at every location for
free, so the network needs vastly fewer parameters, and its feature maps
are /translation-equivariant/: move the input, and the detections move
with it. A subsequent /pooling/ step then summarizes each detector's
responses, trading spatial precision for tolerance to small shifts. This
report builds a small but genuine CNN -- convolution, ReLU, pooling, a
dense head, softmax, and backpropagation through all of it -- and shows
both what it buys and, honestly, exactly where its celebrated translation
invariance comes from.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.4.8) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp= file,
compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is that
program's literal output.

§2 derives the convolution, ReLU and pooling layers, the softmax/cross-
entropy objective, backpropagation through convolution and max-pooling,
the weight-sharing and invariance arguments, and layer-wise relevance
propagation -- each tied to the specific piece of =CNNClassifier= that
implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by chunk. §4 reports the
bar-classification accuracy, the learned filters, the training loss curve,
the relevance-propagation explanations, and the translation-invariance
experiment. §5 discusses what convolution buys, where invariance really
lives, and what the network attends to. §6 concludes.

* Background: Convolutional Neural Networks

** Setup and notation

We classify single-channel (grayscale) images: each example is an \(H
\times W\) matrix of pixel intensities in \([0, 1]\), with a categorical
label in one of \(K\) classes. Because image structure is intrinsically
two-dimensional, =CNNClassifier= takes images directly as a vector of
=linalg::DenseMatrix= objects (all the same size) plus their labels,
rather than through the tabular formula interface the other classifiers
use. The network here has a single convolution layer of \(F\) filters
(=n_filters=), each \(k \times k\) (=kernel_size=).

** The convolution layer

The convolution layer is the heart of the architecture. Each filter
\(f\) is a small \(k \times k\) weight matrix \(W^{(f)}\) with a bias
\(b_f\), and it produces a /feature map/ by computing, at every valid
position \((i, j)\), a weighted sum of the \(k \times k\) image patch
anchored there:

\[
Z^{(f)}_{ij} = b_f + \sum_{a=0}^{k-1} \sum_{b=0}^{k-1}
   X_{\,i+a,\; j+b}\; W^{(f)}_{ab} .
\]

(This is technically a /cross-correlation/, the convention every practical
CNN library uses.) The decisive property is that the /same/ weights
\(W^{(f)}\) are used at every position \((i, j)\): the filter is /shared/
across the whole image. A fully-connected layer would learn an independent
weight for each pixel of each output; the convolution learns just \(k^2\)
weights per filter and reuses them everywhere. =CNNClassifier::fit= builds
these feature maps in its forward pass (the =conv_pre= cache), one \(f\)
per filter, of size \((H-k+1) \times (W-k+1)\) under the "valid"
convolution used here.

** ReLU and pooling

Each feature map passes through a ReLU nonlinearity, \(\max(0, Z^{(f)})\),
exactly as in the [[file:neural_network_analysis.org][dense network]] --
without it, stacked linear maps would collapse. Then a /max-pooling/ layer
downsamples each map by taking the maximum over non-overlapping \(p \times
p\) windows (=pool_size=):

\[
P^{(f)}_{pq} = \max_{0 \le a,b < p}
   \operatorname{ReLU}\!\bigl(Z^{(f)}_{\,p\cdot p_{\mathrm{idx}}+a,\; \ldots}\bigr) .
\]

Pooling serves two ends: it shrinks the representation, and it grants
/local/ translation tolerance -- a feature shifted by less than the pool
window lands in the same pooled cell. Its extreme, /global/ pooling
(a window covering the entire feature map, so each map collapses to a
single number), discards position entirely and is what §4.5 shows confers
genuine invariance. =fit= records, for each pooled cell, the argmax
position (=pool_arg=), which backpropagation needs.

** The dense head, softmax, and loss

The pooled feature maps are flattened into one long vector and fed to a
fully-connected /dense/ hidden layer (=dense_hidden= units, ReLU), whose
output a final linear layer turns into \(K\) class scores. As in the dense
network, a softmax converts the scores to probabilities and the network
minimizes the cross-entropy against the true labels,

\[
\hat{p}_c = \frac{e^{s_c}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}, \qquad
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \hat{p}_{i, y_i} .
\]

So a CNN is a feature-extracting front end (convolution and pooling)
bolted onto the same softmax classifier the dense-network report already
used; =loss_curve()= records this cross-entropy each epoch.

** Backpropagation through convolution and pooling

Training uses backpropagation, extended to the two new layer types. The
dense head's gradients are identical to the ordinary network's. Through
/max-pooling/, the gradient is /routed/: since each pooled output was the
max of its window, only that argmax input affected the loss, so the
incoming gradient flows to that one position and the rest receive zero --
the =pool_arg= cache is exactly what makes this routing possible. Through
the /convolution/, weight sharing means each filter weight influenced the
loss at every position it was applied, so its gradient sums those
contributions,

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(f)}_{ab}} =
   \sum_{i,j} X_{\,i+a,\;j+b}\; \Delta^{(f)}_{ij} ,
\]

where \(\Delta^{(f)}\) is the (post-pool, post-ReLU) gradient at the
convolution output. =CNNClassifier::fit= implements exactly this: route
the flattened gradient back through the argmax positions, mask it by the
ReLU, and accumulate each filter's gradient as a correlation of the input
patches with that error signal, then take a mini-batch SGD step.

** Why convolution: parameters and invariance

Weight sharing has two consequences this report measures directly. First,
/parameter efficiency/: a convolution learns \(F k^2\) filter weights
regardless of image size, where a dense layer over the same pixels learns
a weight per pixel per unit -- §4.5's globally-pooled CNN classifies with
258 parameters against a fully-connected network's 4706. Second,
/equivariance/: because the same filter runs everywhere, shifting the
input shifts the feature map identically, so a feature learned at one
location is recognized at any other. Turning that equivariance into
/invariance/ -- a prediction that does not change when the input shifts --
requires pooling to discard the position information, and §4.5 shows this
is the crux: convolution alone is not enough.

** Explaining predictions: layer-wise relevance propagation

A trained network's decision can be opaque -- which pixels actually made it
say "horizontal"? /Layer-wise relevance propagation/ (LRP; Bach et al.,
2015) answers this by running a conservation principle backward through the
network. It starts with a single number, the target class's output score,
and redistributes it layer by layer -- each neuron passing the relevance it
received down to the inputs that most contributed to it -- until every unit
of relevance has been assigned to an input pixel, producing a heatmap of
support. =CNNClassifier::relevance= implements the /z+ rule/ (LRP-
\(\alpha_1\beta_0\)), which for a linear step \(z_j = \sum_i a_i w_{ij}\)
with non-negative activations \(a_i\) distributes each \(R_j\) in
proportion to the /positive/ contributions,

\[
R_i = \sum_j \frac{a_i\, w_{ij}^{+}}{\sum_{i'} a_{i'} w_{i'j}^{+}}\; R_j ,
\qquad w^{+} = \max(0, w) ,
\]

so relevance stays non-negative and (up to the bias terms) is conserved
\(\sum_i R_i \approx \sum_j R_j\). This rule suits our network exactly: ReLU
makes every hidden activation non-negative and the input pixels lie in
\([0,1]\). =relevance= applies it through each layer in turn -- the output-
to-dense and dense-to-flatten steps by the formula above, max-pooling by
routing each pooled cell's relevance to the /argmax/ position it came from
(the =pool_arg= cache), and the convolution back to the pixels by the same
z+ rule over each filter's receptive field -- returning an
\(H \times W\) relevance map. §4.4 shows these maps land squarely on the
object the network is classifying.

** What we will report

Given the fits, we report the CNN's accuracy on bar images with example

layer-wise relevance-propagation explanations of individual predictions,
and the translation-invariance experiment comparing local-pooled and
globally-pooled convolutional networks against a fully-connected one on
shifted test positions.

* Data and Methods

** Synthetic bar images

To have a task with known structure, we generate \(12 \times 12\) grayscale
images, each containing a thickness-2 /bar/: a horizontal bar spans the
full width at a random row (class "horizontal"), a vertical bar spans the
full height at a random column (class "vertical"), and independent
Gaussian noise is added to every pixel. The two classes differ only in the
/orientation/ of an edge, which is precisely what a small convolution
filter can detect -- and, by weight sharing, detect wherever the bar
happens to sit. We generate 600 such images (300 per class) and split them
70/30 into training and test sets.

** The translation experiment

To probe translation invariance we construct a deliberately adversarial
split: bars appear only in the /top-left/ region during training (rows or
columns 0-3) and only in the /bottom-right/ region at test (rows or columns
8-10) -- positions the network never sees while learning. A model that has
merely memorized which pixels are bright for each class cannot transfer;
only one that has learned a position-independent notion of "horizontal
edge versus vertical edge" can. We compare three models on this split: a
CNN with local (2x2) pooling, a CNN with global pooling, and -- via the
[[file:neural_network_analysis.org][companion]] =MLPClassifier= on the
flattened pixels -- a fully-connected network.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:

(and =stats::MLPClassifier= plus =dstruct::DataFrame= for the

static constexpr std::size_t H = 12, W = 12;

// A 12x12 image with a thickness-2 bar: horizontal (a full-width bar at row `pos`) or vertical
// (a full-height bar at column `pos`), plus Gaussian pixel noise.
static DenseMatrix<double> make_bar(bool horizontal, std::size_t pos, std::mt19937_64& rng) {
    std::normal_distribution<double> noise(0.0, 0.08);
    DenseMatrix<double> img(H, W, 0.0);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < H; ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < W; ++j) {
            double v = 0.0;
            if (horizontal && (i == pos || i == pos + 1)) v = 1.0;
            if (!horizontal && (j == pos || j == pos + 1)) v = 1.0;
            img(i, j) = std::clamp(v + noise(rng), 0.0, 1.0);
        }
    return img;
}

// Flatten images into a DataFrame with pixel columns p0..p{HW-1} plus a class column, so the
// fully-connected MLPClassifier can be fit on the same data as the CNN.
static DataFrame to_pixel_df(const std::vector<DenseMatrix<double>>& imgs, const std::vector<std::string>& labels) {
    DataFrame d;
    for (std::size_t p = 0; p < H * W; ++p) {
        std::vector<double> col(imgs.size());
        for (std::size_t n = 0; n < imgs.size(); ++n) col[n] = imgs[n](p / W, p % W);
        d.add_column("p" + std::to_string(p), col);
    }
    d.add_column("class", labels);
    return d;
}

// Draw a matrix as a grid of filled squares at offset (ox, oy); cmap maps a value to a color.
template <typename Cmap>
static void draw_grid(RPlot& plot, const DenseMatrix<double>& m, double ox, double oy, Cmap cmap) {
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < m.rows(); ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < m.cols(); ++j) {
            const double x0 = ox + static_cast<double>(j);
            const double y0 = oy + static_cast<double>(m.rows() - 1 - i);
            plot.polygon({x0, x0 + 1, x0 + 1, x0}, {y0, y0, y0 + 1, y0 + 1}, cmap(m(i, j)), true);
        }
}

// Draw a background rectangle padding content of size (cw x ch) to the canvas 3:2 aspect, so the
// unit cells above render as squares rather than being stretched by independent x/y scaling.
static void bg_box(RPlot& plot, double cw, double ch) {
    const double aspect = 960.0 / 640.0;
    double bw = cw, bh = cw / aspect;
    if (bh < ch) { bh = ch; bw = ch * aspect; }
    const double ox = (bw - cw) / 2.0, oy = (bh - ch) / 2.0;
    plot.polygon({-ox, cw + ox, cw + ox, -ox}, {-oy, -oy, ch + oy, ch + oy}, {250, 250, 252}, true);
}

*** Explaining predictions with relevance propagation

For a handful of test images we compute the LRP relevance map of the
predicted class and draw the input beside its heatmap, reporting the
fraction of relevance that falls on the actual bar pixels; then, for one
horizontal bar, we contrast the relevance for the "horizontal" target

#+name: cnn-lrp
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Explaining predictions with layer-wise relevance propagation ===\n";
    {
        auto gray = [](double v) { const int g = static_cast<int>(std::clamp(255.0 * (1.0 - v), 0.0, 255.0));
                                   return RGB{static_cast<std::uint8_t>(g), static_cast<std::uint8_t>(g), static_cast<std::uint8_t>(g)}; };
        auto redmap = [](double t) { t = std::clamp(t, 0.0, 1.0);
                                     return RGB{255, static_cast<std::uint8_t>(255 * (1 - t)), static_cast<std::uint8_t>(255 * (1 - t))}; };
        auto normalized = [](const DenseMatrix<double>& R) {
            double mx = 1e-9;
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < R.rows(); ++i)
                for (std::size_t j = 0; j < R.cols(); ++j) mx = std::max(mx, R(i, j));
            DenseMatrix<double> N(R.rows(), R.cols(), 0.0);
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < R.rows(); ++i)
                for (std::size_t j = 0; j < R.cols(); ++j) N(i, j) = std::max(0.0, R(i, j)) / mx;
            return N;
        };

        // Pick two horizontal and two vertical test images.
        std::vector<std::size_t> picks;
        for (const std::string& want : {"horizontal", "vertical"}) {
            std::size_t count = 0;
            for (std::size_t n = 0; n < te_img.size() && count < 2; ++n)
                if (te_lab[n] == want) { picks.push_back(n); ++count; }
        }

        // Figure: inputs (top row) and their LRP relevance heatmaps (bottom row).
        auto plot = RPlot::create();
        bg_box(plot, 4 * (W + 2) - 2, 2 * (H + 2) - 2);
        std::cout << std::left << std::setw(14) << "true class" << std::right << std::setw(14) << "predicted"
                  << std::setw(22) << "relevance on the bar\n";
        for (std::size_t c = 0; c < picks.size(); ++c) {
            const auto& img = te_img[picks[c]];
            const auto R = cnn.relevance(img);  // explain the predicted class
            draw_grid(plot, img, static_cast<double>(c) * (W + 2), 1 * (H + 2), gray);
            draw_grid(plot, normalized(R), static_cast<double>(c) * (W + 2), 0.0, redmap);
            // Concentration: fraction of positive relevance falling on actual bar pixels (input > 0.5).
            double total = 0.0, on_bar = 0.0;
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < H; ++i)
                for (std::size_t j = 0; j < W; ++j) {
                    total += std::max(0.0, R(i, j));
                    if (img(i, j) > 0.5) on_bar += std::max(0.0, R(i, j));
                }
            std::cout << std::left << std::setw(14) << te_lab[picks[c]] << std::right << std::setw(14)
                      << cnn.predict({img})[0] << std::setw(20) << (on_bar / total) << "\n";
        }

*** The translation-invariance experiment

#+name: cnn-translation
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Translation invariance: train on one region, test on a shifted one ===\n";
    // Bars appear only in the top-left region during training, then only in the bottom-right region
    // at test -- positions never seen while learning. What generalizes to the new positions?
    {
        std::mt19937_64 trng(7);
        std::uniform_int_distribution<std::size_t> top(0, 3), bottom(8, 10);
        std::vector<DenseMatrix<double>> train_i, test_i;
        std::vector<std::string> train_l, test_l;
        const std::size_t m = 250;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < m; ++i) {
            train_i.push_back(make_bar(true, top(trng), trng));    train_l.push_back("horizontal");
            train_i.push_back(make_bar(false, top(trng), trng));   train_l.push_back("vertical");
            test_i.push_back(make_bar(true, bottom(trng), trng));  test_l.push_back("horizontal");
            test_i.push_back(make_bar(false, bottom(trng), trng)); test_l.push_back("vertical");
        }
        // Convolution with LOCAL (2x2) pooling: a flatten+dense head still depends on WHERE features
        // are, so novel positions are unseen. GLOBAL pooling (a 10x10 window over the whole conv map)
        // collapses each feature map to "did this filter fire anywhere?", discarding position.
        CNNClassifier cnn_local(train_i, train_l, CNNClassifierOptions{8, 3, 2, 16, 0.05, 40, 16, 0.0, 1});
        CNNClassifier cnn_global(train_i, train_l, CNNClassifierOptions{8, 3, 10, 16, 0.05, 40, 16, 0.0, 1});

        DataFrame mlp_train_df = to_pixel_df(train_i, train_l);
        DataFrame mlp_test_df  = to_pixel_df(test_i, test_l);
        MLPClassifier mlp(mlp_train_df, "class ~ .", MLPClassifierOptions{{32}, "relu", 0.05, 200, 16, 0.0, 1});

        std::cout << std::left << std::setw(28) << "model" << std::right << std::setw(10) << "params"
                  << std::setw(12) << "train acc" << std::setw(16) << "test (shifted)\n";
        auto row = [&](const std::string& name, std::size_t params, double tr, double te) {
            std::cout << std::left << std::setw(28) << name << std::right << std::setw(10) << params << std::setw(12)
                      << tr << std::setw(16) << te << "\n";
        };
        row("CNN, local 2x2 pooling", cnn_local.n_parameters(), cnn_local.training_accuracy(),
            cnn_local.accuracy(test_i, test_l));
        row("CNN, global pooling", cnn_global.n_parameters(), cnn_global.training_accuracy(),
            cnn_global.accuracy(test_i, test_l));
        row("MLP, fully connected", mlp.n_parameters(), mlp.training_accuracy(), mlp.accuracy(mlp_test_df));
    }

The network -- eight \(3 \times 3\) filters, then ReLU, \(2 \times 2\)
pooling, a 32-unit dense layer, and a softmax -- classifies the held-out
images at 100% accuracy, its training cross-entropy driven down to 0.0006.
Because the bars appear at /random/ positions in both the training and
test sets here, the network sees the full range of positions during
training, and its weight-shared filters plus the dense head handle them
easily. §4.5 makes the harder demand, testing at positions never seen.

** The filters it learned

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Eight learned 3x3 filters shown as red/blue heatmaps, several resembling edge detectors :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/cnn_filters.svg]]

Inspecting the eight \(3 \times 3\) filters the network learned -- red for
positive weights, blue for negative -- reveals it discovered exactly the
detectors the task calls for, from labels alone. Several filters show a
clear /horizontal/ split (a red row above a blue row, or vice versa): a
horizontal-edge detector, which fires where image intensity changes from
top to bottom, as at the boundary of a horizontal bar. Others show a
/vertical/ split (red column beside blue column): a vertical-edge detector
for the sides of a vertical bar. This is representation learning made
visible -- nobody told the network what an edge is; minimizing
classification loss made edge detectors the useful thing to build, and
weight sharing made them reusable at every position.

** The training loss curve

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Training cross-entropy falling steeply then flattening toward zero :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/cnn_loss_curve.svg]]

Backpropagation through the pooling and convolution layers works exactly
as it does for a dense network: the mean training cross-entropy falls
steeply over the first several epochs and then flattens toward zero as the
filters settle into good edge detectors. The gradient routed through the
argmax pooling positions and accumulated across each filter's shared
weights (§2.5) is enough to train the whole stack end to end.

** Explaining predictions with relevance propagation

#+begin_example
true class         predicted relevance on the bar
horizontal        horizontal              0.9210
horizontal        horizontal              0.9221
vertical            vertical              0.8975
vertical            vertical              0.9021
#+end_example

Having a network that is 100% accurate is not the same as knowing /why/ it
decides as it does. Layer-wise relevance propagation (§2.7) opens the box:
for each test image it traces the predicted class's score back to the input
pixels. The number that matters is in the last column -- the fraction of the
total relevance that lands on the /actual bar pixels/ (those with intensity
above 0.5), as opposed to the noisy background. Across the four examples it
is 0.90 to 0.92: nine-tenths of the network's evidence comes from the bar
itself, confirming that it classifies on the object and not on some
spurious artifact of the background noise.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Four input images on top with their red LRP relevance heatmaps below, each highlighting the bar :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/cnn_lrp.svg]]

The picture makes it concrete. The top row shows four test inputs -- two
horizontal bars, two vertical bars, at different positions; the bottom row
shows each one's LRP relevance as a red heatmap. In every case the
relevance concentrates exactly on the bar, tracking its position and
orientation: red rows under the horizontal bars, red columns under the
vertical ones, with the noisy background left nearly white. The network
looks where a person would.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt A horizontal bar with strong red relevance for the horizontal class and a nearly blank map for the vertical class :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/cnn_lrp_contrast.svg]]

Relevance is /class-specific/, and that is where it becomes an explanation
rather than a saliency map. For a single horizontal bar (left), asking "why
horizontal?" produces the strong red bar in the middle panel -- the pixels
that evidence the winning class -- while asking "why vertical?" produces the
nearly blank panel on the right: there is essentially /no/ evidence in this
image for the class it did not pick. LRP thus answers not just "where did
the network look" but "which pixels supported /this/ decision over the
alternative," the question interpretability is really after.

** Translation invariance: where it really comes from

#+begin_example
model                           params   train acc test (shifted)
CNN, local 2x2 pooling            3330      1.0000          0.0000
CNN, global pooling                258      1.0000          0.8260
MLP, fully connected              4706      1.0000          0.1720
#+end_example

This is the report's central -- and most honest -- result. All three models
are trained on bars in the /top-left/ and tested on bars in the /bottom-
right/, positions never seen in training, and all three fit the training
data perfectly (train accuracy 1.0000). What differs is transfer to the
new positions. The fully-connected network scores 17.2%: having learned
which specific pixels are bright for each class, it is lost when the bright
pixels move, and 4706 parameters do not help. Strikingly, the convolutional
network with ordinary /local/ (2x2) pooling does /worse/, 0.0%: its filters
are shared and translation-equivariant, but the flatten-and-dense head
still reads /where/ each feature landed, so a feature appearing in the
bottom-right activates dense weights that were trained on the top-left, and
the prediction flips. Only the /globally/-pooled CNN transfers: by
collapsing each feature map to a single "did this filter fire anywhere?"
value, it discards position and reaches 82.6% -- and it does so with just
258 parameters, an eighteenth of the fully-connected network's. The lesson
is precise: weight sharing gives convolution its efficiency and
equivariance, but translation /invariance/ is a separate property that
comes specifically from pooling away the spatial dimensions.

* Discussion

**Convolution learns the right features, and shares them.** §4.2 is
representation learning caught in the act: the network was told only
"horizontal" or "vertical," yet it built horizontal- and vertical-edge
detectors, because those are what minimize the loss -- and, being shared
filters, each is learned once and applied everywhere. This is the standard
that dispenses with hand-designed image features: rather than a human
choosing edge filters, gradient descent discovers them, and the same
mechanism scales up to the rich hierarchies of features that make deep CNNs
work on real images. The 258-parameter globally-pooled model in §4.5 shows
how frugal this can be relative to connecting every pixel to every unit.

**Accuracy is not understanding, and relevance propagation supplies some.**
A model that is right 100% of the time can still be right for the wrong
reasons -- keying on a background correlation, a watermark, a dataset
artifact -- and no accuracy number would reveal it. §4.4's relevance maps
are a cheap, principled check: by running the prediction backward under a
conservation rule (§2.7), they show that our network's evidence really does
come from the bar (0.90-0.92 of it) and not the noise, and that its evidence
is /class-specific/ -- strong for the class it chose, absent for the one it
did not. This is the same machinery, unchanged in principle, that flags
when a real image classifier has latched onto the wrong cue; building it
from the same forward-pass caches the training already needs makes
interpretability a byproduct rather than an afterthought.

**Equivariance and invariance are different, and the difference is
pooling.** The temptation is to say "CNNs are translation invariant," but
§4.5 shows that is imprecise, and the imprecision matters. Convolution is
translation /equivariant/: shift the input and the feature maps shift with
it. That is not the same as an invariant /prediction/, and a CNN whose
dense head reads the full feature map -- the standard local-pooling design --
is /not/ position-invariant, which is exactly why it scored 0% on shifted
bars while training perfectly. Invariance is manufactured by /discarding/
position, and global pooling is the operation that does it. (In practice,
networks also buy invariance cheaply through /data augmentation/ -- training
on randomly shifted copies -- which is the analogue of §4.1's random-
position training set, where even the local-pooling CNN succeeded because
it saw every position.) Naming the mechanism precisely is the difference
between a model that transfers and one that silently does not.

**A CNN is a feature extractor bolted to a classifier.** Structurally
(§2.4) the network is nothing more exotic than the convolution-and-pooling
front end feeding the very same softmax cross-entropy classifier the
[[file:neural_network_analysis.org][dense-network report]] used, trained by
the very same backpropagation, merely extended (§2.5) to route gradients
through pooling and to sum them across shared filter weights. This is worth
holding onto against the mystique of "deep learning": each ingredient here
-- an affine map, a nonlinearity, a pooling summary, a softmax, a gradient
step -- is simple and was implemented from scratch above, and it is their
composition, plus the architectural priors of locality and weight sharing,
that turns them into a working image classifier.

* Conclusion

Implementing a convolutional neural network in =datamunge='s C++
=stats::CNNClassifier= -- convolution, ReLU, pooling, a dense head, softmax,
and backpropagation through all of it -- we classify synthetic bar images at
100% test accuracy with the training cross-entropy driven to 0.0006, and
find that the eight filters it learns are precisely the horizontal- and
vertical-edge detectors the task rewards, discovered from labels alone. The
translation experiment is the report's central and most careful result:
trained on shapes in one region and tested on the same shapes shifted to
another, a fully-connected network transfers at only 17.2% and -- revealingly
-- a convolutional network with ordinary local pooling does worse still, 0%,
because its dense head remains position-sensitive; only a globally-pooled
CNN, which discards position, generalizes, reaching 82.6% with just 258
parameters against the fully-connected network's 4706. Convolution's weight
sharing buys parameter efficiency and equivariance; translation invariance
is the separate gift of pooling. And looking inside the trained network
with layer-wise relevance propagation confirms it decides for the right
reasons -- 0.90 to 0.92 of each prediction's evidence lands on the bar
itself, and that evidence is specific to the class chosen -- turning a black
box into an inspectable one with the same forward-pass machinery training
already builds. As with its companions, this entire report
is reproducible end to end via =just org-example cnn_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.4.8's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure
above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
