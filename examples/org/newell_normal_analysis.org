#+begin_abstract
A triangle's normal is one cross product, but a polygon with more vertices may be slightly
*non-planar*, and picking two edges to cross gives an unreliable, vertex-order-dependent
answer -- especially at near-collinear corners. *Newell's method* computes a robust,
area-weighted normal by summing a contribution from every edge. This report implements it in
=datamunge='s =algorithms::newell_normal= as a literate program: we recover the exact normal
of a planar square, match a tilted triangle's cross-product normal, and read the polygon area
straight out of the same computation.
#+end_abstract

* Summing projected areas

Newell's normal accumulates, over the polygon's edges taken cyclically,
\[
  n_x = \sum_i (y_i - y_{i+1})(z_i + z_{i+1}),\quad
  n_y = \sum_i (z_i - z_{i+1})(x_i + x_{i+1}),\quad
  n_z = \sum_i (x_i - x_{i+1})(y_i + y_{i+1}).
\]
Each component is twice the *signed area* of the polygon projected onto a coordinate plane
(it is the shoelace formula in disguise), so the vector \((n_x,n_y,n_z)\) is the polygon's
projected-area vector. Its direction is the average surface normal -- stable even when the
vertices are not exactly coplanar or when some corners are nearly collinear (those edges just
contribute little) -- and its length is *twice the polygon's area*. So one loop yields both
the plane orientation and the area. This is the standard way to find a face's supporting
plane in mesh processing, back-face culling, and the depth sort of the painter's algorithm.

** Pseudocode

#+begin_example
newell_normal(polygon):
    n = (0,0,0)
    for i in 0..m-1:
        cur = polygon[i];  nxt = polygon[(i+1) mod m]
        n.x += (cur.y - nxt.y) * (cur.z + nxt.z)
        n.y += (cur.z - nxt.z) * (cur.x + nxt.x)
        n.z += (cur.x - nxt.x) * (cur.y + nxt.y)
    area = 0.5 * length(n)
    return normalize(n), area
#+end_example

* A worked example

We compute the normal of a planar square in the \(z=0\) plane (expect \((0,0,1)\) and area
4), a tilted triangle (compare to its cross product), and a slightly *non-planar* quad to
show Newell averages gracefully where a single cross product would not.

#+name: nn-run
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << std::fixed << std::setprecision(4);

    std::vector<Point3D> square = {{0,0,0},{2,0,0},{2,2,0},{0,2,0}};
    auto ns = newell_normal(square);
    std::cout << "=== Planar square in z=0 plane ===\n";
    std::cout << "normal = (" << ns[0] << ", " << ns[1] << ", " << ns[2] << ")  (expect 0,0,1)\n";
    std::cout << "area   = " << newell_polygon_area(square) << "  (expect 4)\n";

    std::vector<Point3D> tri = {{0,0,0},{1,0,1},{0,1,1}};
    auto nt = newell_normal(tri);
    double inv = 1.0/std::sqrt(3.0);
    std::cout << "\n=== Tilted triangle ===\n";
    std::cout << "Newell normal = (" << nt[0] << ", " << nt[1] << ", " << nt[2] << ")\n";
    std::cout << "cross product = (" << -inv << ", " << -inv << ", " << inv << ")\n";

    std::vector<Point3D> quad = {{0,0,0},{2,0,0.1},{2,2,0},{0,2,-0.1}};  // slightly warped
    auto nq = newell_normal(quad);
    std::cout << "\n=== Slightly non-planar quad (Newell averages the tilt) ===\n";
    std::cout << "normal = (" << nq[0] << ", " << nq[1] << ", " << nq[2] << ")\n";
    std::cout << "area   = " << newell_polygon_area(quad) << "\n";

    // Plot the square (xy projection) and its normal direction as an arrow.
    RPlot p = RPlot::create();
    p.polygon({0,2,2,0}, {0,0,2,2}, {191, 219, 254}, 1.0);
    p.segments({1}, {1}, {1.0 + ns[0]}, {1.0 + ns[1]}, {220, 38, 38}, 2.5);  // normal (projects to a point for z-normal)
    p.text(1.0, 1.0, "n=(0,0,1)  (out of page)", {17,24,39}, 13.0);
    p.save_svg("build/org/newell_square.svg");

The planar square gives the exact normal \((0,0,1)\) and area 4, and the tilted triangle's
Newell normal matches its cross product to full precision. The warped quad -- whose corners
do not share a plane -- still yields a sensible averaged normal and area, where naively
crossing two of its edges would depend on which pair you chose. One loop over the edges
delivers both the orientation and the area.

* Uses and cautions

Newell's method is the go-to for a polygon's supporting plane: face normals in mesh
processing, back-face culling, lighting, and the depth ordering of the painter's algorithm.
Notes: the normal's sign follows the vertex winding (reverse the order to flip it); a
degenerate (zero-area or collinear) polygon yields a zero vector, which callers should guard;
and for a genuinely curved surface a single face normal is only an average -- per-vertex
normals give smoother shading.

* Complexity

\(O(m)\) time for an \(m\)-vertex polygon -- one pass over the edges -- and \(O(1)\) memory.
