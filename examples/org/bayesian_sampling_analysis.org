#+begin_abstract
We fit a correlated bivariate Gaussian target -- deliberately unnormalized,
with a known true mean $(2,-1)$ and a known true log-normalizing constant
$\approx1.6941$ -- using three gradient-free =datamunge= C++ =bayes=
methods, and report it as a literate program: every fragment below is a
real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++ binary that produced
every number and figure in this document. Random-walk Metropolis (RWM)
recovers the mean as $(1.9704, -0.9578)$ with an acceptance rate of
$0.2332$, essentially exactly its 0.234 theoretical target (Roberts,
Gelman & Gilks 1997); Metropolis-within-Gibbs recovers $(2.0274,
-0.9646)$ with per-coordinate acceptance rates $(0.4346, 0.4410)$
against its own, different theoretical target of 0.44 (Roberts &
Rosenthal 2001); and self-normalized importance sampling against a
wider, uncorrelated Gaussian proposal recovers $(2.0127, -0.9650)$ with
an effective sample size of 3708 out of 5000 draws and an estimated
log-normalizer of $1.6856$, within 0.0085 of the true value with zero
Markov-chain machinery at all. A direct scatter overlay shows RWM and
Metropolis-within-Gibbs converging to visually identical elliptical
posterior shapes despite entirely different proposal mechanisms (a
joint multivariate step versus one coordinate at a time), and a
weight-tiered scatter of the importance-sampling proposal draws reveals
a genuine, non-obvious structural fact: the top 5% of importance weights
concentrate almost exactly along the target's correlated major axis,
visualizing precisely how self-normalized weights correct for a
circular proposal's mismatch with an elliptical target. We derive the
Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule, coordinate-wise
Metropolis-within-Gibbs updating, and the self-normalized importance-sampling estimator
(including its effective-sample-size diagnostic), tying every derivation
to the exact library call that implements it.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The [[file:bayesian_modeling_analysis.org][Bayesian modeling companion
report]] characterized a posterior using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and the
No-U-Turn Sampler -- both of which require the log-posterior's exact
*gradient*, obtained there via reverse-mode automatic differentiation.
That gradient is not always available: a black-box simulation model, a
posterior evaluated by an external program, a discontinuous or
non-differentiable log-density, or simply a caller in a language
without access to this library's C++-only autodiff facade all need a
sampler that works from log-density *evaluations alone*. This report
covers =datamunge='s three gradient-free =bayes= tools: random-walk
Metropolis (RWM), the classical Metropolis-Hastings baseline;
Metropolis-within-Gibbs, which updates one coordinate at a time instead
of the whole parameter vector jointly; and importance sampling, which
produces a weighted i.i.d. sample in one shot with no Markov chain, no
warmup, and no mixing to check at all.

As in the Bayesian modeling companion report, this report's target has a
*known answer*: a deliberately unnormalized correlated bivariate
Gaussian log-density with true mean $(2,-1)$ and a true log-normalizing
constant computable in closed form ($\approx1.6941$), so every method's
posterior-mean estimate and (for importance sampling) log-evidence
estimate can be checked directly against ground truth. §4.1-4.2 fit RWM
and Metropolis-within-Gibbs and visualize both together to show they
converge to the identical target shape despite proposing very
differently; §4.3 fits importance sampling and visualizes which
proposal draws end up mattering most.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.6) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule, coordinate-wise
Metropolis-within-Gibbs, and the self-normalized importance-sampling
estimator, each tied to the specific library call that implements it.
§3 describes the program chunk by chunk. §4 reports the results of all
three methods alongside three figures. §5 discusses what the RWM/Gibbs
overlay and the weight-tiered scatter each reveal. §6 concludes.

* Background: Random-Walk Metropolis, Metropolis-within-Gibbs, and Importance Sampling

** The shared target: an unnormalized log-density

All three methods in this report take an =optim::ArbitraryFunction=
(the base evaluate-only interface every optimizer and sampler in this
project shares) rather than the =optim::DifferentiableFunction= HMC and
NUTS require -- no gradient method, just a single =evaluate()= returning
the log-density (up to an additive constant) at a point. This report's
target is a deliberately *unnormalized* correlated bivariate Gaussian --
its =evaluate()= omits the $-\tfrac{1}{2}\log((2\pi)^d|\Sigma|)$
normalizing term entirely -- specifically so that §4.3's
importance-sampling log-evidence estimate has a known, independently-computable
target to check against, exactly the same "validate against a
closed-form answer" device the Bayesian modeling companion report's conjugate
model used for MAP/HMC/NUTS.

** Random-walk Metropolis

=bayes::RandomWalkMetropolis= is the classical Metropolis-Hastings
algorithm: propose $x' = x + \epsilon\,z$ for $z\sim N(0, I)$, and accept
the proposal with probability

$$\alpha = \min\!\left(1,\ \exp\bigl(\ell(x') - \ell(x)\bigr)\right)$$

where $\ell$ is the log-density. Because the proposal is symmetric, the
Hastings correction term cancels and the acceptance probability depends
only on the log-density difference. Like HMC/NUTS, =RandomWalkMetropolis=
adapts its step size $\epsilon$ during =num_warmup= iterations via the
same dual-averaging scheme -- but targets a *different* theoretical
acceptance rate, 0.234, the asymptotically optimal rate for a joint,
multivariate random-walk proposal in high dimensions (Roberts, Gelman &
Gilks 1997), as opposed to HMC/NUTS's 0.8 (trajectory-based proposals)
or Metropolis-within-Gibbs's own 0.44 below. RWM needs no gradient at
all, at the cost of the classic random-walk inefficiency: because the
proposal ignores the target's local geometry entirely, it can take
many more iterations than HMC/NUTS to explore a given posterior to the
same precision.

** Metropolis-within-Gibbs

=bayes::GibbsSampler= updates one coordinate $x_i$ at a time via a
scalar random-walk Metropolis step, holding every other coordinate
fixed, evaluated against the *same joint log-density* -- mathematically
equivalent to a Metropolis step targeting the exact full conditional
$p(x_i \mid x_{-i})$, since the joint-density ratio with everything else
held fixed *is* the full-conditional-density ratio by definition. This
is "Metropolis-within-Gibbs" in the sense BUGS/JAGS use the term: the
standard fallback whenever a full conditional isn't conjugate or
directly sampleable in closed form, not classical Gibbs sampling via
model-specific conditional samplers (which a generic engine driven only
by a joint log-density evaluation cannot provide, since it would need
to know each conditional's actual distributional family). Each
coordinate adapts its own step size independently during warmup,
targeting 0.44 -- the asymptotically optimal acceptance rate for a
*scalar* random-walk proposal (Roberts & Rosenthal 2001), distinct from
RWM's joint-proposal optimum of 0.234. Updating one coordinate at a time
rather than proposing the whole vector jointly can mix substantially
better when parameters live on very different scales or are only weakly
correlated -- though, as §4.2 shows, on a target that is only
*moderately* correlated (this report's bivariate Gaussian, correlation
0.5) the two methods produce visually indistinguishable results.

** Self-normalized importance sampling

=bayes::ImportanceSampling= takes an entirely different approach: rather
than constructing a Markov chain that eventually visits the target's
high-density regions, it draws i.i.d. samples directly from a tractable
*proposal* distribution $q$ (here a multivariate Gaussian
$N(\text{proposal\_mean}, \text{proposal\_covariance})$) and reweights
them to correct for $q$ differing from the target $p$. For each draw
$x_i \sim q$, the importance weight is $w_i \propto p(x_i)/q(x_i)$;
self-normalizing (dividing by $\sum_j w_j$) gives weights that sum to 1
and require only the target's *unnormalized* density, exactly this
report's =UnnormalizedGaussian::evaluate()=. The weighted sample then
estimates any posterior expectation as $\hat{E}[f(x)] = \sum_i
\hat{w}_i\,f(x_i)$ -- this report's =weighted_mean_of()= helper applied
to each coordinate. Because it produces an i.i.d.-weighted sample in one
shot, importance sampling needs no warmup and has no chain-mixing
diagnostics to check at all -- but its accuracy depends entirely on how
well $q$ covers $p$'s mass, which =effective_sample_size= (defined as
$1/\sum_i \hat{w}_i^2$, the "effective" number of independent draws the
weighted sample is worth) is the standard diagnostic for: low relative
to the number of proposal draws signals a proposal that is too narrow,
poorly located, or lighter-tailed than the target. A useful byproduct
of the same weights is =log_evidence=, the standard log-mean-exp
importance-sampling estimator of the target's log-normalizing constant
$\log\!\int p(x)\,dx$ -- directly comparable to, e.g., a Laplace
approximation's marginal likelihood, and this report's only method that
estimates that normalizing constant at all (RWM and
Metropolis-within-Gibbs, like HMC/NUTS, never need it).

** What we will report

§4.1 fits RWM and reports its posterior mean and acceptance rate against
the known truth, with a scatter figure showing the target's correlated
elliptical shape directly. §4.2 fits Metropolis-within-Gibbs the same
way and overlays both methods' samples on one figure. §4.3 fits
importance sampling, reports its posterior mean, effective sample size,
and log-evidence estimate against the known truth, and visualizes which
proposal draws carry the most weight.

* Data and Methods

** The data

This report uses a single synthetic target: a correlated bivariate
Gaussian log-density with mean $(2, -1)$ and covariance matrix
$\Sigma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0.5\\0.5 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
deliberately unnormalized so its known true log-normalizing constant
provides a ground-truth check for §4.3's importance-sampling estimate -- the same
"construct a target with a known answer" device used throughout this
project's Bayesian reports rather than real, truth-unknown data.

** Software

All computation uses =datamunge='s C++ =bayes= module --
=bayes::RandomWalkMetropolis=, =bayes::GibbsSampler=, and
=bayes::ImportanceSampling= -- together with =optim::ArbitraryFunction=
(the plain evaluate-only interface all three take, requiring no
gradient) and =plot::RPlot= for visualization.

#+name: bsamp-includes
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
#include <datamunge/bayes/bayes.hpp>
#include <datamunge/optim/function_types.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>

#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>

using datamunge::bayes::GibbsOptions;
using datamunge::bayes::GibbsSampler;
using datamunge::bayes::ImportanceSampling;
using datamunge::bayes::ImportanceSamplingOptions;
using datamunge::bayes::RandomWalkMetropolis;
using datamunge::bayes::RWMOptions;
using datamunge::optim::ArbitraryFunction;
using datamunge::plot::RPlot;

namespace {

class UnnormalizedGaussian : public ArbitraryFunction {
  public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double dx = x[0] - 2.0;
        const double dy = x[1] + 1.0;
        const double quad = (dx * dx - dx * dy + dy * dy) / 0.75;
        return -0.5 * quad;
    }
};

double mean_of(const std::vector<std::vector<double>>& samples,
               std::size_t dim) {
    double s = 0.0;
    for (const auto& row : samples) s += row[dim];
    return s / static_cast<double>(samples.size());
}

double weighted_mean_of(const std::vector<std::vector<double>>& samples,
                         const std::vector<double>& weights, std::size_t dim) {
    double s = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < samples.size(); ++i) {
        s += weights[i] * samples[i][dim];
    }
    return s;
}

std::vector<double> column(const std::vector<std::vector<double>>& samples,
                            std::size_t dim) {
    std::vector<double> out;
    out.reserve(samples.size());
    for (const auto& row : samples) out.push_back(row[dim]);
    return out;
}

} // namespace
#+end_src

This mirrors the reference example
(=examples/cpp/source/bayes_sampling_ex.cpp=), extended below with three
=RPlot= visualizations. The =Sigma^-1= comment in the reference example
records the algebra behind =UnnormalizedGaussian::evaluate()=: for
$\Sigma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0.5\\0.5 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
$\Sigma^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{0.75}
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -0.5\\-0.5 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
giving the quadratic form computed directly in code.

* Results

** Random-walk Metropolis

#+begin_example
Target: correlated bivariate Gaussian, true mean (2, -1), true log-normalizer ~1.6941

=== Random-Walk Metropolis ===
posterior mean estimate: (1.9704, -0.9578) (true: (2, -1))
acceptance rate: 0.2332 (target 0.234)
#+end_example

RWM's posterior mean estimate, $(1.9704, -0.9578)$, sits within about
0.04 of the true $(2, -1)$ on each coordinate -- well within the sampling
noise expected from 4000 post-warmup draws of a distribution with unit
marginal variance. The realized acceptance rate, $0.2332$, matches its
0.234 theoretical target (§2.2) to three decimal places, confirming the
dual-averaging step-size adaptation found essentially the
textbook-optimal step size for this two-dimensional target.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Scatter plot of Random-Walk Metropolis samples with the true mean marked :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/bsamp_rwm_scatter.svg]]

The scatter plot makes the target's structure directly visible: an
elliptical cloud tilted along the diagonal, exactly the shape a
positively-correlated ($\rho=0.5$) bivariate Gaussian produces, centered
almost exactly on the true mean (the red marker).

** Metropolis-within-Gibbs

#+begin_example
=== Metropolis-within-Gibbs ===
posterior mean estimate: (2.0274, -0.9646) (true: (2, -1))
per-coordinate acceptance rates: (0.4346, 0.4410) (target 0.44)
#+end_example

Metropolis-within-Gibbs's posterior mean, $(2.0274, -0.9646)$, is
similarly close to the truth, and its two per-coordinate acceptance
rates ($0.4346$ and $0.4410$) both land close to their own 0.44
theoretical target (§2.3) -- a different optimum from RWM's 0.234, since
each coordinate here is updated via its own scalar (not joint)
random-walk proposal.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Scatter plot overlaying Random-Walk Metropolis and Metropolis-within-Gibbs samples :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/bsamp_rwm_vs_gibbs.svg]]

Overlaying both methods' samples on one figure shows them converging to
the same elliptical shape and the same center, despite proposing in
entirely different ways -- RWM moves the whole two-dimensional vector at
once, while Metropolis-within-Gibbs updates one coordinate at a time
holding the other fixed. On this target's moderate correlation
($\rho=0.5$), neither approach shows a visible mixing advantage over the
other; §5 discusses when that would change.

** Importance sampling

#+begin_example
=== Importance Sampling ===
posterior mean estimate: (2.0127, -0.9650) (true: (2, -1))
effective sample size: 3708.1542 / 5000
estimated log-normalizer: 1.6856 (true: ~1.6941)
#+end_example

Importance sampling's posterior mean, $(2.0127, -0.9650)$, is again
close to the truth, obtained with no Markov chain, no warmup, and no
mixing diagnostics at all -- just 5000 i.i.d. draws from a wider,
uncorrelated $N((2,-1), 1.5I)$ proposal, reweighted. The effective
sample size, $3708$ out of $5000$ raw draws ($74.2\%$), signals a
reasonably well-matched proposal: not every draw is wasted, though the
proposal's lack of correlation (unlike the target's $\rho=0.5$) does
cost some efficiency relative to a perfectly-matched proposal, where the
effective sample size would approach the full 5000. The estimated
log-normalizing constant, $1.6856$, differs from the true value
($\approx1.6941$) by only $0.0085$ -- a genuine, non-trivial estimation
task (unlike RWM/Gibbs, which never need or estimate this quantity at
all) recovered to within less than 1% relative error from a single batch
of weighted draws.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Scatter plot of importance-sampling proposal draws colored by weight tier :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/bsamp_importance_weights.svg]]

This figure reveals something the summary numbers alone do not: the top
5% of importance weights (red) are not scattered randomly across the
high-density region -- they concentrate almost exactly along the
target's correlated major axis, forming a visibly narrow diagonal band
running through the wider gray cloud of lower-weight draws. This is a
direct visualization of what an importance weight $w_i \propto
p(x_i)/q(x_i)$ actually measures: because the proposal $q$ is circular
(uncorrelated) while the target $p$ is elliptical (correlated), a
proposal draw that happens to land along the target's true correlated
ridge is *far* more probable under $p$ than under $q$ would predict from
its distance from the mean alone, earning it a disproportionately large
weight. Draws off that ridge -- plausible under the circular proposal but
comparatively unlikely under the true correlated target -- are
correspondingly downweighted.

* Discussion

**Both MCMC methods matched their own distinct theoretical
acceptance-rate optimum, not a single shared number.** RWM's realized rate
($0.2332$) and Metropolis-within-Gibbs's two realized rates ($0.4346$,
$0.4410$) each closely tracked a *different* target (0.234 vs. 0.44)
because each is the asymptotically optimal rate for a structurally
different proposal -- a joint multivariate step versus a sequence of
scalar steps. This is worth remembering when reading acceptance-rate
diagnostics across different samplers in general: there is no single
universal "good" acceptance rate, and the [[file:bayesian_modeling_analysis.org][Bayesian
modeling companion report]]'s own HMC/NUTS target a third value (0.8)
again for a structurally different reason (a trajectory-based
proposal, not a random walk at all).

**On this report's moderately-correlated target, RWM and
Metropolis-within-Gibbs showed no visible efficiency difference -- but that is a
property of this specific target, not a general result.** §2.3 notes
that coordinate-wise updating can mix substantially better than a joint
proposal when parameters are only weakly correlated or live on very
different scales; this report's target ($\rho=0.5$, both coordinates
unit-scaled) is specifically the kind of moderately-correlated,
similar-scale case where the two approaches are expected to perform comparably,
which §4.2's overlay confirms directly rather than merely asserting.
Extending this comparison to a target with $\rho$ close to $\pm1$, or
with wildly different per-coordinate scales, would be the natural next
experiment to make the coordinate-wise method's real advantage (or, for
extreme correlation, its real disadvantage relative to a joint proposal
that can move diagonally in one step) directly visible.

**A diagnostic number and a diagnostic figure can tell genuinely
different stories from the same data.** The effective-sample-size number
alone ($3708/5000$) correctly signals "a reasonably good but imperfect
proposal," but only the weight-tiered scatter plot reveals *why* it is
imperfect: not a location problem (the proposal is centered exactly at
the true mean) but a *shape* problem (the proposal is circular while the
target is elliptical), visible directly as the top-weight draws'
concentration along the correlated ridge. A caller relying only on the
=effective_sample_size= number would know something was slightly off but
not what to fix; the figure makes the fix -- give the proposal the
target's correlation structure, not just its mean -- immediately obvious.

* Conclusion

Fitting a correlated bivariate Gaussian target with known truth using
=datamunge='s C++ =bayes::RandomWalkMetropolis=, =bayes::GibbsSampler=,
and =bayes::ImportanceSampling= recovers the true posterior mean to
within sampling noise by all three methods, with each MCMC method's
realized acceptance rate matching its own distinct theoretical optimum
and importance sampling's log-evidence estimate landing within 1%
relative error of the true log-normalizing constant. Visualizing RWM
against Metropolis-within-Gibbs directly confirms they converge to the
identical target shape despite fundamentally different proposal
mechanisms, and visualizing the importance-sampling draws by weight tier
surfaces a genuine structural insight -- that the highest-weight draws
concentrate along the target's correlated axis -- that the summary
statistics alone do not reveal. As with its companions, this entire
report is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example bayesian_sampling_analysis=, which re-executes
§3.3.6's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above --
from the =datamunge= source tree.
