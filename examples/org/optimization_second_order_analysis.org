#+begin_abstract
We fit two second-order optimizers -- =Newton= and
=TrustRegionNewton= -- against objectives that supply an exact,
analytic Hessian, and report it as a literate program: every fragment
below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++ binary
that produced every number and figure in this document. On the
companion report's three-dimensional quadratic bowl, both methods
reach the exact minimum $(4,-2,1)$ almost immediately -- =Newton= in 2
iterations, =TrustRegionNewton= in 4 -- confirming that a single
Newton step on an exactly-quadratic objective with an exact Hessian
/is/ the exact minimizer. On the harder Rosenbrock function, started
from the companion report's exact point $(-1.2, 1.0)$, the three-way
comparison is stark: =LBFGS= needs 61 evaluations to reach $f=0$
(self-verified here, matching the companion report exactly);
=Newton= needs only 7 iterations to reach $f=0.000000$ at
$(1.000000, 1.000000)$, its quadratic convergence winning decisively
despite Rosenbrock's Hessian being indefinite over parts of the
search space; but =TrustRegionNewton= exhausts its full 200-iteration
budget and stalls at $f=0.074290$, $x=(0.728, 0.528)$, never reaching
the minimum -- a direct, measured consequence of this implementation's
Cauchy-point-only subproblem solve, which can only rescale the
steepest-descent step's /length/, never bend its /direction/, along
Rosenbrock's curved valley. We also demonstrate the implementation's
real singular-Hessian failure modes: starting =Newton= exactly on the
curve where Rosenbrock's Hessian is analytically singular
($x{=}0,\,y{=}0.005$) does /not/ throw the documented
=std::invalid_argument= -- the fixed $10^{-10}$ diagonal damping is
just large enough to keep every pivot above the $10^{-15}$ throw
threshold, so instead =Newton= takes a catastrophic step and diverges
to $x\approx(2.0\times10^{10},\,4.0\times10^{20})$ silently; a second,
deliberately engineered objective with a Hessian entry of exactly
$-10^{-10}$ (canceling the damping to bit-exact zero) does trigger the
real exception. We derive Newton's method from the second-order
Taylor expansion, confirm its sign convention algebraically against
the actual code, and derive the Cauchy-point step and trust-region
accept/reject rule this implementation actually uses, contrasting it
explicitly with the fuller dogleg/exact-subproblem methods it
simplifies away from.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

Every method in the companion report (=optimization_analysis.org=)
either ignores curvature entirely (=GradientDescent=, =Adam=) or
approximates it indirectly from a history of gradients
(=LBFGS='s limited-memory quasi-Newton update). This report covers
the two optimizers in =datamunge::optim= that instead consume an
/exact/, analytically-supplied Hessian: =Newton= and
=TrustRegionNewton=, both built around the =HessianFunction=
interface (=function_types.hpp=), which adds a single method,
=hessian(coordinates)=, on top of =DifferentiableFunction='s
evaluate+gradient pair. Exact second-order information is the most a
smooth objective can offer an optimizer -- it is what makes Newton's
method converge quadratically near a well-behaved minimum, far faster
than any first-order method -- but it is also what makes Newton's
method fail sharply, rather than gracefully, when the Hessian is not
positive definite where it is evaluated, exactly the situation
Rosenbrock's curved valley creates over parts of its domain. This
report's central experiment restarts the companion report's LBFGS
run on Rosenbrock from the identical point $(-1.2, 1.0)$ with both
second-order methods, for a direct, three-way, same-start comparison,
and deliberately provokes both a real silent-divergence failure and a
real thrown-exception failure to show exactly where the line falls
between them.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose
and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling
the program" step weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp= file,
compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from the Results
section onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives Newton's method and the Cauchy-point trust-region step,
each tied to the exact formula in the =.cpp= source. §3 describes the
program chunk by chunk, including the two different
evaluate()/gradient()-hooking decorators this report needs (Newton's
control flow defeats the companion report's original logging trick,
a real, reportable quirk in its own right). §4 reports results from
the bowl, the three-way Rosenbrock comparison and its convergence
figure, and the two singular-Hessian demonstrations. §5 discusses
what those results show. §6 concludes.

* Background: Second-Order Optimization Methods

** The function-type hierarchy: =HessianFunction=

=datamunge::optim='s =HessianFunction= interface is
=DifferentiableFunction= (evaluate + exact gradient) plus one further
method:

#+begin_src c++ :eval no
class HessianFunction : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    virtual std::vector<std::vector<double>>
    hessian(const std::vector<double>& coordinates) = 0;
};
#+end_src

Both optimizers in this report require it. This report builds two
custom =HessianFunction= subclasses: a re-implementation of the
companion report's =QuadraticBowl= (Hessian exactly $2I$ everywhere,
the trivial sanity check) and a new =RosenbrockHessianFn= supplying
the Rosenbrock function's exact analytic Hessian, derived below.

** Newton's method

Expanding $f$ to second order around the current point $x_k$,

$$f(x_k+\delta) \approx f(x_k) + g^\top\delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^\top H \delta,$$

where $g=\nabla f(x_k)$ and $H$ is the exact Hessian at $x_k$, and
minimizing the right-hand side over $\delta$ (setting its gradient
with respect to $\delta$ to zero, $g + H\delta = 0$) gives the Newton
system $H\delta = -g$, i.e. the classic Newton step
$\delta = -H^{-1}g$, $x_{k+1} = x_k + \delta = x_k - H^{-1}g$.

=Newton::optimize= (=newton.hpp= / =.cpp=) computes this every
iteration slightly differently on the page but identically in effect:
each step, if $\lVert g\rVert \le$ =tolerance= it stops; otherwise it
forms $H=$ =hessian(x)=, adds a small fixed =damping= to every
diagonal entry ($H_{ii} \mathrel{+}= $ =damping=, =1e-10= by default
-- purely a numerical-conditioning constant applied identically on
every iteration, /not/ an adaptive trust-region-style damping that
grows or shrinks, worth stating plainly since the name invites that
comparison), solves $H\delta=g$ (note: =g=, not =-g=) for $\delta$ via
hand-rolled Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting, and sets
=x -= delta=. Algebraically this is exactly the standard step: solving
$H\delta=g$ gives $\delta=H^{-1}g$, and =x -= delta= is
$x_{k+1}=x_k-H^{-1}g$ -- identical to $x_k + (-H^{-1}g)$ above, just
solved for the positive gradient and subtracted rather than solved
for the negated gradient and added. The two formulations agree
exactly; this is a real implementation choice to note, not a sign
error.

Because it forms and inverts (via elimination) the exact Hessian
every iteration, Newton's method converges quadratically once close
to a well-behaved (positive-definite-Hessian) minimum -- each
iteration roughly squares the number of accurate digits, the source
of its 7-iteration Rosenbrock result in §4.3, dramatically faster than
any first-order method's linear convergence rate. The cost is
fragility: if $H$ is not positive definite where it is evaluated (the
classic failure mode on a saddle or an indefinite region, exactly
what Rosenbrock's curved valley contains), the Newton step can point
in an /ascent/ direction, overshoot arbitrarily far, or -- if a pivot
during elimination is smaller than =1e-15= even after damping --
throw =std::invalid_argument("Newton: singular Hessian")= outright.
This fragility, not any deficiency specific to this implementation, is
exactly what motivates trust-region methods generally, including
=TrustRegionNewton= below.

** Trust-region Newton and the Cauchy point

A trust-region method restricts each step to a ball of radius $r$
around $x_k$ and only trusts the local quadratic model
$m(\delta)=f(x_k)+g^\top\delta+\tfrac12\delta^\top H\delta$ inside it,
rather than solving the (possibly ill-behaved) Newton system directly.
The textbook subproblem is $\min_{\lVert\delta\rVert\le r} m(\delta)$,
solved either approximately via the dogleg method (blending the
steepest-descent and Newton directions) or exactly via the
Moré-Sorensen algorithm (Nocedal & Wright, ch. 4). =datamunge='s
=TrustRegionNewton= (=trust_region_newton.hpp= / =.cpp=) solves
neither:
it uses only the *Cauchy point*, the exact minimizer of $m$ restricted
to the ray $\delta(\alpha)=-\alpha g$, $\alpha\ge0$, clipped to the
trust-region boundary.

Substituting $\delta(\alpha)=-\alpha g$ into $m$ gives a scalar
quadratic in $\alpha$,
$m(\alpha) = f(x_k) - \alpha\,g^\top g + \tfrac12\alpha^2\,g^\top H g$,
whose unconstrained minimizer (when $g^\top H g>0$, i.e. positive
curvature along $-g$) is $\alpha^\star = g^\top g / g^\top H g$ --
exactly the code's =a = gg/ghg=. If this unconstrained minimizer would
leave the trust region ($\alpha^\star\lVert g\rVert > r$), or if
$g^\top H g\le0$ (the model is flat or unbounded below along $-g$, so
no interior minimizer exists), the Cauchy point instead sits exactly
on the trust-region boundary: $\alpha=r/\lVert g\rVert$. The code's
=a = ghg>0 ? std::min(gg/ghg, r/std::sqrt(gg)) : r/std::sqrt(gg)=
implements exactly this case split. The trial point is
$y = x_k - \alpha g$ -- note this is =-alpha \cdot g=, /not/
$-\alpha H^{-1}g$: the Hessian here only ever chooses the step's
*length* along the fixed steepest-descent *direction*, never a
different direction. This is a real, significant simplification
relative to a textbook trust-region-Newton method: it is
length-adaptive gradient descent with second-order length selection,
not a genuine change of search direction, and it is exactly what
§4.3's Rosenbrock result exposes.

The accept/reject rule is likewise simpler than the textbook's
predicted-vs-actual-reduction ratio test (Nocedal & Wright's $\rho_k$
with grow/shrink thresholds at, e.g., 0.25 and 0.75): this
implementation accepts the trial step whenever it merely improves the
objective at all ($f(y)<f(x_k)$), growing the radius
($r\leftarrow\min(2r,$ =max_radius=$)$); otherwise it rejects the step
(keeps $x_k$) and halves the radius. It stops when $\lVert g\rVert\le$
=tolerance= or $r\le$ =tolerance=.

** Deriving the Rosenbrock Hessian

The companion report's Rosenbrock function is
$f(x,y)=(1-x)^2+100(y-x^2)^2$, with gradient
$\partial f/\partial x = -2(1-x)-400x(y-x^2)$,
$\partial f/\partial y = 200(y-x^2)$. Differentiating again,

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = 2 - 400\big[(y-x^2) - 2x^2\big]
  = 2 - 400y + 1200x^2, \qquad
  \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} = -400x, \qquad
  \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} = 200.$$

This was verified against a central-difference numeric Hessian
(step $10^{-4}$) at four points spanning the search space, including
the report's own start point $(-1.2, 1.0)$, before being trusted for
any number in this report; every analytic entry agreed with its
numeric counterpart to within about $10^{-5}$, the expected
finite-difference truncation error at that step size. The determinant
$\det H = 400 H_{00}/2 \cdot 1 - H_{01}^2$ simplifies to
$400 - 80000y + 80000x^2$, zero exactly along the parabola
$y=x^2+0.005$ -- close to, but not on, Rosenbrock's own valley floor
$y=x^2$ -- which §4.4 uses to construct a genuinely, analytically
singular starting Hessian.

** Building a convergence curve for iteration-driven methods

The companion report's =LoggingDifferentiable= decorator logs every
=evaluate()= call, which works because every method in that report
calls =evaluate()= repeatedly inside a line search. =Newton::optimize=
does not: reading its source shows it calls =gradient()= and
=hessian()= every iteration but =evaluate()= exactly *once*, at the
very end, to compute the value it returns. The direct
=HessianFunction= analog of =LoggingDifferentiable=
(=LoggingHessian=, forwarding =hessian()= as instructed) therefore
produces a one-point log for Newton -- confirmed directly in §4.2, a
real quirk of this implementation's control flow, not a bug in the
decorator. Since both =Newton= and =TrustRegionNewton= call
=gradient()= exactly once per iteration, this report instead uses a
second decorator, =IterationLoggingHessian=, that hooks =gradient()=
to additionally evaluate and log the objective at the start of every
iteration -- an iteration-indexed convergence curve in place of the
companion report's evaluation-indexed one, used consistently for both
methods so §4.3's figure compares like with like.

*** Includes, objectives, and the logging decorators

#+name: optim2-includes
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
#include <datamunge/optim/optim.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>

#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>
#include <vector>

using namespace datamunge::optim;
using datamunge::plot::RPlot;

namespace {

class QuadraticBowl : public HessianFunction {
public:
    explicit QuadraticBowl(std::vector<double> target)
        : target_(std::move(target)) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& coordinates) override {
        double total = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            const double d = coordinates[i] - target_[i];
            total += d * d;
        }
        return total;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(
        const std::vector<double>& coordinates) override {
        std::vector<double> grad(coordinates.size());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            grad[i] = 2.0 * (coordinates[i] - target_[i]);
        }
        return grad;
    }
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> hessian(
        const std::vector<double>& coordinates) override {
        std::size_t n = coordinates.size();
        std::vector<std::vector<double>> h(n, std::vector<double>(n, 0.0));
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) h[i][i] = 2.0;
        return h;
    }
private:
    std::vector<double> target_;
};

class RosenbrockFn : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double a = 1.0 - x[0];
        const double b = x[1] - x[0] * x[0];
        return a * a + 100.0 * b * b;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return {-2.0 * (1.0 - x[0]) - 400.0 * x[0] * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0]),
                200.0 * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0])};
    }
};

class RosenbrockHessianFn : public HessianFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double a = 1.0 - x[0];
        const double b = x[1] - x[0] * x[0];
        return a * a + 100.0 * b * b;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return {-2.0 * (1.0 - x[0]) - 400.0 * x[0] * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0]),
                200.0 * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0])};
    }
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> hessian(
        const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double h00 = 2.0 - 400.0 * x[1] + 1200.0 * x[0] * x[0];
        const double h01 = -400.0 * x[0];
        const double h11 = 200.0;
        return {{h00, h01}, {h01, h11}};
    }
};

// Engineered so that damping (+1e-10 on the diagonal) cancels H[0][0]
// to bit-exact 0.0, deliberately triggering Newton's singular-Hessian
// exception (see Section 4.4).
class EngineeredSingularFn : public HessianFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return x[0] - 5e-11 * x[0] * x[0] + x[1] + 0.5 * x[1] * x[1];
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return {1.0 - 1e-10 * x[0], 1.0 + x[1]};
    }
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> hessian(
        const std::vector<double>&) override {
        return {{-1e-10, 0.0}, {0.0, 1.0}};
    }
};

// The direct HessianFunction analog of the companion report's
// LoggingDifferentiable: logs every evaluate() call, forwards
// gradient() and hessian() untouched.
class LoggingHessian : public HessianFunction {
public:
    LoggingHessian(HessianFunction& inner, std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->gradient(x);
    }
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> hessian(
        const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->hessian(x);
    }
private:
    HessianFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

class LoggingDifferentiable : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    LoggingDifferentiable(DifferentiableFunction& inner,
                           std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->gradient(x);
    }
private:
    DifferentiableFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

// Newton only calls evaluate() once, at the very end (see Section
// 2.5), so LoggingHessian's evaluate()-hook is useless for its
// convergence curve. Both Newton and TrustRegionNewton call
// gradient() exactly once per iteration, so this decorator hooks
// gradient() instead, evaluating and logging the objective at the
// start of every iteration.
class IterationLoggingHessian : public HessianFunction {
public:
    IterationLoggingHessian(HessianFunction& inner,
                             std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->evaluate(x);
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        log_->push_back(inner_->evaluate(x));
        return inner_->gradient(x);
    }
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> hessian(
        const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->hessian(x);
    }
private:
    HessianFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

void print_vec(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << "[";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << (i ? ", " : "") << v[i];
    }
    std::cout << "]";
}

std::vector<double> indices(std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> out(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) out[i] = static_cast<double>(i + 1);
    return out;
}

} // namespace
#+end_src

* Results

** Newton and TrustRegionNewton on the quadratic bowl

#+begin_example
=== HessianFunction: Newton and TrustRegionNewton on the quadratic bowl ===
Newton:            f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000]
  (2 iterations)
TrustRegionNewton: f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000]
  (4 iterations)
#+end_example

Both methods reach the exact minimum $(4,-2,1)$. =Newton= needs only
2 iterations: the first computes and takes the exact Newton step
(which, on a function whose Hessian is exactly $2I$ everywhere, /is/
the exact minimizer, modulo the negligible =1e-10= damping term), and
the second iteration merely confirms $\lVert g\rVert\le$ =tolerance=
and stops -- exactly the "one real step" behavior predicted going in.
=TrustRegionNewton= needs a few more (4) because its first accepted
Cauchy-point step is deliberately clipped to the trust region's
initial radius ($1.0$ by default) rather than allowed to jump straight
to the minimizer the way Newton's unclipped step does; each subsequent
iteration both grows the radius and takes another step, converging
fully within a handful of iterations once the radius exceeds the
distance to the minimum.

** Newton's evaluate()-call quirk

#+begin_example
=== Newton's evaluate()-call quirk on Rosenbrock ===
LoggingHessian (evaluate()-hook) log size for Newton: 1
#+end_example

This confirms §2.5's prediction directly: wrapping =Newton='s

pattern (extended to forward =hessian()=) produces a log with exactly

** Three-way comparison on Rosenbrock

#+begin_example
=== Three-way comparison on Rosenbrock from (-1.2, 1.0) ===
LBFGS:             f=0.000000 x=[1.000000, 1.000001]
  (61 evaluations)
Newton:            f=0.000000 x=[1.000000, 1.000000]
  (7 iterations)
TrustRegionNewton: f=0.074290 x=[0.727911, 0.528249]
  (200 iterations)
#+end_example

=LBFGS='s own number reproduces the companion report's exactly (61
evaluations, $f=0.000000$ at $(1.000000, 1.000001)$), confirming this
program's Rosenbrock objective is identical and this comparison is a
genuine same-start match. =Newton= reaches $f=0.000000$ at exactly

here despite Rosenbrock's Hessian being indefinite over parts of the

region (see §4.4 for a starting point deliberately chosen so it does).
=TrustRegionNewton=, in sharp contrast, runs its entire 200-iteration
budget (the default =max_iterations=) without ever satisfying either
stopping condition, ending at $f=0.074290$, $(0.728, 0.528)$ -- close
to, but nowhere near, the true minimum.

** Singular and near-singular Hessian failure modes

#+begin_example
=== Singular / near-singular Hessian failure modes ===
At x=[0, 0.005]: H=[[0.000000,-0.000000],[-0.000000,200.000000]]
  det=0.000000 gradient=[-2.000000, 1.000000]
Newton from exact-singular point: f=399999998982648168448.000000
  x=[19999999975.566204, 399999999022648131584.000000]
Newton on engineered singular fn THREW: Newton: singular Hessian
#+end_example

At $(0, 0.005)$ -- exactly on the curve §2.4 derived where
Rosenbrock's Hessian is analytically singular -- the printed Hessian
confirms $H_{00}=0$, $H_{01}=H_{10}=0$, $\det H=0$, with a genuinely
nonzero gradient $(-2, 1)$: precisely the "nonzero gradient, singular
Hessian" scenario the implementation's exception exists to guard
against. It does /not/ fire here. Instead, =Newton= takes one
catastrophic step (the damped pivot $H_{00}+\text{damping}=10^{-10}$
survives the $10^{-15}$ throw threshold by five orders of magnitude,
but is still far too small to safely invert) and diverges to
$x\approx(2.0\times10^{10}, 4.0\times10^{20})$, silently returning an
astronomical function value rather than erroring out. The second,
deliberately engineered objective (Hessian entry exactly $-10^{-10}$,
canceling the damping to bit-exact $0.0$ by IEEE-754 arithmetic) does
trigger the real exception immediately on its first iteration,
confirming the guard code path exists and works exactly as documented
-- just not for every intuitively "singular" starting point, only for
ones where the /damped/ pivot itself underflows the threshold.

* Discussion

**A fixed, always-on diagonal damping constant changes what "singular"
means operationally, and this report caught the gap in practice, not
just in the source.** The documented exception
(=std::invalid_argument("Newton: singular Hessian")=) reads as a
general safety net for ill-conditioned or singular Hessians, and the
brief motivating this report speculated it might fire on a genuinely
singular Rosenbrock point. It does not: §4.4 shows a real, analytically
singular Hessian (determinant exactly zero, confirmed both
symbolically in §2.4 and numerically in the program's own printed
=det= field, set to =0.000000=) produces silent catastrophic
divergence instead of an error, because the =1e-10= damping constant
is exactly large enough to
keep the damped pivot above the =1e-15= throw threshold. The exception
only fires for the narrower case this report had to construct
deliberately: a Hessian entry close enough to $-\text{damping}$ that
the addition cancels to (near) bit-exact zero. A caller relying on the
exception as a general "did my Hessian go singular" signal would be
silently misled on Rosenbrock's own singular curve -- a real, useful
finding about where this implementation's safety net does and does not
catch.

**=TrustRegionNewton='s Cauchy-point-only subproblem solve is not a
minor simplification on a genuinely curved landscape -- it caps the
method's achievable progress at whatever a rescaled steepest-descent
direction alone can reach.** §4.3's comparison makes this concrete:
=Newton=, given the same starting point and an equally exact Hessian,
reaches machine-precision $f=0$ in 7 iterations, while
=TrustRegionNewton= is still at $f=0.074$ after 200 -- nearly 30x more
iterations for a dramatically worse answer. The bowl result in §4.1
shows this is not intrinsic to trust-region methods generally: on an
isotropic quadratic, $-g$ already points exactly at the minimum, so a
Cauchy-point-only trust region loses nothing. Rosenbrock's valley is
exactly the landscape where steepest descent's direction and the true
Newton direction diverge sharply, and that is exactly where this
implementation's simplification -- adapting step /length/ via the
Hessian but never step /direction/ -- shows its cost most clearly.

**Newton's evaluate()-call quirk is a small but genuine reminder that
a decorator technique verified on one class of optimizer does not
automatically generalize to another with different control flow.**
The companion report's =LoggingDifferentiable= trick works because
every method it wraps calls =evaluate()= repeatedly, inside a line
search or a per-epoch loop. =Newton='s single end-of-run =evaluate()=
call breaks that assumption outright (a one-point log, confirmed in
§4.2), not because of any bug, but because Newton's whole iteration
loop operates purely on =gradient()= and =hessian()= and never needs
the raw objective value until it is ready to report a final answer.
Building this report's actual convergence curve required hooking a
different method (=gradient()=) entirely -- a concrete illustration
that "no optimizer exposes an iteration history directly" (true of
every method in this series so far) can still require a genuinely
different decorator strategy per method, not one universal trick.

* Conclusion

Fitting the same quadratic bowl and the same Rosenbrock start point
$(-1.2, 1.0)$ used in the companion report with exact-Hessian methods
surfaces results the gradient-only comparison could not: =Newton=
needs only 7 iterations to reach Rosenbrock's exact minimum, faster in
iteration count than =LBFGS='s 61 evaluations even accounting for the
extra per-iteration Hessian cost, while =TrustRegionNewton='s
Cauchy-point-only subproblem solve leaves it stalled at $f=0.074$
after its full 200-iteration budget on the identical problem. The two
deliberately constructed singular-Hessian experiments show the
documented exception is real but narrower than its name suggests --
it catches deliberately engineered cancellation, not every
analytically singular Hessian a caller might construct, a genuine,
checkable limitation worth knowing before relying on it as a general
safety net. As with its companions, this entire report is reproducible
end to end via
=just org-example optimization_second_order_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.3.6's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
