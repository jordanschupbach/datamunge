#+begin_abstract
We fit an ordinary-least-squares linear model predicting body mass from bill
and flipper measurements in the Palmer Archipelago penguins data, using
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::LM= class, and report it as a literate program:
every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++
binary that produced every number and figure in this document. Bill length,
bill depth, flipper length, and species together explain 84.71% of the
variance in body mass ($R^2 = 0.8471$, adjusted $0.8449$) across 342
penguins (2 of 344 dropped for missing measurements); every predictor is
significant at $p < 0.001$, and holding bill and flipper measurements fixed,
Gentoo penguins are predicted to weigh 934.9g more than Adelie and Chinstrap
penguins 513.2g less. We derive the least-squares estimator, its sampling
distribution, the confidence/prediction-interval distinction, and the four
regression diagnostics from first principles, connect each to the
corresponding library call, and use the fitted model's own diagnostic plots
to check whether its assumptions actually hold on this data.
#+end_abstract

* Background: Ordinary Least Squares

** Setup and notation

We observe $n$ penguins $(x_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, where
$y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is body mass in grams and $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is a
row of predictors -- here bill length, bill depth, flipper length, and a
species indicator, plus an intercept. Stacking rows gives the design matrix
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ and response vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
The linear model assumes

\[
y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}
\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) ,
\]

and the task is to estimate the unknown coefficient vector $\beta$ and
noise variance $\sigma^2$ from the data.

** Least-squares estimation

Ordinary least squares picks the $\hat\beta$ that minimizes the residual
sum of squares $\mathrm{RSS}(\beta) = \lVert y - X\beta \rVert^2$.
Differentiating and setting the gradient to zero gives the /normal
equations/ $X^\top X \beta = X^\top y$, so whenever $X$ has full column
rank,

\[
\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y .
\]

Geometrically, the fitted values $\hat y = X\hat\beta = Hy$ are the
orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $X$, where
$H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ is the /hat matrix/ ($H_{ii}$ is exactly the
/leverage/ of §2.6); the residuals $e = y - \hat y = (I - H)y$ are
orthogonal to that column space by construction. This is precisely
=LM::coefficients()=, =LM::fitted_values()=, and =LM::residuals()=.

** Sampling distribution and inference

Under the Gaussian noise assumption, $\hat\beta$ is itself Gaussian:
$\hat\beta \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta, \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1})$ --
exactly =LM::covariance()=. $\sigma^2$ is estimated unbiasedly by
$\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{RSS} / (n - p)$ (=LM::sigma()= squared, with
$n - p$ = =LM::degrees_of_freedom()=), and each coefficient's standard
error is the square root of the corresponding diagonal entry of
$\hat\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}$ (=LM::standard_errors()=). Dividing each
coefficient by its standard error gives a $t$-statistic
(=LM::t_values()=), tested against a $t_{n-p}$ distribution to get the
per-coefficient $p$-values in =LM::p_values()= -- the classic "is this
predictor's effect distinguishable from zero" test reported in §4.2.

** Goodness of fit and the ANOVA decomposition

Decomposing total variation, $\mathrm{TSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$
splits into explained and unexplained parts, $\mathrm{TSS} =
\mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}$, giving $R^2 = \mathrm{ESS}/\mathrm{TSS} = 1 -
\mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}$ (=LM::r_squared()=) and an adjusted version that
penalizes additional predictors,
$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p}$ (=LM::adjusted_r_squared()=). The
overall $F$-test compares a model with only an intercept against the full
model: $F = \frac{\mathrm{ESS}/(p-1)}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)} \sim F_{p-1,\,n-p}$
under the null that every slope is zero (=LM::f_statistic()= and
=LM::f_p_value()=). =LM::anova()= goes further and decomposes $\mathrm{ESS}$
/sequentially/, term by term, in the order each appears in the formula --
each row's sum of squares is that term's marginal contribution /given every
term already added before it/, which is why, unlike the per-coefficient
$t$-tests above, reordering the formula's right-hand side changes every row
of the ANOVA table even though it changes nothing else about the fit.

** Categorical predictors

=species= is a three-level factor (Adelie, Chinstrap, Gentoo), handled by
the same =Formula= machinery the companion reports use for LDA/SVM
categorical outcomes: one level (alphabetically first, Adelie) becomes the
reference and is absorbed into the intercept, and the model gets one dummy
coefficient per remaining level. =speciesChinstrap= and =speciesGentoo= in
§4.2's table are therefore each read as "difference in mean body mass from
an Adelie penguin with identical bill and flipper measurements," not as a
species' mass in isolation.

** Confidence intervals versus prediction intervals

For a new predictor row $x_0$, two different questions have two different
answers. "What is the /average/ body mass of penguins with these
measurements?" asks about the mean response $x_0^\top\beta$, estimated by
$x_0^\top\hat\beta$ with variance $\sigma^2 x_0^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_0$ --
a /confidence interval/. "What will /this one new penguin/ weigh?" asks
about a single new draw $y_0 = x_0^\top\beta + \varepsilon_0$, which adds
the new observation's own irreducible noise: $\mathrm{Var}(y_0 -
x_0^\top\hat\beta) = \sigma^2\big(1 + x_0^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_0\big)$ --
a /prediction interval/, always wider than the confidence interval for the
same $x_0$. This is exactly the =PredictionInterval::Confidence= versus
=PredictionInterval::Prediction= choice in =LM::predict()=; §4.7 uses the
latter, since "how much will this penguin weigh" is a prediction question,
not a mean-response question.

** Regression diagnostics

Four quantities, each built from the hat matrix and residuals above, check
whether OLS's assumptions actually hold: /leverage/ $h_{ii} = H_{ii}$
(=LM::leverage()=) measures how unusual a point's /predictors/ are, since a
high-leverage point can pull the fit toward itself regardless of its
response value; /standardized residuals/
$e_i / (\hat\sigma\sqrt{1-h_{ii}})$ (=LM::standardized_residuals()=)
rescale residuals to unit variance so they're comparable across points;
/studentized residuals/ (=LM::studentized_residuals()=) go further and
refit $\hat\sigma$ with point $i$ excluded, giving a more sensitive outlier
flag; and /Cook's distance/ (=LM::cooks_distance()=) combines leverage and
residual size into a single measure of how much removing point $i$ would
change the fitted coefficients. Plotted against each other, these four
quantities give the standard four-panel regression checkup (matching R's
=plot.lm=, and =LM::plot_residuals_vs_fitted()= /
=plot_normal_qq()= / =plot_scale_location()= /
=plot_residuals_vs_leverage()= here): residuals-vs-fitted for
linearity/homoscedasticity, normal Q-Q for normality of the errors,
scale-location for homoscedasticity again (easier to spot a trend in), and
residuals-vs-leverage, annotated by Cook's distance, for influential points.
§4.5 reads all four together.

* Data and Methods

** The data

=datamunge::datasets::penguins()= returns the Palmer Archipelago penguins
dataset (344 rows, matching the well-known =palmerpenguins::penguins= R
package): =species= (Adelie, Chinstrap, Gentoo), =island=,
=bill_length_mm=, =bill_depth_mm=, =flipper_length_mm=, =body_mass_g=,
=sex= (a handful missing), and =year=. A few rows have missing
measurements; =LM= drops any row with a null value in a column the formula
uses before fitting (matching R's =na.omit= default), which §4.2 reports
explicitly rather than silently.

** Software

Once again, the entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, the formula-driven =stats::LM= for the
model and every diagnostic in §2, and =plot::RPlot=/=plot::RLayout= for
every figure -- including the four diagnostic panels, which =LM= itself
knows how to draw. No external plotting or statistics package is used.

*** Coefficient plot

A forest plot of every coefficient's estimate and 95% confidence interval

#+name: lm-coefficients
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    {
        const auto& names = model.coefficient_names();
        const auto& est = model.coefficients();
        const auto ci = model.confidence_intervals();
        auto coefplot = RPlot::create();
        coefplot.x_limits(-7200.0, 2000.0);
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < names.size(); ++i) {

* Discussion

**Model fit and what the diagnostics add.** An $R^2$ of 0.8471 says the
model explains most, but not all, of the variation in body mass -- the
remaining 15% is individual variation §4.1's four measurements simply
don't capture (age, sex, precise health, and so on are not in this
formula). The diagnostic panels in §4.5 are what turn that single $R^2$
number into a defensible claim rather than a hopeful one: they check the
/assumptions/ the $t$-tests, $F$-test, and confidence/prediction intervals
throughout this report all silently rely on (linearity, constant variance,
approximately normal errors, no single dominating observation), and on
this data those assumptions hold up reasonably well rather than merely
being asserted.

**Sequential ANOVA vs the per-coefficient table.** §4.2's $t$-tests and
§4.3's ANOVA table can look like they're answering the same question twice,
but they're not: a $t$-test asks whether a term matters /given every other
term already in the model/ (order-independent), while a sequential ANOVA
row asks whether a term matters /given only the terms before it/
(order-dependent). They happen to agree here -- every term is significant
either way -- but on a dataset with more collinear predictors they can
disagree sharply, which is exactly why =datamunge= exposes both rather than
only one.

**Comparison to the classification reports.** Where the LDA and SVM
companion reports each produce a single class label (with, respectively, a
posterior probability and a coarse vote count expressing confidence), this
report's =predict()= call returns a continuous number /with an explicit
interval attached/ -- and §2.6 is explicit about which of two different
intervals that number should carry, a distinction that has no real
classification analogue. That's the qualitative difference between
predicting a category and predicting a quantity: the uncertainty is native
to the number itself, not a separate posterior computed alongside it.

**Limitations.** =species= being significant even after all three physical
measurements are already in the model (§4.3) suggests body mass depends on
something about species beyond what bill/flipper geometry alone captures --
plausibly sex, which this formula omits entirely despite being available in
the raw dataset, and which is well known in the palmerpenguins literature
to interact with species-level mass differences. A model that included
=sex= (and possibly a =species:sex= interaction) would very likely improve
on 84.71% $R^2$; this report deliberately keeps the formula to four terms
to keep the diagnostics and every plot legible, not because a richer model
wouldn't fit better.

* Conclusion

An ordinary-least-squares model, fit with =datamunge='s C++ =stats::LM=,
predicts penguin body mass from bill length, bill depth, flipper length,
and species with $R^2 = 0.8471$ across 342 penguins, every predictor
significant at $p < 0.001$. The four regression diagnostics -- derived in
§2.7 and read together in §4.5 -- support rather than merely assume OLS's
usual conditions on this data, and the confidence/prediction-interval
distinction derived in §2.6 is carried all the way through to §4.7's three
concrete predictions. As with its companions, this entire report is
reproducible end to end via =just org-example lm_penguins_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.3.8's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
