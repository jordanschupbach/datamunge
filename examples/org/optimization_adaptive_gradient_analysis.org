#+begin_abstract
We fit five per-coordinate adaptive-learning-rate optimizers --
=AdaGrad=, =RMSProp=, =AdaDelta=, =AMSGrad=, and =Nadam= -- from
=datamunge='s C++ =optim= module against two objectives, and report it
as a literate program: every number and figure below comes from a
single compiled, executed C++ binary. On the same isotropic quadratic
bowl the companion report =optimization_analysis.org= fits with
=GradientDescent= (59 evaluations), =Adam= (7874 evaluations), and
=LBFGS= (3 evaluations), the five new methods split sharply on their
*default* options: =RMSProp= (4085 evaluations), =AdaDelta= (1267
evaluations), and =Nadam= (9739 evaluations) all reach the exact
minimum, while =AdaGrad= and =AMSGrad= both run out their full
10000-iteration budget without converging ($f=5.106793$ and
$f=0.016618$ respectively) -- a real, direct demonstration of
=AdaGrad='s textbook weakness (its accumulator only ever grows, so its
effective step size eventually decays toward zero) and a genuine
surprise for =AMSGrad=, whose non-decreasing second-moment estimate
makes it converge even more slowly than plain =Adam= on this easy,
noise-free problem. On a second, deliberately ill-conditioned
anisotropic bowl ($f(x,y,z)=100(x-4)^2+(y+2)^2+0.01(z-1)^2$, a
10000:1 curvature ratio between its stiffest and softest coordinates),
plain =GradientDescent= at the largest stable-looking step size
($\eta=0.01$) does not diverge to infinity but oscillates in a stable
period-2 cycle on the stiff coordinate ($\eta$ sitting exactly at the
stability boundary $2/L$), and even at a safe step size ($\eta=0.005$)
needs 20001 evaluations and still has not fully converged on the soft
coordinate ($z=0.864678$, target $1$). All five adaptive methods
handle both coordinates gracefully in one run with their own default
or lightly-tuned step sizes, converging in 134 to 1276 evaluations --
roughly 16x to 150x fewer than stable =GradientDescent= needed, and
without diverging at any step size we tried. We derive all five
methods' per-coordinate update rules from the exact formula in each
=.cpp= file, in the order each was designed to fix the previous
method's specific weakness.

* Introduction

The companion report =optimization_analysis.org= shows that
=GradientDescent='s single, global step size $\eta$ is a real
liability: too large and it diverges, too small and convergence is
needlessly slow, and the "right" $\eta$ is a property of the
objective's *worst-conditioned* coordinate, not a good general choice
for every coordinate at once. All five methods in this report target
=DifferentiableFunction= (evaluations plus an exact gradient, the same
interface =GradientDescent=, =Adam=, and =LBFGS= use) but answer this
problem differently from all three: instead of one global $\eta$, each
maintains a running *per-coordinate* statistic of past squared
gradients and divides the step by its square root, so a coordinate
with a history of large gradients automatically gets a smaller
effective step, and a coordinate with a history of small gradients
gets a larger one. This report's job is to make the real differences
between five specific accumulation-and-correction strategies for that
one shared idea concrete with real numbers, not just list five
formulas that look superficially similar.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose
and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling
the program" step (§3.3.4) weaves every named chunk below into one
=.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4
onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives every method's update rule, each tied to the specific
formula in its =.cpp= file, in the order each was designed to fix the
previous method's specific weakness. §3 describes the program chunk by
chunk, reusing the =LoggingDifferentiable= decorator technique from the
companion report to build this report's convergence-curve figure. §4
reports both experiments' results alongside one figure. §5 discusses
what =AdaGrad='s and =AMSGrad='s non-convergence on an "easy" problem
and =GradientDescent='s period-2 oscillation on the ill-conditioned
bowl both reveal. §6 concludes.

* Background: Optimization Methods

** The function-type hierarchy

Every method in this report targets =datamunge::optim='s
=DifferentiableFunction= interface -- =evaluate(coordinates)= plus an
exact gradient =gradient(coordinates)=, the same interface
=GradientDescent=, =Adam=, and =LBFGS= use in the companion report.
None of the five needs anything beyond this: no Hessian, no
separability into per-example terms, no proximal operator. What
distinguishes them from each other is entirely how each turns a stream
of past gradients into a per-coordinate step-size correction.

** AdaGrad: the naive accumulator, and its real weakness

=AdaGrad= (Duchi, Hazan & Singer 2011) accumulates the *sum* of every
past squared gradient, per coordinate,

$$G_j \leftarrow G_j + g_j^2, \qquad x_j \leftarrow x_j - \eta\,
\frac{g_j}{\sqrt{G_j+\epsilon}}$$

where $g=\nabla f(x)$ and $\epsilon$ (=AdaGradOptions::epsilon=,
default $10^{-8}$) keeps the division finite before any gradient has
been observed. This is exactly =adagrad.cpp='s update: accumulate
=accumulated_squares[j]= by adding =gradient[j] * gradient[j]=, then
update =coordinates[j]= by subtracting
=step_size * gradient[j] / sqrt(accumulated_squares[j] + epsilon)=.
Because $G_j$ is a running *sum* that never decays, it
only ever grows, which means the effective step size
$\eta/\sqrt{G_j+\epsilon}$ only ever shrinks, monotonically, for the
rest of the run -- even long after the gradient in that coordinate has
become small and the optimizer would benefit from taking larger steps
again. This is a real, citable weakness of the original 2011 method,
and §4.1 measures it directly: with =AdaGradOptions='s default
=step_size= of =0.01=, =AdaGrad= does not reach this report's simple
bowl's minimum within its default 10000-iteration budget.

** RMSProp: replacing the sum with a decaying average

=RMSProp= (Tieleman & Hinton, unpublished, Coursera lecture 6.5, 2012)
fixes exactly this problem by replacing =AdaGrad='s ever-growing sum
with an exponential moving average controlled by
=RMSPropOptions::decay_rate= (default $0.9$):

$$v_j \leftarrow \rho v_j + (1-\rho) g_j^2, \qquad x_j \leftarrow x_j -
\eta\,\frac{g_j}{\sqrt{v_j}+\epsilon}$$

matching =rmsprop.cpp= exactly (note =epsilon= is added *outside* the
square root here, =sqrt(mean_squares[j]) + epsilon=, unlike =AdaGrad='s
=epsilon= inside it -- a small but real implementation difference
between the two). Because $v_j$ is now a weighted average of only the
*recent* squared gradients rather than the full history, it can rise
again if the gradient in that coordinate grows again later, so the
effective step size no longer decays to zero unconditionally -- this
is RMSProp's whole point, and the reason it was proposed as a direct
fix to =AdaGrad='s weakness.

** AdaDelta: removing the global step size entirely

=AdaDelta= (Zeiler 2012) goes one step further and removes the global
step-size hyperparameter $\eta$ altogether -- there is no =step_size=
field at all in =AdaDeltaOptions=, only =decay_rate= (default $0.95$)
and =epsilon= (default $10^{-6}$). In place of a fixed $\eta$, it
tracks a *second* running average, of the squared *updates*
themselves, and uses the ratio of RMS-past-updates to
RMS-past-gradients as an implicit, units-matched step size:

$$v_j \leftarrow \rho v_j + (1-\rho) g_j^2, \qquad \Delta x_j = -
\frac{\sqrt{u_j+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{v_j+\epsilon}}\, g_j, \qquad x_j
\leftarrow x_j + \Delta x_j, \qquad u_j \leftarrow \rho u_j + (1-\rho)
\Delta x_j^2$$

matching =adadelta.cpp='s =gradient_squares=, =update=, and
=update_squares= sequence exactly (note the update is computed and
applied to =coordinates[j]= *before* =update_squares[j]= is refreshed
with it, so each step uses the *previous* step's update statistics,
not its own). The ratio $\sqrt{u_j}/\sqrt{v_j}$ carries the same units
as $x_j$ regardless of the units $g_j$ happens to be in, which is what
lets =AdaDelta= supply an implicit step size without any
hand-tuned global $\eta$ at all.

** AMSGrad: fixing a real, published Adam counterexample

=AMSGrad= (Reddi, Kale & Kumar 2018) returns to Adam's combination of a
first-moment (momentum) estimate and a second-moment (variance)
estimate,

$$m_j \leftarrow \beta_1 m_j + (1-\beta_1) g_j, \qquad v_j \leftarrow
\beta_2 v_j + (1-\beta_2) g_j^2$$

but inserts one extra step before using $v_j$: a running elementwise
maximum, $\hat{v}^{\max}_j \leftarrow \max(\hat{v}^{\max}_j, v_j)$,
matching =amsgrad.cpp='s =max_second_moment[j] =
std::max(max_second_moment[j], second_moment[j])= exactly. After bias
correction ($\hat{m}_j=m_j/(1-\beta_1^t)$,
$\hat{v}_j=\hat{v}^{\max}_j/(1-\beta_2^t)$), the update is
$x_j \leftarrow x_j - \eta\,\hat{m}_j/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_j}+\epsilon)$.
Reddi et al. (2018) proved that plain Adam's un-maxed $v_j$ admits a
real, published counterexample where Adam provably fails to converge,
because $v_j$ can shrink from one step to the next, letting the
effective step size *increase* right when it should not; forcing
$\hat{v}^{\max}_j$ to be non-decreasing restores a convergence
guarantee Adam lacks in general, at the direct cost of an effective
step size that can now only ever shrink over a run, never grow back --
a cost §4.1 measures concretely against plain Adam's own numbers from
the companion report.

** Nadam: Nesterov look-ahead grafted onto Adam

=Nadam= (Dozat 2016) keeps Adam's exact second-moment update
($v_j \leftarrow \beta_2 v_j + (1-\beta_2)g_j^2$, bias-corrected the
usual way) but replaces the bias-corrected first moment $\hat{m}_j$
used in the update with a Nesterov-style blend of the bias-corrected
running average *and* the current bias-corrected gradient:

$$m_j \leftarrow \beta_1 m_j + (1-\beta_1) g_j, \qquad \tilde{m}_j =
\beta_1\,\frac{m_j}{1-\beta_1^t} + (1-\beta_1)\,\frac{g_j}{1-\beta_1^t},
\qquad x_j \leftarrow x_j - \eta\,\frac{\tilde{m}_j}{\sqrt{v_j/(1-
\beta_2^t)}+\epsilon}$$

which is the literal formula in =nadam.cpp=:
=nesterov_first = beta1 * first[j] / first_correction + (1.0 - beta1) *
gradient[j] / first_correction=. Unlike plain Adam, which uses only
$\hat{m}_j$ (a lagging average of gradients seen *before* the current
step), Nadam's $\tilde{m}_j$ incorporates the *current* step's raw
gradient directly into the direction used for that same step -- the
same "look ahead using an estimate of where momentum is about to carry
you" idea behind Nesterov's classical accelerated gradient method,
grafted onto Adam's adaptive per-coordinate step size.

** Building a convergence curve without a native history API

As in the companion report, none of these five optimizer classes
expose an iteration callback, a returned trace, or a verbose/logging
option -- every =optimize()= call returns only the final objective
value after mutating the caller's coordinates in place. This report
reuses the exact same =LoggingDifferentiable= decorator from
=optimization_analysis.org=: it wraps a real objective, forwards every
call, and records each =evaluate()= call's return value into an
external vector, giving a per-evaluation convergence trace for any
optimizer in this module without modifying the library itself.

** What we will report

§4.1 fits the isotropic bowl from the companion report (target
$(4,-2,1)$, start $(0,0,0)$) with all five methods at their library
defaults, directly comparable to that report's own =GradientDescent=
(59 evaluations), =Adam= (7874 evaluations), and =LBFGS= (3
evaluations) numbers. §4.2 fits a deliberately ill-conditioned
anisotropic bowl (curvature ratio 10000:1 between coordinates) with
plain =GradientDescent= at two step sizes and all five adaptive
methods, and visualizes all six methods' logged convergence curves
together on one log-log plot.

* Data and Methods

** The data

Both experiments use purely synthetic objectives with known analytic
minima, so every optimizer's result can be checked directly against
ground truth. §4.1 reuses the exact isotropic 3D quadratic bowl
($f(x)=\sum_i(x_i-t_i)^2$, target $(4,-2,1)$) the companion report
uses for its own =GradientDescent= / =Adam= / =LBFGS= comparison, so this
report's five new numbers sit on the same footing as numbers a reader
may already know from that report. §4.2 constructs a second,
axis-aligned but *anisotropic* bowl,
$f(x,y,z)=100(x-4)^2+(y+2)^2+0.01(z-1)^2$ -- no cross terms, so
curvature differs *only* by coordinate, isolating exactly the failure
mode (a single global step size cannot simultaneously suit a stiff and
a soft coordinate) these five per-coordinate methods are specifically
designed to fix.

** Software

All computation uses =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module --
=GradientDescent= (for the ill-conditioned-bowl baseline), =AdaGrad=,
=RMSProp=, =AdaDelta=, =AMSGrad=, and =Nadam= -- together with
=optim::DifferentiableFunction=, the interface every custom objective
in this report implements, and =plot::RPlot= for visualization.

namespace {

class QuadraticBowl : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    explicit QuadraticBowl(std::vector<double> target)
        : target_(std::move(target)) {}

    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& coordinates) override {
        double total = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            const double d = coordinates[i] - target_[i];
            total += d * d;
        }
        return total;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& coordinates)
        override {
        std::vector<double> grad(coordinates.size());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            grad[i] = 2.0 * (coordinates[i] - target_[i]);
        }
        return grad;
    }

private:
    std::vector<double> target_;
};

// Anisotropic bowl: f(x,y,z) = 100*(x-4)^2 + (y+2)^2 + 0.01*(z-1)^2
// curvature ratio 10000:1 between stiffest (x) and softest (z) coordinate.
class IllConditionedBowl : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double dx = x[0] - 4.0;
        const double dy = x[1] + 2.0;
        const double dz = x[2] - 1.0;
        return 100.0 * dx * dx + dy * dy + 0.01 * dz * dz;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return {200.0 * (x[0] - 4.0), 2.0 * (x[1] + 2.0),
                0.02 * (x[2] - 1.0)};
    }
};

class LoggingDifferentiable : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    LoggingDifferentiable(DifferentiableFunction& inner,
                           std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->gradient(x);
    }

private:
    DifferentiableFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

void print_vec(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << "[";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << (i ? ", " : "") << v[i];
    }
    std::cout << "]";
}

std::vector<double> log10_shifted(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::vector<double> out(v.size());
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        out[i] = std::log10(v[i] + 1e-12);
    }
    return out;
}
std::vector<double> log10_index(std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> out(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        out[i] = std::log10(static_cast<double>(i + 1));
    }
    return out;
}

} // namespace

At =step_size= set to =0.01=, =GradientDescent= does not blow up to infinity --
it settles into a stable period-2 oscillation on the stiff $x$
coordinate, ending back at exactly its starting value $x=0$ after an
even number of steps, because $\eta=0.01$ sits exactly at that
coordinate's stability boundary $2/L_x=2/200=0.01$ (the update becomes
$x \leftarrow -x+8$, whose fixed point is unstable and whose orbit is a
period-2 cycle, not a divergence). At the safe =step_size= set to

stiff and mid coordinates exactly, but after 20000 iterations
(20001 evaluations) still has not converged on the soft $z$ coordinate

small to move the soft coordinate quickly. All five adaptive methods,
run with lightly hand-tuned step sizes (=AdaGrad='s benefits from a
noticeably larger =step_size= (set to =0.5=) to compensate for how
fast its denominator grows; the other three all work well with
=step_size= set to =0.05=; =AdaDelta= needs no step size at all),
reach the exact minimum on
*both* coordinates in one run, in 134 to 1276 evaluations -- between
about 16x (=AdaDelta= vs. stable =GradientDescent='s 20001) and 150x
(=RMSProp=) fewer evaluations than stable =GradientDescent= needed.

* Discussion

**=AdaGrad='s and =AMSGrad='s non-convergence in §4.1 is not a harness
bug -- it is each method's core design tradeoff showing up exactly
where theory predicts it should.** =AdaGrad='s accumulator
=accumulated_squares[j]= is a running *sum* that never decays by
construction; once it has grown large early in a run (as it does here,
starting far from the target at the origin), the effective step size
$\eta/\sqrt{G_j+\epsilon}$ can only keep shrinking, so a fixed
iteration budget that would be plenty for a decaying-average method
can simply run out before $G_j$'s growth-induced slowdown is overcome.
=AMSGrad='s failure is a subtler version of the identical mechanism:
its whole design point (Reddi et al.'s fix to Adam's non-convergence
counterexample) is that $\hat{v}^{\max}_j$ may never *decrease*, which
is exactly what makes it provably safe -- but that same guarantee means
an early large gradient (again, starting far from the target) can lock
in a large denominator for the rest of the run even after the true
local gradient magnitude has shrunk, making =AMSGrad= at its default
=step_size= (=0.001=) actually *slower* to converge than plain =Adam=
at the identical default step size on this easy, noise-free problem (see
the companion report's own $f=0.000001$ after 7874 evaluations, versus
this report's $f=0.016618$ after the identical 10001-evaluation-ish
budget here). This is a genuine, if narrow, illustration of a point the
AMSGrad literature itself makes: the safety guarantee against Adam's
non-convergence counterexample is not free, and on a landscape where
that counterexample was never going to occur in the first place
(a single, static, noise-free convex bowl), paying for the guarantee
buys nothing and costs real convergence speed.

**=GradientDescent='s period-2 oscillation in §4.2, rather than an
outright divergence to infinity, is a real and instructive edge case
of the stability boundary $\eta=2/L$.** Because the stiff coordinate's
curvature is exactly $L_x=200$ and the chosen step size was exactly
$\eta=0.01=2/L_x$, the per-coordinate update collapses to the exactly
linear, exactly periodic map $x\leftarrow -x+8$ -- a knife-edge case
that a slightly larger step size would push into true exponential
divergence, and a slightly smaller one would make convergent (as
=step_size= set to =0.005= demonstrates). This distinction matters in practice:
a period-2 oscillation and a genuine divergence look similar from a
single final-iterate check (=x= "did not reach the target"), but the
convergence curve's shape -- and in this case, exact arithmetic -- tells
them apart immediately, one more example (alongside the companion
report's Rosenbrock overshoot) of a convergence trace carrying real
diagnostic information a bare final answer does not.

**No single method in this report dominates on both experiments,
which is itself the report's central finding.** =AdaDelta= is the most
evaluation-efficient method on the easy isotropic bowl (1267
evaluations, beating even =Adam='s 7874) and needs no step-size tuning
at all, but is the *slowest to start* falling on the harder
ill-conditioned bowl in the convergence figure -- its implicit,
ratio-based step size needs a longer warm-up before it becomes
effective. =RMSProp= is the fastest of all six methods on the
ill-conditioned bowl (134 evaluations) but needs 4085 on the easy
bowl -- more than =AdaDelta= despite the easy bowl nominally being the
simpler problem, a reminder that "easy" and "ill-conditioned" measure
genuinely different properties of an objective, and a method tuned for
one is not automatically well suited to the other. Every result in
this report used either library defaults or one lightly hand-picked
step size per method (documented directly in each program chunk above,
not tuned by a search) -- a more careful per-method tuning pass could
plausibly close some of these gaps, but the fact that meaningfully
different behavior shows up even at reasonable, un-tuned settings is
itself informative about how sensitive each accumulation strategy is
to its own hyperparameters.

* Conclusion

Fitting five per-coordinate adaptive-learning-rate optimizers against
two objectives surfaces real, measurable behavior a formula listing
alone would not: on an easy, noise-free isotropic bowl, three of five
methods (=RMSProp=, =AdaDelta=, =Nadam=) converge exactly at their
library defaults while two (=AdaGrad=, =AMSGrad=) run out their full
iteration budget without converging, a direct, reproducible
demonstration of each method's specific, citable design tradeoff; on a
10000:1-curvature-ratio ill-conditioned bowl, plain =GradientDescent=
either oscillates in a stable period-2 cycle at the stability boundary
or converges 16x-150x slower than every adaptive method tried, while
every adaptive method handles both coordinates gracefully in one run.
As with its companions, this entire report is reproducible end to end
via =just org-example optimization_adaptive_gradient_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.3.4's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
