#+begin_abstract
We fit Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net regression -- three penalized variants
of ordinary least squares connected by a single mixing parameter $\alpha$ --
to a continuous prediction of iris petal length from the other three
measurements plus one interaction term, using =datamunge='s C++
=stats::ElasticNet= (and its =Ridge= / =Lasso= convenience subclasses), and
report it as a literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled,
executed chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and
figure in this document. Ridge ($\alpha=0$) shrinks all four coefficients
smoothly toward zero without eliminating any of them (R^2 = 0.9625); Lasso
($\alpha=1$) drives the deliberately near-collinear
=Sepal.Length:Petal.Width= interaction term to /exactly/ zero
(R^2 = 0.9678); Elastic Net at $\alpha=0.5$ keeps all four predictors but
shrinks the same interaction term to a small nonzero value (0.038,
R^2 = 0.9672), the textbook "grouping effect" of blending L1 and L2
penalties on correlated predictors. A five-point sweep over $\alpha$ shows
non-zero coefficient count step down from four to three between
$\alpha=0.5$ and $\alpha=0.75$, exactly where the interaction term's
credited signal becomes too small for the L1 component to keep. Refitting
the identical formula by unregularized OLS reaches a slightly /higher/
in-sample R^2 (0.9688) than every regularized fit -- an expected, not
paradoxical, result we use to make precise exactly what regularization does
and does not buy a practitioner on a small, already well-behaved dataset.
We derive the elastic net objective, its closed-form Ridge special case, the
coordinate-descent algorithm and its soft-thresholding update, and the
automatic cross-validated $\lambda$ selection from first principles, tying
each to the corresponding library call, and along the way report two small
numerical-robustness fixes this report's own verification process motivated
in =ElasticNet::plot_cv_curve()=.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

Every other report in this project's growing series fits a /classifier/ to
Fisher's (1936) iris species labels, or, in the case of
[[file:lm_penguins_analysis.org][=lm_penguins_analysis.org=]], an
unregularized /linear regression/ to a continuous penguin measurement. This
report is the direct regularized-regression counterpart to that second
report: same continuous-response OLS machinery underneath, but now with an
explicit penalty on the coefficients themselves. We deliberately reuse the
iris flowers rather than the penguins, predicting the continuous
=Petal.Length= from the other three measurements plus one interaction term,
=Sepal.Length:Petal.Width= -- chosen specifically because it is
substantially correlated with the =Sepal.Length= main effect already in the
model (Pearson correlation 0.826 in this data), the exact condition under
which Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net are known to behave differently from
each other and from OLS.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.10) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives the elastic net objective as a generalization of OLS, its Ridge
and Lasso special cases, the coordinate-descent algorithm that fits it, and
the automatic cross-validated $\lambda$ selection, each tied to the specific
library call that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by chunk,

§6 concludes.

* Background: Penalized Least Squares

** Setup and notation

As in the [[file:lm_penguins_analysis.org][linear regression companion
report]]: $n = 150$ flowers, a continuous response
$y_i = \text{Petal.Length}_i$, and here $p = 4$ predictor columns
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$ -- =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Width=, and
the product term =Sepal.Length:Petal.Width= -- built by the same formula
engine (=stats::Formula=) every other report in this project uses.

** Ordinary least squares and why it can misbehave

OLS chooses $\hat\beta$ to minimize the residual sum of squares
$\|y - X\beta\|_2^2$, with the closed-form solution
$\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$ the
[[file:lm_penguins_analysis.org][linear regression companion report]]
derives and =stats::LM= implements directly. That closed form is exact and
unbiased, but it is also unstable whenever columns of $X$ are strongly
correlated: $X^\top X$ becomes nearly singular, so its inverse -- and
therefore $\hat\beta$ itself -- becomes highly sensitive to small changes in
the data. This dataset's =Sepal.Length:Petal.Width= interaction term is
built from =Sepal.Length= itself and is consequently correlated with it by
construction, making it a realistic, deliberately chosen stress case for
this instability; §4.7 shows the effect directly on this exact formula.

** The elastic net objective

Elastic net (Zou & Hastie, 2005) replaces OLS's unconstrained minimization
with a /penalized/ one,

\[
\hat\beta(\lambda,\alpha) = \arg\min_\beta \; \frac{1}{2n}
\|y - X\beta\|_2^2 \; + \; \lambda \Big( \alpha \|\beta\|_1 +
\frac{1-\alpha}{2} \|\beta\|_2^2 \Big) ,
\]

matching =ElasticNetOptions='s =alpha= and =lambda= fields directly: $\lambda
\geq 0$ (=lambda=) controls the overall penalty strength, and
$\alpha \in [0,1]$ (=alpha=) sets the mixture between an L1 penalty
$\|\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j|$ and an L2 penalty
$\|\beta\|_2^2 = \sum_j \beta_j^2$. Two special cases matter enough to have
their own library classes: $\alpha = 0$ is pure L2 (=Ridge=) and $\alpha = 1$
is pure L1 (=Lasso=). The intercept, by convention and by
=ElasticNet='s implementation, is never penalized.

** Ridge's closed form and why it never zeros a coefficient

At $\alpha = 0$ the penalty term is differentiable everywhere, so the whole
objective has a closed-form minimizer, the direct penalized analogue of
OLS's normal equations:

\[
\hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + n\lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y .
\]

Adding $n\lambda I$ to $X^\top X$ before inverting is precisely what fixes
OLS's instability under correlated columns: the matrix being inverted is now
guaranteed well-conditioned for any $\lambda > 0$, regardless of how close
to singular $X^\top X$ itself is. But because the penalty is smooth (its
gradient is $\lambda\beta$, finite everywhere including at $\beta_j = 0$),
gradient-based shrinkage can move a coefficient arbitrarily close to zero
without an optimality condition ever forcing it to land there /exactly/
-- §4.4's ridge coefficient path shows all four predictors shrinking
smoothly but staying visibly nonzero across the whole $\lambda$ path.

** Lasso, soft-thresholding, and sparsity

At $\alpha = 1$ the penalty $\|\beta\|_1$ is not differentiable at
$\beta_j = 0$, and it is exactly that kink that gives Lasso its
qualitatively different behavior. For a single coordinate $\beta_j$, holding
every other coefficient fixed, the penalized objective's minimizer has the
closed form

\[
\hat\beta_j = S(z_j, \lambda) = \mathrm{sign}(z_j)\max(|z_j| - \lambda, 0) ,
\]

where $z_j$ is the ordinary (unpenalized) least-squares coefficient update
for coordinate $j$ given the current residual and $S(\cdot,\lambda)$ is the
/soft-thresholding operator/. Unlike Ridge's smooth shrinkage,
soft-thresholding can and does map $z_j$ to /exactly/ zero whenever
$|z_j| \leq \lambda$ -- Lasso performs variable selection as a direct
consequence of this operator, not as a separate post-processing step.
§4.5's Lasso coefficient path shows exactly this: the interaction term's
coefficient is driven to precisely 0.000000 (not merely small) at the
selected $\lambda$.

** Coordinate descent

Because the general elastic net objective has no closed form for
$\alpha \in (0,1)$, =ElasticNet= fits it by /cyclic coordinate descent/
(Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2010): cycle through coordinates
$j = 1, \dots, p$ one at a time, and at each step minimize the objective
over $\beta_j$ alone, holding every other coefficient fixed, repeating until
the coefficients stop changing by more than =tol= (default $10^{-7}$) or
=max_iter= (default 10000) cycles elapse. Assuming standardized predictors
(§2.7), the per-coordinate update generalizes Lasso's soft-thresholding
operator with an extra denominator from the L2 term:

\[
\hat\beta_j \leftarrow \frac{S\big(\tfrac1n \sum_i x_{ij}
r_i^{(j)}, \; \lambda\alpha\big)}{1 + \lambda(1-\alpha)} , \qquad
r_i^{(j)} = y_i - \hat y_i + x_{ij}\hat\beta_j ,
\]

where $r_i^{(j)}$ is the /partial residual/ -- the residual with
coordinate $j$'s own current contribution added back in, so that the update
sees only what the /other/ coordinates fail to explain. At $\alpha = 1$ the
denominator collapses to 1 and this is exactly §2.4's Lasso update; at
$\alpha = 0$ the numerator's soft-threshold has no effect (its threshold is
zero) and the update reduces to Ridge's smooth shrinkage, confirming both
special cases are genuinely nested inside the same algorithm rather than
handled by separate code paths.

** Standardization

Coordinate descent's update (§2.6) assumes every predictor column has been
centered and scaled to unit variance -- otherwise a single $\lambda$
would penalize large-scale and small-scale predictors unequally. With
=ElasticNetOptions::standardize= at its default =true=, =ElasticNet=
standardizes internally before fitting and transforms the fitted
coefficients back to the original predictor scale before ever returning
them, so every coefficient reported in §4 is directly interpretable in the
original measurement units (cm, and cm^2 for the interaction term) exactly
like the [[file:lm_penguins_analysis.org][linear regression companion
report]]'s OLS coefficients.

** The automatic $\lambda$ path and cross-validated selection

Passing =lambda = -1= (the default) tells =ElasticNet= to select $\lambda$
automatically rather than fit at a single fixed value: it builds a
log-spaced path of =n_lambda= (default 100) candidate values from a
data-derived $\lambda_{\max}$ (the smallest $\lambda$ at which every coefficient
is exactly zero, for the given $\alpha$) down to a small fraction of it, and
picks the value minimizing average mean squared error under =cv_folds=
(default 5)-fold cross-validation, refitting on the full data at that
$\lambda$ for the final model. =lambda()= reports the selected value,
=lambda_path()= and =cv_mean_squared_error()= expose the full curve behind
that choice, and =non_zero_coefficients()= counts how many of the $p$
coefficients survived at it -- the three quantities §4.4-4.6's coefficient
paths and cross-validation curves visualize directly.

** What we will report

Given the three fits, we report exploratory data, each model's own summary
(coefficients, selected $\lambda$, R^2, RMSE), a side-by-side comparison
table, each model's coefficient path and cross-validation curve,
predicted-versus-actual for Ridge, a sweep over $\alpha$ itself, predictions for three
new flowers, and a direct comparison against unregularized OLS on the
identical formula.

* Data and Methods

** The data

As in every companion report: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns
Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ --
measured on =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=
(cm), with no missing values. This report predicts =Petal.Length= from the
other three measurements plus their engineered interaction term
=Sepal.Length:Petal.Width=, in contrast to every classification companion
report's use of all four measurements to predict =Species=.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, the formula-driven =stats::ElasticNet=
(and its =Ridge= / =Lasso= convenience subclasses, which simply fix
=alpha=) for every regularized model, =stats::LM= for the unregularized OLS
comparison in §4.7, and =plot::RPlot= / =plot::RLayout= for every figure. No
external plotting or statistics package is used.

** A numerical-robustness note

Producing this report's cross-validation-curve figures (§4.4-4.6) surfaced
a genuine library bug rather than an authoring mistake: a handful of
coordinate-descent CV folds can numerically diverge at the smallest
$\lambda$ values on the Lasso/Elastic Net path (never observed for Ridge, an
effect of the near-collinear interaction term this report deliberately
includes), producing squared errors that are either infinite or merely
astronomically large but still technically finite. Either corrupts a plot's
automatic axis range -- the first renders every axis tick as "-nan", the
second compresses the entire meaningful curve into a single pixel-wide
sliver against an unreadable axis. =ElasticNet::plot_cv_curve()= now filters
both classes of point before plotting, the second via a threshold of
1000 times the minimum (best-achieved, and therefore guaranteed
well-behaved) cross-validated error. This is itself a small demonstration of
the literate-programming principle this whole project follows: every figure
below, including this fix's own effect on §4.5-4.6's curves, was produced
by code the reader can inspect, not asserted.

OLS's R^2 (0.9688) is the /highest/ of all four fits, including every
regularized one -- and this is expected, not a failure of regularization:
OLS minimizes in-sample residual sum of squares exactly, by construction, so
no penalized fit on the same predictors can beat its in-sample R^2. §5
discusses why this makes in-sample R^2 the wrong yardstick for judging
regularization's value. The far more consequential difference is the
interaction term's /sign/: OLS estimates it at -0.1042, while every
regularized fit that keeps it nonzero estimates it as /positive/ (+0.0994
Ridge, +0.0381 Elastic Net). A coefficient whose sign flips depending on
whether a correlated predictor is penalized is a textbook symptom of the
instability §2.2 describes, made concrete on this report's own data rather
than left as an abstract warning.

* Discussion

**What does regularization actually buy here?** §4.7's headline number --
OLS beating every regularized fit's in-sample R^2 -- can look like an
argument against regularization, but it is exactly what §2.2's theory
predicts and says nothing against these methods' real purpose. OLS is, by
construction, the unique minimizer of in-sample residual sum of squares
among /all/ linear fits on these predictors; regularization deliberately
trades a small amount of that in-sample fit for coefficient stability and
(on data where it matters) better out-of-sample generalization, neither of
which a single in-sample R^2 comparison on one fixed dataset can measure.
This report's evidence for that trade is the interaction term's sign
flip (§4.7), not the R^2 column.

**Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net, read against their derivations.** Every
qualitative prediction §2 makes is visible in §4's real output: Ridge's
path (§4.4) never touches zero; Lasso's path (§4.4) touches it exactly,
at exactly one predictor, the one this report chose specifically because it
is correlated with another; and Elastic Net's path (§4.4) shrinks that same
predictor's magnitude nearly as hard as Lasso does while preserving Ridge's
never-exactly-zero character -- the grouping effect Zou and Hastie's (2005)
original elastic net paper introduces the method to solve, reproduced here
on a real, if small and simple, regression problem.

**Where this report's evidence is thin.** All three regularized models'
$\lambda$ was selected by cross-validation, but the R^2 and RMSE figures
reported for the /final/ model in §4.2-4.3 and §4.7 are computed by
refitting on the full dataset at that selected $\lambda$ -- an in-sample
number for the final model, same as OLS's, even though $\lambda$ itself was
chosen by an out-of-sample criterion. A genuinely out-of-sample comparison
between OLS and the three regularized fits, on a held-out test set never
touched by either the coefficient fit or the $\lambda$ selection, would be
needed to make §4.7's stability argument into a predictive-accuracy
argument as well -- this report deliberately stops short of that stronger
claim.

**Connection to the wider project.** Every method in this project so far
that reports resubstitution accuracy or in-sample R^2 -- LDA, SVM, the
decision tree, GBM, XGBoost, and now every model in this report -- shares
the same caveat: a number computed on the same data used to fit the model
is, at best, an upper bound on real predictive performance. Only the random
forest companion report's out-of-bag estimate and this report's
cross-validation curves (§4.4-4.6, even though the /final/ headline numbers
are still in-sample) actually incorporate any held-out signal at all.

* Conclusion

Fitting Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net to the same regression problem -- with
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::ElasticNet= and its convenience subclasses --
reproduces every qualitative distinction their derivations predict on real
data: Ridge's smooth, never-exactly-zero shrinkage; Lasso's exact
soft-thresholding of a deliberately correlated predictor to zero; and
Elastic Net's intermediate grouping effect, confirmed further by a
five-point $\alpha$ sweep whose non-zero coefficient count steps down
precisely where theory says it should. Comparing all three against
unregularized OLS on the identical formula shows OLS winning on in-sample
R^2, exactly as expected, while its interaction-term coefficient flips sign
relative to every regularized fit that keeps that term -- concrete evidence
for regularization's real benefit (coefficient stability under
collinearity) that a bare R^2 comparison would have missed entirely. Along
the way, this report's own verification process found and fixed two real
numerical-robustness gaps in the library's cross-validation-curve plotting,
itself an instance of the literate-programming discipline this whole
project follows: the figures above are what actually running this code
produces, including the parts that required fixing the library first. As
with its companions, this entire report is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example elastic_net_iris_analysis=, which re-executes §3.3.10's
assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above -- from the
=datamunge= source tree.
