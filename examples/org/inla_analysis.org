#+begin_abstract
We fit a Bayesian binomial mixed model to the Palmer penguins data using
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::INLAMixedModel= -- Integrated Nested Laplace
Approximation (Rue, Martino & Chopin 2009) applied to the same
=sex ~ body_mass_g + (1 | island)= model the
[[file:glmm_analysis.org][GLMM companion report]] fits by penalized
quasi-likelihood -- and separately apply three multiple-comparison
corrections to a set of ten p-values using =stats::p_adjust=, reporting
both as a literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled,
executed chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and
figure in this document. INLA returns a genuine posterior for every
quantity in the model, including the random-effect variance itself,
rather than a point estimate plus an asymptotic standard error: the
island-level random intercept has posterior mean $0.8385$ (Torgersen),
$-1.7258$ (Biscoe), and $0.8872$ (Dream) on the log-odds scale, each
with a posterior SD around $1.63$ -- large enough, with only three
groups to pool information across, that no single island's effect is
clearly separated from zero even though the three posterior means
themselves are well spread out. Separately, applying Bonferroni, Holm,
and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction to the same ten raw p-values
(three genuinely below 0.01, the rest noise) shows all three methods
correctly flag the same three tests as significant at $\alpha=0.05$
after correction, while diverging sharply on power for the borderline
fourth test ($p=0.03$ raw becomes $0.30$ under Bonferroni, $0.21$ under
Holm, but only $0.075$ under BH) -- a direct, visualized illustration of
the family-wise-error-rate versus false-discovery-rate tradeoff. We
derive INLA's nested Laplace approximation (mode-finding, hyperparameter
integration, and marginal combination), and the Bonferroni, Holm, and
BH correction procedures, tying every derivation to the exact library
call that implements it.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The [[file:glmm_analysis.org][GLMM companion report]] fit mixed models by
penalized quasi-likelihood (PQL): GLM's IRLS outer loop wrapped around
LMM's exact random-effects machinery, returning point estimates and
asymptotic standard errors for every parameter, including the
random-effect variance. INLA takes a different, genuinely Bayesian
approach to the identical model class: rather than optimizing a single
point estimate, it approximates the *full posterior distribution* over
every latent quantity -- fixed effects, random effects, and the
hyperparameters (here, the random-intercept variance) governing them --
via a sequence of nested Laplace (Gaussian) approximations, integrated
numerically over hyperparameter uncertainty. The practical payoff is a
posterior standard deviation for quantities like the random-effect
variance itself, not just for the fixed effects -- something PQL's
asymptotic-SE machinery does not provide directly.

This report's second half is unrelated in subject but shares this
project's reference example's pairing: multiple-comparison correction
via =stats::p_adjust=, matching R's =p.adjust()=. Both halves of this
report concern a shared statistical theme -- accounting honestly for
uncertainty or error that a naively-reported single number would
understate, whether that is parameter uncertainty (INLA's posteriors)
or the inflated false-positive rate from testing many hypotheses at
once (multiple-comparison correction).

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.3) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives INLA's nested Laplace approximation and the Bonferroni, Holm,
and BH correction procedures, each tied to the specific library call
that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by chunk. §4 reports
the results of both halves alongside two figures. §5 discusses what the
random effects' large posterior SDs and the correction-method comparison
each reveal. §6 concludes.

* Background: INLA and Multiple-Comparison Correction

** The latent Gaussian model INLA targets

INLA (Rue, Martino & Chopin, 2009) targets any *latent Gaussian model*:
observations $y_i$ with likelihood $p(y_i \mid \eta_i)$ depending on a
linear predictor $\eta = X x$ built from a latent field $x$ (fixed and
random effects together, exactly =datamunge='s convention of
=INLAMixedModel= building "a single latent field x = [fixed effects; one
random-effect block per group]"), where the latent field itself has a
Gaussian prior $x \mid \theta \sim N(0, Q(\theta)^{-1})$ governed by
hyperparameters $\theta$ (here, the log-scale random-intercept variance)
with their own prior $\pi(\theta)$. This is exactly the same model class
GLM, LMM, and GLMM fit elsewhere in this project's reports -- the
difference is entirely in *how* the posterior is characterized, not in
what is being modeled.

** Step 1: the Laplace approximation for a fixed $\theta$

For a fixed hyperparameter value $\theta$, INLA finds the mode
$x^*(\theta)$ of the joint log-density $\log p(y \mid x) + \log p(x \mid
\theta)$ via Newton-Raphson (=INLAOptions::newton_max_iterations= /
=newton_tolerance=), using each likelihood's score and curvature at the
current linear predictor (=INLALikelihoodPoint::score= / =curvature=,
supplied per family by =inla_binomial_logit_likelihood= /
=inla_poisson_log_likelihood= / =inla_gaussian_likelihood=). The
log-likelihood's curvature at the mode gives a Gaussian approximation to
$x \mid y, \theta$ with mean $x^*(\theta)$ and covariance
$H^*(\theta)^{-1}$ -- the "Laplace approximation" step, exact for a
genuinely Gaussian likelihood (e.g. LMM) and an approximation whenever
the likelihood is merely log-concave (Binomial, Poisson), matching every
canonical-link GLM family this project's classes support.

** Step 2: integrating over hyperparameter uncertainty

$\theta$'s own marginal posterior is approximated by evaluating the
same Laplace machinery at $\theta$'s own mode (found via the same
differential-evolution search =GLMM= / =LMM= use for their variance
components, =mode_population_size= / =mode_max_generations=) and then
combined numerically over a grid of $\theta$ values
(=INLAIntegrationStrategy::Grid=, =grid_points_per_dim= points per axis
spanning =grid_span= posterior-SD units around the mode) -- this is the
"integrated" and "nested" in Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation:
nested, because each grid point requires its own inner Laplace
approximation of the latent field; integrated, because the final
answer combines every grid point's contribution weighted by its
approximate posterior mass. Combining the per-$\theta$ Gaussian
approximations into final latent-field marginal means and SDs
(=INLAResult::latent_mean= / =latent_sd=) is what gives every parameter
in the model -- including $\theta$ itself, unlike PQL -- a real posterior
standard deviation, not just an asymptotic one computed at a single
point estimate.

** =INLAMixedModel=: the lme4-style facade

=stats::INLAMixedModel= wraps =bayes::INLA= behind the same
formula-and-grouping-factor interface =GLMM= / =LMM= use
(=y ~ x1 + x2 + (1 | group)=), building the design matrix, precision
function, and per-family likelihood automatically from the formula --
the same "parallel SWIG-facade-class" pattern documented in this
project's INLA feature-development history, letting a caller write
=INLAMixedModel model(df, "sex ~ body_mass_g + (1 | island)", options)=
exactly as they would write the corresponding =GLMM= call, with
=family= selecting Gaussian/Binomial/Poisson exactly as =GLMMFamily=
does elsewhere.

** Bonferroni and Holm: controlling the family-wise error rate

Testing $m$ hypotheses at nominal level $\alpha$ each inflates the
probability of at least one false positive well above $\alpha$. The
*Bonferroni* correction is the simplest fix: reject hypothesis $i$ only
if $p_i \le \alpha/m$, equivalently report an adjusted p-value
$\tilde{p}_i = \min(1,\, m\,p_i)$ -- exact and assumption-free, but
conservative, since it treats every test as if it alone determined the
family-wise error rate. *Holm's* step-down procedure is uniformly more
powerful while still exactly controlling the family-wise error rate
with no extra assumptions: sort p-values ascending, and reject
$p_{(1)}, \ldots, p_{(k)}$ where $k$ is the largest index such that
$p_{(j)} \le \alpha/(m-j+1)$ for all $j \le k$ -- each successive
p-value is compared against a progressively less strict threshold,
since fewer hypotheses remain "in play" once earlier ones are rejected.

** Benjamini-Hochberg: controlling the false discovery rate

Rather than bounding the probability of *any* false positive,
*Benjamini-Hochberg* (BH) bounds the *expected proportion* of false
positives among the rejected hypotheses -- the false discovery rate
(FDR). Sort p-values ascending; reject $p_{(1)}, \ldots, p_{(k)}$ where
$k$ is the largest index with $p_{(k)} \le (k/m)\,\alpha$. Because
controlling a proportion of errors among rejections is a fundamentally
weaker requirement than controlling the probability of any error at
all, BH is substantially more powerful than Bonferroni or Holm whenever
many tests are truly non-null -- the standard justification for using it
by default in large-scale settings (genomics, and generally any setting
with dozens or more simultaneous tests) where family-wise control is
needlessly conservative.

** What we will report

§4.1 fits the INLA mixed model, reports its posterior fixed and random
effects against the GLMM companion report's PQL point estimates, and
visualizes the three islands' posterior mean random intercepts. §4.2
applies Bonferroni, Holm, and BH correction to the same ten p-values and
visualizes all three alongside the raw values and a 0.05 significance
reference line.

* Data and Methods

** The data

§4.1 uses the same Palmer penguins =sex ~ body_mass_g + (1 | island)=
model as the [[file:glmm_analysis.org][GLMM companion report]] (333
complete cases across 3 islands), letting this report's Bayesian
posterior be compared directly against that report's PQL point estimate
and asymptotic SE. §4.2 uses ten synthetic p-values (three genuinely
significant at $p<0.01$, seven pure noise) chosen to make the three
correction methods' differing power visible on the same, deliberately
small and interpretable set.

** Software

All computation uses =datamunge='s C++ =stats::INLAMixedModel= (built on
=bayes::INLA=) and =stats::p_adjust=, together with =plot::RPlot= for
visualization.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** INLA mixed model, and visualizing the random effects

#+name: inla-model
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
#include <datamunge/datasets/datasets.hpp>
#include <datamunge/dstruct/dstruct.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>
#include <datamunge/stats/inla_mixed_model.hpp>
#include <datamunge/stats/p_adjust.hpp>

#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>

using datamunge::dstruct::DataFrame;
using datamunge::plot::RPlot;
using datamunge::stats::INLAMixedModel;
using datamunge::stats::INLAMixedModelFamily;
using datamunge::stats::INLAMixedModelOptions;
using datamunge::stats::p_adjust;
using datamunge::stats::PAdjustMethod;

int main() {
    std::cout << std::fixed << std::setprecision(4);

    std::cout << "=== INLAMixedModel: Bayesian mixed model via"
                 " Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation ===\n";
    const auto penguins = datamunge::datasets::penguins();
    std::vector<double> is_male, body_mass;
    std::vector<std::string> island;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < penguins.nrows(); ++i) {
        if (penguins.is_null("sex", i) || penguins.is_null("body_mass_g", i) ||
            penguins.is_null("island", i)) {
            continue;
        }
        is_male.push_back(penguins.string_at("sex", i) == "male" ? 1.0 : 0.0);
        body_mass.push_back(penguins.double_at("body_mass_g", i));
        island.push_back(penguins.string_at("island", i));
    }
    DataFrame sex_df;
    sex_df.add_column("is_male", is_male);
    sex_df.add_column("body_mass_g", body_mass);
    sex_df.add_column("island", island);

    INLAMixedModelOptions options;
    options.family = INLAMixedModelFamily::Binomial;
    INLAMixedModel model(sex_df, "is_male ~ body_mass_g + (1 | island)",
                          options);
    model.print_summary();

    std::cout << "\nlog marginal likelihood: "
              << model.log_marginal_likelihood() << "\n";
    std::cout << "random-effect (island) standard deviation: "
              << model.random_effect_std_devs()[0] << "\n\n";

    DataFrame newdata;
    newdata.add_column("body_mass_g",
                        std::vector<double>{3500.0, 4500.0, 5500.0});
    newdata.add_column("island", std::vector<std::string>{"Biscoe", "Dream",
                                                            "Torgersen"});
    const auto predictions = model.predict(newdata);
    std::cout << "P(male) predictions:\n";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < predictions.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << "  body_mass_g=" << newdata.double_at("body_mass_g", i)
                   << ", island=" << newdata.string_at("island", i) << " -> "
                   << predictions[i] << "\n";
    }
#+end_src

#+end_example

The fixed-effect posterior means (intercept $-10.2560$, =body_mass_g=
slope $0.0025$) closely match the GLMM companion report's PQL point
estimates for the same formula, as expected: both approaches target the
same likelihood, and only differ in whether the final answer is a point
estimate with an asymptotic SE (PQL) or a full posterior with a directly
computed SD (INLA). The island-level random-intercept SD, $2.5582$ on
the log-odds scale, is large -- a one-SD shift in a hypothetical island's
baseline log-odds of being male is comparable in magnitude to the entire
=body_mass_g= effect's contribution over the observed weight range, a
genuinely substantial amount of unexplained island-to-island variation
for sex ratio to show. The =P(male)= predictions must be read carefully:
each row varies *both* =body_mass_g= and =island= simultaneously (as in
the reference example), so the steep gradient from $0.0417$ to $0.9888$
reflects the =body_mass_g= fixed effect and the island random effect
acting together, not the island effect in isolation -- §5 returns to
this.

Biscoe's posterior mean random intercept ($-1.7258$) sits well below
zero while Torgersen's and Dream's ($0.8385$ and $0.8872$) sit well

but every island's posterior SD ($\approx1.63$) is comparable to or
larger than its own mean's distance from zero, meaning individually,
none of the three islands' random intercepts is clearly distinguishable
from zero at conventional posterior-interval widths. This is an honest
consequence of having only three groups: INLA (like PQL) can only pool
information across as many groups as the data provide, and three is a
small number to estimate a variance component and three individual
group effects from simultaneously.

All three methods agree on which tests are significant at
$\alpha=0.05$ after correction: the first three (raw $p=0.001, 0.004,
0.009$) stay below 0.05 under every method, and the remaining seven do
not. The methods diverge sharply, however, on the borderline fourth
test (raw $p=0.03$): Bonferroni's adjusted value ($0.30$) and Holm's
($0.21$) both place it far from significance, while BH's ($0.075$) --
though still just above 0.05 -- is more than 2.5x smaller than the
worse of the two family-wise methods, illustrating directly how much
more conservative FWER control is than FDR control once a test sits
near, rather than far below, the significance boundary.

* Discussion

**A large posterior SD relative to the mean is itself a finding, not
just a caveat to mention in passing.** §4.1's three islands' random
intercepts are individually indistinguishable from zero despite having
visibly different posterior means -- exactly the kind of nuance a PQL
point-estimate-plus-asymptotic-SE report can obscure if the SD isn't
examined carefully, and exactly what INLA's genuine posterior
(computed the same way, by the same nested-Laplace-and-integration
machinery, for every quantity including the variance component) makes
straightforward to state precisely: not "the effect varies by island"
alone, but "the effect varies by island, though not enough relative to
its own uncertainty, with only three islands' worth of data, to trust
any single island's estimate in isolation."

**Reading a prediction that varies two predictors at once requires
attributing the change to both, not silently crediting just the one
being discussed.** §4.1's =P(male)= predictions vary =body_mass_g= and
=island= together across the three rows, so the visible gradient from
$0.0417$ to $0.9888$ cannot be attributed to the island random effect
alone -- doing so would silently double-count part of the =body_mass_g=
fixed effect's own substantial contribution. This is a general
reading-comprehension hazard for any =predict()= call across
=newdata= rows that differ in more than one column at once, not
specific to INLA; the fix is always to hold every other predictor fixed
across the rows being compared if the goal is isolating one variable's
effect, which the reference example's three rows deliberately do not
do.

**Choosing Bonferroni/Holm versus BH is a substantive scientific
decision, not a computational detail.** §4.2's borderline fourth test
would be reported as "significant after correction" under BH but "not
significant" under Bonferroni or Holm -- a real difference in scientific
conclusion driven entirely by which error rate the analyst chooses to
control, not by any difference in the underlying data. Family-wise
control is the right choice when even one false discovery is costly
(e.g. a single misleading clinical claim); false-discovery-rate control
is the right choice when the goal is a good-quality ranked list
tolerating a controlled fraction of false positives (e.g. genome-wide
screening for further follow-up) -- the correction method should be
chosen for this reason before looking at the data, not selected after
the fact based on which one crosses 0.05.

* Conclusion

Fitting the same Palmer penguins mixed model as the GLMM companion
report using =datamunge='s C++ =stats::INLAMixedModel= reproduces its
fixed-effect point estimates closely while additionally providing a
genuine posterior SD for the random-effect variance itself -- revealing
that despite visibly different posterior means, none of the three
islands' random intercepts is individually distinguishable from zero
given only three groups. Applying Bonferroni, Holm, and
Benjamini-Hochberg correction to the same ten p-values via
=stats::p_adjust= shows all three methods agreeing on the three clearly
significant tests while diverging sharply on a borderline case, a
direct, visualized illustration of the family-wise-error-rate versus
false-discovery-rate tradeoff. As with its companions, this entire
report is reproducible end to end via =just org-example inla_analysis=,
which re-executes §3.3.3's assembly block -- and therefore every number
and figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
