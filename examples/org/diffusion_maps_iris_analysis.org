#+begin_abstract
We fit Diffusion Maps (Coifman & Lafon, 2006) to Fisher's (1936) iris
measurements and to a synthetic Swiss roll using =datamunge='s C++
=stats::DiffusionMaps=, and report it as a literate program: every
fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++
binary that produced every number and figure in this document. On iris,
with =heat_kernel_epsilon= chosen from the data's own real median
squared pairwise distance (5.5700), the symmetrized Markov matrix's top
eigenvalue is 1.0000, exactly as the theory of row-stochastic matrices
requires, and the two next eigenvalues (0.8132, 0.2910) drive a
2-dimensional embedding. Refitting with the density-normalization
exponent \(\alpha\) at 0 versus 1 (same epsilon) produces genuinely
different embeddings -- a maximum per-point coordinate difference of
0.7211 on the first diffusion coordinate, with the two versions still
correlating at 0.9985 -- and \(\alpha=0\) gives the cleanest species
separation of the three values tested (a between/within centroid ratio
of 12.9760, versus 10.9239 at \(\alpha=0.5\) and 9.3860 at
\(\alpha=1\)), recovering the same /setosa/-versus-overlapping-pair
structure every companion report finds. On a 400-point synthetic Swiss
roll (seed 42), the real value of a nonlinear, density-aware method
becomes concrete: with =heat_kernel_epsilon= naively chosen the same way
as for iris (the global median squared distance, 227.6), Diffusion Maps
recovers almost nothing of the roll's true arc-length parametrization
\(t\) (correlation \(-\)0.2138 at best) -- but with epsilon instead
matched to the data's /local/ neighbor scale (an 8th-nearest-neighbor
squared distance of 11.4589, motivating =heat_kernel_epsilon= set to 5),
correlation with \(t\) reaches 0.9375 in magnitude, and improves
monotonically with \(\alpha\) (0.9172, 0.9304, 0.9375 for
\(\alpha=0,0.5,1\)) -- a direct, quantitative confirmation of
Coifman and Lafon's claim that density normalization matters most under
non-uniform sampling. Plain (unscaled) PCA and classical MDS on the
identical roll data reach only 0.2426, unable to see past the coils'
straight-line shortcuts. The method is not unconditionally successful,
however: no diffusion coordinate among the first four recovers the
roll's second ground-truth coordinate, height, at better than 0.3550 in
magnitude -- an honest limitation discussed in §5. We derive the heat
kernel, the alpha-normalization step, the row-stochastic Markov matrix,
its symmetrization, and the role of diffusion time, tying each to the
corresponding library call.

* Introduction

This project's manifold-learning batch includes two methods built on
the same starting idea -- weight nearby points by
\(\exp(-d^2/\text{bandwidth})\) -- but with genuinely different recipes.
The companion [[file:laplacian_eigenmaps_iris_analysis.org][Laplacian
Eigenmaps report]] builds a /sparse/ \(k\)-nearest-neighbor graph
Laplacian and solves a generalized eigenproblem for its smallest
non-trivial eigenvectors. This report covers the other one:
=stats::DiffusionMaps= builds a /dense/, all-pairs heat kernel, adds an
\(\alpha\)-density-normalization step before ever forming a graph
Laplacian analogue, and embeds using a diffusion-time-scaled
eigendecomposition of a genuine Markov transition matrix. The
density-normalization step is the Coifman-Lafon (2006) paper's headline
contribution and has no counterpart in Laplacian Eigenmaps at all: it is
what lets Diffusion Maps recover an approximation of the manifold's
intrinsic (Laplace-Beltrami) geometry independent of how densely
different regions happened to be sampled, a distinction this report
makes concrete in §4.8-4.9.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.4.11) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward
is that program's literal output.

§2 derives the heat kernel, the \(\alpha\)-normalization step, the
row-stochastic Markov matrix, its symmetrization for eigendecomposition,
and the role of diffusion time, each tied to the specific
=DiffusionMaps= method that implements it. §3 describes the program
chunk by chunk. §4 reports the fitted iris embedding, its eigenvalues, a
direct \(\alpha=0\) versus \(\alpha=1\) comparison, species separation,
and a full epsilon-tuning and \(\alpha\)-sensitivity study on the Swiss
roll against plain PCA and MDS. §5 discusses what the epsilon-tuning
failure mode reveals and reports the height-recovery limitation
honestly. §6 concludes.

* Background: Diffusion Maps

** Setup and notation

\(n\) observations \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^p\) -- for iris,
\(n=150\), \(p=4\) (=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=,
=Petal.Width=), with =Species= withheld from fitting exactly as in every
companion report; for the Swiss roll, \(n=400\) synthetic 3D points
described in §3.2. =DiffusionMaps= exposes no =metric= or
=n_neighbors= option at all (unlike the Laplacian Eigenmaps companion,
which needs a neighbor count): distances are always Euclidean and the
kernel always dense, matching the method's standard literature
definition.

** The heat kernel and alpha-normalization

The starting point is a dense, all-pairs Gaussian heat kernel,

\[
K_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x_i - x_j \rVert_2^2}{\epsilon}\right),
\]

with \(\epsilon\) the =DiffusionMapsOptions::heat_kernel_epsilon= field:
large when \(x_i\) and \(x_j\) are close, decaying toward zero once
their squared distance substantially exceeds \(\epsilon\) -- so
\(\epsilon\) plays the same qualitative role as a neighbor count in a
\(k\)-NN method, even though =DiffusionMaps= has no explicit neighbor
parameter (§4.7-4.8 shows this matters enormously in practice). Define
each point's kernel-weighted degree, \(q_i = \sum_j K_{ij}\), a proxy
for how densely \(x_i\)'s neighborhood was sampled. The
Coifman-Lafon /alpha-normalization/ step,

\[
K'_{ij} = \frac{K_{ij}}{q_i^\alpha \, q_j^\alpha},
\]

with \(\alpha\) the =DiffusionMapsOptions::alpha= field (constrained to
\([0,1]\)), removes exactly this density dependence. Without it
(\(\alpha=0\), where \(K'=K\)), two points in a densely-sampled region of
the manifold end up with artificially high kernel-weighted connectivity
purely because many other points happen to sit nearby -- an artifact of
/sampling density/, not of the manifold's actual geometry. Dividing by
\(q_i^\alpha q_j^\alpha\) cancels this out: \(\alpha=1\) gives the
fullest correction, making the resulting operator approximate the
Laplace-Beltrami operator as though the manifold had been sampled
perfectly uniformly, while \(\alpha=0.5\) is a common practical
compromise. §4.4 and §4.8 both measure this effect directly rather than
just asserting it.

** The row-stochastic Markov matrix

Row-normalizing \(K'\) turns it into a genuine random-walk transition
matrix,

\[
P_{ij} = \frac{K'_{ij}}{d_i}, \qquad d_i = \sum_j K'_{ij},
\]

so \(P_{ij}\) is literally the probability that a random walker sitting
at \(x_i\) steps to \(x_j\) in one "diffusion" step -- the name of the
method. \(P\) is row-stochastic (\(\sum_j P_{ij} = 1\) for every \(i\)),
which by the Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees its leading eigenvalue
is exactly 1, with the all-ones vector as its corresponding
eigenvector -- a genuine, checkable theoretical property, not merely
asserted here: §4.3 confirms =eigenvalues()[0]= really does equal 1.0000
for the fitted iris model.

** Symmetrization for eigendecomposition

\(P\) itself is not symmetric in general, but a similarity transform,

\[
P_{\text{sym}} = D^{1/2} P D^{-1/2}, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,
\ldots, d_n),
\]

is: \(P_{\text{sym}}\) has exactly the same eigenvalues as \(P\) (a
standard similarity-transform fact), but being symmetric it can be
eigendecomposed with a real symmetric eigensolver rather than a general
(and less numerically stable) one. This is precisely the matrix
=eigenvalues()= reports on: the /full/ spectrum of \(P_{\text{sym}}\),
length =observations()=, descending, with =eigenvalues()[0]= the
trivial \(\approx 1\) eigenvalue that gets discarded, and
=eigenvalues()[1 .. n_components()]= the ones actually used to build the
embedding.

** Diffusion time and the embedding

The \(c\)-th diffusion coordinate scales the \(c\)-th non-trivial
eigenvector \(v_c\) of \(P_{\text{sym}}\) by its eigenvalue raised to the
=diffusion_time= field, \(t_{\text{diff}}\) (default 1.0):

\[
\text{embedding}_{:,c} = \lambda_c^{\,t_{\text{diff}}} \, v_c .
\]

The intuition ties directly back to \(P\) being a random-walk operator:
raising \(P\) to a higher power corresponds to running the random walk
for more steps, and in eigenspace this is exactly raising each
eigenvalue to that power. Since eigenvalues are already
\(\leq 1\) in magnitude, a larger \(t_{\text{diff}}\) shrinks
smaller-magnitude eigenvalues /faster/ than eigenvalues near 1 -- fine,
small-scale structure (associated with smaller eigenvalues, i.e. faster
local mixing) decays out of the embedding more quickly than coarse,
large-scale structure does, so larger =diffusion_time= increasingly
emphasizes the manifold's global shape over its local detail. This
report leaves =diffusion_time= at its default of 1.0 throughout and
instead studies \(\epsilon\) and \(\alpha\), the two parameters §4 shows
actually determine whether the method succeeds or fails on a given
dataset.

** What we will report

Given the fits, we report exploratory data (true species, for later
visual comparison), the fitted iris embedding and its eigenvalues, a
direct \(\alpha=0\) versus \(\alpha=1\) comparison, species separation
across \(\alpha\), and -- on the Swiss roll -- a full epsilon-tuning and
\(\alpha\)-sensitivity study benchmarked directly against plain PCA and
classical MDS on the identical data.

* Data and Methods

** The iris data

As in every companion report: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns
Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ --
measured on =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=,
=Petal.Width= (cm), with no missing values. All four measurements are
used as =DiffusionMaps= features throughout; =Species= is withheld from
the constructor and used only for the exploratory and embedding
figures.

** The Swiss roll: a nonlinear ground truth

Iris is close enough to linearly structured that PCA and classical MDS
already do reasonably well on it (as the
[[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and
[[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][MDS]] companion reports show), so it
cannot by itself demonstrate /why/ a nonlinear manifold method is worth
having. This report additionally generates the classic "Swiss roll": 400
points sampled uniformly at random (=std::mt19937_64=, seed 42,
matching every sibling report in this batch that uses the same
construction) via

\[
t = 1.5\pi (1 + 2u), \quad h = 21u', \quad x = t\cos t, \quad y = h,
\quad z = t\sin t,
\]

with \(u, u' \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) independently drawn per point.
\(t\), the true position along the rolled-up sheet, and \(h\)
("height"), the true position across it, are the withheld
2-dimensional ground truth: =DiffusionMaps= only ever sees the 3D
=(x,y,z)= coordinates, and \(t\)/\(h\) are used purely afterward to
compute a real correlation with the recovered embedding and to color a
comparison figure.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::DiffusionMaps= (and, for
§4.9's comparison, =stats::PCA= and =stats::MDS=) for the models, and
=plot::RPlot= for every figure. No external plotting or statistics
package is used.

static double pearson(const std::vector<double>& a,
                       const std::vector<double>& b) {
    const std::size_t n = a.size();
    double ma = 0.0, mb = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) { ma += a[i]; mb += b[i]; }
    ma /= static_cast<double>(n);
    mb /= static_cast<double>(n);
    double num = 0.0, da = 0.0, db = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        const double xa = a[i] - ma, xb = b[i] - mb;
        num += xa * xb; da += xa * xa; db += xb * xb;
    }
    return num / std::sqrt(da * db);
}

*** Choosing heat_kernel_epsilon for iris

Rather than trust the =heat_kernel_epsilon= default of 1.0, we compute
the real median squared pairwise distance among the 150 flowers and use
that to scale \(\epsilon\), following the header documentation's own
guidance that \(\epsilon\) "should be on the order of the typical
squared pairwise distance."

#+name: dmap-epsilon-iris
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Choosing heat_kernel_epsilon for iris ===\n";
    {
        std::vector<std::vector<double>> X(iris.nrows());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < iris.nrows(); ++i)
            for (const auto& f : features)
                X[i].push_back(iris.numeric_column(f).at(i));
        std::vector<double> sqd;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < X.size(); ++i)
            for (std::size_t j = i + 1; j < X.size(); ++j) {
                double s = 0.0;
                for (std::size_t k = 0; k < features.size(); ++k) {
                    const double d = X[i][k] - X[j][k];
                    s += d * d;
                }
                sqd.push_back(s);
            }
        std::sort(sqd.begin(), sqd.end());
        const double mean_sqd = std::accumulate(sqd.begin(), sqd.end(),
                                                  0.0) / sqd.size();
        std::cout << "median squared pairwise distance: "
                   << sqd[sqd.size() / 2] << ", mean: " << mean_sqd << "\n";
    }

*** Species separation vs alpha, and the best-alpha embedding

We measure separation with a simple between/within centroid-distance

\(\alpha=0, 0.5, 1\), then refit once more at whichever \(\alpha\) wins

#+name: dmap-species-sep
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Species separation (between/within ratio)"
               << " vs. alpha ===\n";
    {
        const std::vector<std::string> sp =
            {"setosa", "versicolor", "virginica"};
        std::cout << std::setw(10) << "alpha" << std::setw(22)
                   << "between/within\n";
        for (double alpha : {0.0, 0.5, 1.0}) {
            DiffusionMapsOptions o = dm_opt; o.alpha = alpha;
            DiffusionMaps dm(iris, features, o);
            std::vector<double> cx(3, 0.0), cy(3, 0.0);
            std::vector<int> cnt(3, 0);
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < dm.observations(); ++i) {
                const std::size_t orig = dm.kept_row_indices()[i];
                for (int k = 0; k < 3; ++k) {
                    if (species[orig] != sp[k]) continue;
                    cx[k] += dm.embedding()(i, 0);
                    cy[k] += dm.embedding()(i, 1);
                    ++cnt[k];
                }
            }
            for (int k = 0; k < 3; ++k) { cx[k] /= cnt[k]; cy[k] /= cnt[k]; }
            double gx = 0.0, gy = 0.0;
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < dm.observations(); ++i) {
                gx += dm.embedding()(i, 0);
                gy += dm.embedding()(i, 1);
            }
            gx /= static_cast<double>(dm.observations());
            gy /= static_cast<double>(dm.observations());
            double between = 0.0, within = 0.0;
            for (std::size_t i = 0; i < dm.observations(); ++i) {
                const std::size_t orig = dm.kept_row_indices()[i];
                for (int k = 0; k < 3; ++k) {
                    if (species[orig] != sp[k]) continue;
                    const double dx = dm.embedding()(i, 0) - cx[k];
                    const double dy = dm.embedding()(i, 1) - cy[k];
                    within += dx * dx + dy * dy;
                }
            }
            for (int k = 0; k < 3; ++k) {
                const double dx = cx[k] - gx, dy = cy[k] - gy;
                between += cnt[k] * (dx * dx + dy * dy);
            }
            std::cout << std::setw(10) << alpha << std::setw(22)
                       << (between / within) << "\n";
        }
    }

    const std::size_t n = 400;

    std::uniform_real_distribution<double> u(0.0, 1.0);

                    {"y", DataFrame::numeric_column_type(y)},
                    {"z", DataFrame::numeric_column_type(z)}};
    const std::vector<std::string> rfeatures{"x", "y", "z"};
    std::cout << "generated " << n << " swiss-roll points\n";

Using the same tuning heuristic that worked for iris -- scale \(\epsilon\)

* Discussion

**Epsilon tuning is not optional, and "typical squared pairwise
distance" means something different on a curved manifold.** §4.2's
iris tuning and §4.7's Swiss-roll tuning both start from the same
heuristic -- scale =heat_kernel_epsilon= to a real, data-derived squared
distance rather than trusting the 1.0 default -- but §4.8 shows this
heuristic silently breaks when "typical squared pairwise distance" is
computed /globally/ on a manifold whose extrinsic (3D) distances do not
match its intrinsic (along-the-surface) distances. Iris's four
measurements are close enough to uniformly spread through
\(\mathbb{R}^4\) that global and local distance scales roughly agree, so
the naive heuristic works. The Swiss roll's coiled geometry breaks this
agreement by roughly 20-fold (§4.7): most points are far apart in 3D
purely because they are on different coils, not because they are
distant along the manifold. Using the global scale lets the dense kernel
connect points across coils -- a direct analogue of the "short-circuit"
problem the [[file:isomap_iris_analysis.org][companion Isomap report]]
discusses for geodesic graphs, arising here for a different reason (a
too-large kernel bandwidth rather than a bad graph edge). The practical
lesson is concrete: for a genuinely curved manifold, =heat_kernel_epsilon=
should be tied to a /local/ (near-neighbor) distance scale, not a global
one, even though =DiffusionMaps= itself exposes no explicit
=n_neighbors= parameter to enforce this automatically.

**Alpha-normalization's effect depends on whether the data is actually
non-uniformly sampled.** §4.5 (iris) and §4.8 (Swiss roll) reach opposite
conclusions about whether increasing \(\alpha\) helps, and both are
genuine, real findings rather than a contradiction: iris's three species
are sampled at similar densities, so §2.2's density-bias correction has
little real bias to remove, and it measurably /costs/ this dataset some
of its natural density-contrast signal. The Swiss roll is a different
story: even though \(t\) itself is drawn from a /uniform/ distribution
in the code (§3.2), the roll's arc length grows with \(t\) (its radius
is literally \(t\) itself, confirmed directly against the generating
formula), so points at large \(t\) are spread across more 3D space per
unit of true \(t\) than points at small \(t\) -- a genuine non-uniform
sampling density /along the manifold/, exactly the situation Coifman and
Lafon's \(\alpha\)-normalization targets. §4.8's monotonic improvement
(0.9172 to 0.9375 as \(\alpha\) rises from 0 to 1) is a real, if modest,
confirmation of their claim on data constructed independently of that
claim.

**An honest limitation: height is not recovered.** Every fit in this
report -- iris and Swiss roll alike -- used only =n_components= set to 2
except §4.8's explicit 4-dimensional check, and that check found no
diffusion coordinate correlating with the roll's second true coordinate
(height) at better than 0.3550 in magnitude, far weaker than \(t\)'s
0.9375. The Swiss roll figure in §4.9 makes the likely reason visible:
the embedding is a single smooth arc, essentially 1-dimensional, with
both plotted diffusion coordinates varying together along the same
direction rather than one tracking \(t\) and the other tracking height
independently. This is a known characteristic of heat-kernel and
graph-Laplacian embeddings on long, thin manifolds -- the "horseshoe
effect" -- where the leading eigenfunctions are dominated by the
manifold's longest extent (here, several full turns of the spiral, far
exceeding the height range of 21 units) before any eigenfunction turns
its attention to a shorter, secondary direction. Retrieving height would
likely require substantially more diffusion coordinates, a different
kernel bandwidth better matched to the height direction specifically, or
a method built to disentangle multiple scales explicitly -- this report
reports the null result plainly rather than only showing the
\(t\)-recovery success that flatters the method.

* Conclusion

Fitting =datamunge='s C++ =stats::DiffusionMaps= to Fisher's iris
measurements -- with =Species= withheld throughout -- confirms the
symmetrized Markov matrix's leading eigenvalue is exactly 1.0000, as
Perron-Frobenius theory requires, and shows that the alpha-normalization
step genuinely changes the fitted embedding (a 0.7211 maximum
coordinate shift between \(\alpha=0\) and \(\alpha=1\)) even while both
versions recover the same /setosa/-versus-overlapping-pair structure
every companion report finds. The Swiss roll experiment is this report's
central result: with a properly locally-scaled =heat_kernel_epsilon=,
Diffusion Maps recovers the roll's true arc-length coordinate at 0.9375
correlation magnitude, nearly four times what unscaled PCA or classical
MDS achieve (0.2426) on the identical data -- but the same experiment
also shows a real limitation, since no tested configuration recovers the
roll's second (height) coordinate above 0.3550. Both the success and the
limitation trace back to the same two knobs this report studied
directly rather than assumed: =heat_kernel_epsilon= must match the
manifold's local geometry, not its global extent, and \(\alpha\)'s
correct value depends on whether the data is actually non-uniformly
sampled, which iris and the Swiss roll answer oppositely. As with its
companions, this entire report is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example diffusion_maps_iris_analysis=, which re-executes
§3.4.11's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above
-- from the =datamunge= source tree.
