#+begin_abstract
We fit seasonal ARIMA and exponential smoothing models to simulated time
series using =datamunge='s C++ =stats::ARIMA= and
=stats::ExponentialSmoothing=, and report it as a literate program: every
fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++
binary that produced every number and figure in this document. An

forecast whose standard error grows from 1.11 to 4.32 -- the textbook
widening-uncertainty signature of forecasting a non-stationary series. A
SARIMA(1,0,0)(1,1,0)$_{12}$ model fit to a seasonal series with a linear
trend correctly extrapolates the seasonal oscillation twelve steps ahead.
A Holt-Winters model fit to simulated retail-style data (upward trend,
multiplicative December spike) recovers a near-zero trend-smoothing
parameter ($\beta \approx 0.0001$, the trend barely needs updating once
set) alongside a high seasonal-smoothing parameter
($\gamma \approx 0.80$), and forecasts a proportionally large December
spike matching the simulation's own seasonal shape. A final comparison
between simple exponential smoothing and Holt's linear trend model on a
genuinely flat, trendless series produces a real cautionary finding: Holt's
trend-smoothing parameter is driven to its numerical boundary
($\beta \approx 0.9999$), fitting a spurious downward trend to what is
actually noise, while simple exponential smoothing correctly forecasts a
flat line. We derive the Box-Jenkins ARIMA and seasonal SARIMA models,
conditional-sum-of-squares estimation, forecast standard errors via
psi-weights, and the exponential smoothing family's state-space
recursions, tying each to the corresponding library call.

* Introduction

Every report in this project so far has modeled /cross-sectional/ data:
observations assumed independent (or grouped, per the
[[file:lmm_analysis.org][LMM]] and [[file:glmm_analysis.org][GLMM
companion reports]]) but never /ordered in time/ in a way the model
itself needs to respect. This report's subject, time series forecasting,
inverts that assumption entirely: a time series' defining feature is that
observation $t$ depends on observations $t-1, t-2, \ldots$, and the whole
point of fitting a model is to extrapolate that dependence forward, not
to explain one variable from others. =stats::ARIMA= and
=stats::ExponentialSmoothing= also depart from every other class in this
project structurally: neither takes a =DataFrame= or a formula string --
each takes a single raw =std::vector<double>=, the series itself, with no
predictors at all.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.6) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives the Box-Jenkins ARIMA and seasonal SARIMA models,
conditional-sum-of-squares estimation, forecast standard errors, and the exponential
smoothing family, each tied to the specific library call that implements
it. §3 describes the program chunk by chunk. §4 reports all four fitted
models and their forecasts. §5 discusses what the Holt-versus-SES
comparison reveals about model complexity and genuinely trendless data.
§6 concludes.

* Background: Box-Jenkins and Exponential Smoothing

** Setup and notation

Every model in this report is fit to a single, ordered numeric sequence
$y_1, \ldots, y_n$ -- no grouping factor, no predictor matrix, and (unlike
every regression report in this project) no train/test split via
leave-one-out or cross-validation, since a time series cannot be randomly
resampled without destroying the ordering the whole method depends on.

** The ARIMA(p, d, q) model

An ARIMA model composes three ideas into one difference equation. /AR/
(autoregressive, order $p$) expresses $y_t$ as a linear combination of its
own $p$ most recent values; /I/ (integrated, order $d$) applies the
difference operator $(1-B)^d$ (where $B$ is the backshift operator,
$By_t = y_{t-1}$) $d$ times before fitting, so the model works with a
/stationary/ series even when the raw data trends or wanders; /MA/ (moving
average, order $q$) expresses the current shock as a linear combination of
the $q$ most recent shocks. Combined,

\[
\phi(B) (1-B)^d y_t = \theta(B) \, \varepsilon_t ,
\]

with $\phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \cdots - \phi_p B^p$ (=ar_coefficients()=)
and $\theta(B) = 1 + \theta_1 B + \cdots + \theta_q B^q$
(=ma_coefficients()=), matching =ARIMAOptions='s =p=, =d=, =q= directly.
=include_mean= (default =true=) additionally fits an intercept, but --
matching R's =arima()= convention exactly, per the library's own
documentation -- only when $d = 0$ and =seasonal_d= $= 0$, since fitting a
mean on an already-differenced series would be fitting the mean of the
/changes/, not a meaningful level.

** The seasonal extension: SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)$_s$

A seasonal ARIMA model applies the identical AR/I/MA logic a second time,
at lag $s$ (the seasonal period) instead of lag 1: a seasonal difference
$(1-B^s)^D$ removes a repeating seasonal pattern the same way regular
differencing removes a trend, and seasonal AR/MA polynomials
$\Phi(B^s)$, $\Theta(B^s)$ (=seasonal_ar_coefficients()=,
=seasonal_ma_coefficients()=) capture autocorrelation at multiples of the
seasonal lag. =ARIMA= builds the /combined/ AR and MA polynomials by
directly multiplying the regular and seasonal polynomials together
(=combined_ar_=, =combined_ma_= internally) -- setting
=ARIMAOptions::seasonal_period= to 0 (the default) reduces every seasonal
polynomial to the identity, making plain ARIMA(p,d,q) exactly the special
case $P=D=Q=0$ of the general seasonal model, matching R's =arima()= and
statsmodels' =SARIMAX()='s own unification.

** Conditional sum of squares estimation

Exact ARIMA likelihood requires filtering the /entire/ series (typically
via a Kalman filter, the subject of a separate companion report); =ARIMA=
instead uses /conditional sum of squares/ (CSS), R's =arima(method =
"CSS")=: condition on the first $\max(p + Ps, q + Qs)$ observations
(=n_used()= reports how many of the =observations()= total actually
contribute to the fitted residual sum of squares) and minimize the
residual sum of squares over the remainder with
=datamunge::optim::DifferentialEvolution=. This is, per the library's own
documentation, "a standard, textbook ARIMA fitting method" that "will
differ slightly from exact-ML fits" -- an honestly stated approximation,
in the same spirit as the [[file:glmm_analysis.org][GLMM companion
report]]'s PQL caveat relative to exact (RE)ML.

** Forecasting and forecast uncertainty

=forecast()= recurses the fitted difference equation forward the
requested number of steps and re-integrates through every differencing
step applied during fitting (=regular_diff_tail_=, =seasonal_diff_tail_=
internally) to return forecasts on the original, undifferenced scale.
=forecast_with_intervals()= additionally computes each forecast step's
standard error from the /psi-weights/ -- the coefficients of the
equivalent infinite-order moving-average (MA($\infty$)) representation of
the full, non-stationary model -- so forecast uncertainty grows correctly
with horizon even though the model itself has only finitely many AR and
MA parameters. §4.1's figure shows this growth directly.

** Exponential smoothing and the Holt-Winters family

=ExponentialSmoothing= unifies simple exponential smoothing (level only),
Holt's linear trend (level and trend), and seasonal Holt-Winters (level,
trend, and a repeating seasonal index) under one class, matching R's
=HoltWinters()= and statsmodels' =ExponentialSmoothing()=. Which
components are active is controlled by =TrendType= (=None=, =Additive=,
or =AdditiveDamped=) and =SeasonalType= (=None=, =Additive=, or
=Multiplicative=); each active component has its own smoothing parameter
-- $\alpha$ for the level, $\beta$ for the trend, $\gamma$ for the
seasonal index, $\phi$ for damping -- each a weight in $(0,1)$ controlling
how quickly that component's estimate updates in response to new data
versus how much it retains its previous value. Initial states are set
once from a classical decomposition of the first one or two seasonal
cycles and then held fixed; only the smoothing parameters themselves are
fit, by minimizing one-step-ahead sum of squared errors with
=DifferentialEvolution=, the same optimizer =ARIMA= uses for its own very
differently structured parameters.

** Two traditions for the same problem

ARIMA and exponential smoothing solve the same forecasting problem from
different directions: ARIMA models the /autocorrelation structure/ of the
(differenced) series explicitly, via AR and MA polynomial coefficients
with no separate notion of "trend" or "seasonal index" as such (a linear
trend is instead implied by a unit AR root after differencing);
exponential smoothing models /level, trend, and seasonal components/
directly and updates each with its own smoothing recursion, with no
explicit autocorrelation parameters at all. §4.4's SES-versus-Holt
comparison and §5's discussion return to what this structural difference
means when a series' true trend is genuinely absent.

** What we will report

Given the fits, we report the ARIMA(1,1,1) random-walk model's
coefficients and a forecast with intervals, the seasonal SARIMA model's
coefficients and forecast, the Holt-Winters retail model's smoothing
parameters and forecast, and a direct
simple-exponential-smoothing-versus-Holt comparison on a trendless series.

* Data and Methods

** The data

Unlike every other report in this project, no shared dataset (iris,
penguins) applies here -- every series in this report is /simulated/ by
this report's own program, each with a known generating structure
(a random walk with drift and MA(1) shocks; an AR(1)-plus-seasonal-AR
process with a linear trend; a multiplicative-seasonal retail-style
series with a known December spike shape; a short, genuinely flat, noisy
series), specifically so this report's qualitative claims about each
model's behavior can be checked against a known ground truth.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=stats::ARIMA= and =stats::ExponentialSmoothing= for every model in this
report, =std::mt19937_64= and the standard library's distribution types
for the simulated series, and =plot::RPlot= for every figure. No external
plotting, statistics, or time-series package is used.

void print_vector(const std::string& label, const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << label << ": ";
    for (const double x : v) std::cout << x << " ";
    std::cout << "\n";

std::vector<double> iota_range(double start, std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> r(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) r[i] = start + static_cast<double>(i);
    return r;

*** ARIMA(1,1,1) on a random walk with drift

The true generating process is an integrated MA(1) with drift
(=level += 0.3 + 0.4*prev_shock + shock=) -- deliberately not an AR
process at all, so the fitted ARIMA(1,1,1)'s AR term has no single "true"
value to recover exactly, only a joint AR/MA approximation to the true
MA(1) structure.

#+name: arima-rw-fit
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "=== ARIMA(1,1,1) on a simulated random walk"
               << " with drift ===\n";
    std::vector<double> rw_y;
    {
        std::mt19937_64 rng(42);
        std::normal_distribution<double> noise(0.0, 1.0);
        std::vector<double> y(150);
        double level = 100.0;
        double prev_shock = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < y.size(); ++i) {
            const double shock = noise(rng);
            level += 0.3 + 0.4 * prev_shock + shock;
            y[i] = level;
            prev_shock = shock;
        }
        rw_y = y;

        ARIMAOptions options;
        options.p = 1;
        options.d = 1;
        options.q = 1;
        options.de_population_size = 80;
        options.de_max_generations = 400;
        const ARIMA model(y, options);

        std::cout << "AR coefficient: " << model.ar_coefficients()[0] << "\n";
        std::cout << "MA coefficient: " << model.ma_coefficients()[0] << "\n";
        std::cout << "sigma^2: " << model.sigma2() << ", AIC: " << model.aic()
                   << ", BIC: " << model.bic() << "\n";

        const auto [forecast, se] = model.forecast_with_intervals(6);
        print_vector("6-step forecast", forecast);
        print_vector("forecast std. errors", se);

        auto plot = RPlot::create();
        plot.line(iota_range(1, y.size()), y, "observed", {37, 99, 235}, 1.5);
        plot.line(iota_range(1, model.fitted_values().size()),
                   model.fitted_values(), "fitted", {22, 163, 74}, 1.0);
        std::vector<double> fc_x =
            iota_range(static_cast<double>(y.size()) + 1, forecast.size());
        std::vector<double> fc_lo(forecast.size()), fc_hi(forecast.size());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < forecast.size(); ++i) {
            fc_lo[i] = forecast[i] - 1.96 * se[i];
            fc_hi[i] = forecast[i] + 1.96 * se[i];
        }
        plot.line(fc_x, forecast, "forecast", {220, 38, 38}, 1.5);
        plot.line(fc_x, fc_lo, "lower 95%", {252, 165, 165}, 1.0);
        plot.line(fc_x, fc_hi, "upper 95%", {252, 165, 165}, 1.0);
        plot.title("ARIMA(1,1,1): Random Walk with Drift")
            .x_label("t").y_label("y").background({250, 250, 252});
        plot.save_svg("build/org/arima_rw_forecast.svg");
        std::cout << "wrote arima_rw_forecast.svg\n";
    }
#+end_src

*** SARIMA(1,0,0)(1,1,0)$_{12}$ on a seasonal series

The true generating process combines an AR(1) noise component with a
deterministic linear trend and a sine-wave seasonal shape at period 12 --
seasonal differencing (=seasonal_d = 1=) should remove the deterministic
trend and seasonal shape together, leaving the AR(1) noise for the
regular and seasonal AR terms to capture.

#+name: arima-seasonal-fit
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== SARIMA(1,0,0)(1,1,0)_12 on a seasonal series ===\n";
    std::vector<double> seasonal_y;
    {
        std::mt19937_64 rng(7);
        std::normal_distribution<double> noise(0.0, 1.0);
        std::vector<double> s(120);
        double prev = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < s.size(); ++i) {
            prev = 0.5 * prev + noise(rng);
            s[i] = 20.0 + 0.2 * static_cast<double>(i) +
                   5.0 * std::sin(2.0 * 3.14159265358979
                                  * static_cast<double>(i) / 12.0) + prev;
        }
        seasonal_y = s;

        ARIMAOptions options;
        options.p = 1;
        options.seasonal_p = 1;
        options.seasonal_d = 1;
        options.seasonal_period = 12;
        options.de_population_size = 100;
        options.de_max_generations = 500;
        const ARIMA model(s, options);

        std::cout << "AR coefficient: " << model.ar_coefficients()[0]
                   << ", seasonal AR coefficient: "
                   << model.seasonal_ar_coefficients()[0] << "\n";
        std::cout << "sigma^2: " << model.sigma2()
                   << ", AIC: " << model.aic() << "\n";
        const auto sfc = model.forecast(12);
        print_vector("12-step forecast", sfc);

        auto plot = RPlot::create();
        plot.line(iota_range(1, s.size()), s, "observed", {37, 99, 235}, 1.5);
        std::vector<double> fc_x =
            iota_range(static_cast<double>(s.size()) + 1, sfc.size());
        plot.line(fc_x, sfc, "forecast", {220, 38, 38}, 1.5);
        plot.title("SARIMA(1,0,0)(1,1,0)_12: Seasonal Series")
            .x_label("t").y_label("y").background({250, 250, 252});
        plot.save_svg("build/org/arima_seasonal_forecast.svg");
        std::cout << "wrote arima_seasonal_forecast.svg\n";
    }
#+end_src

*** Holt-Winters on retail-style seasonal data

A synthetic monthly series with an upward trend and a repeating 12-month
seasonal shape, roughly evoking retail sales with a holiday spike (the
true seasonal multiplier peaks at 1.6 in December, versus a low of 0.75
in February).

#+name: arima-hw-fit
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Exponential smoothing: Holt-Winters"
               << " on retail-style seasonal data ===\n";
    std::vector<double> retail_y;
    {
        const std::vector<double> seasonal_shape{
            0.8, 0.75, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0, 1.05,
            1.1, 1.05, 1.0, 1.1, 1.3, 1.6};
        std::mt19937_64 rng(11);
        std::normal_distribution<double> noise(0.0, 3.0);
        std::vector<double> y(48);
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < y.size(); ++i) {
            const double level = 100.0 + 2.0 * static_cast<double>(i);
            y[i] = level * seasonal_shape[i % 12] + noise(rng);
        }
        retail_y = y;

        ExponentialSmoothingOptions options;
        options.trend = TrendType::Additive;
        options.seasonal = SeasonalType::Multiplicative;
        options.seasonal_period = 12;
        options.de_population_size = 60;
        options.de_max_generations = 300;
        const ExponentialSmoothing model(y, options);

        std::cout << "alpha=" << model.alpha() << " beta=" << model.beta()
                   << " gamma=" << model.gamma() << "\n";
        std::cout << "sigma^2: " << model.sigma2()
                   << ", AIC: " << model.aic() << "\n";
        const auto rfc = model.forecast(12);
        print_vector("12-month forecast", rfc);

        auto plot = RPlot::create();
        plot.line(iota_range(1, y.size()), y, "observed", {37, 99, 235}, 1.5);
        plot.line(iota_range(1, model.fitted_values().size()),
                   model.fitted_values(), "fitted", {22, 163, 74}, 1.0);
        std::vector<double> fc_x =
            iota_range(static_cast<double>(y.size()) + 1, rfc.size());
        plot.line(fc_x, rfc, "forecast", {220, 38, 38}, 1.5);
        plot.title("Holt-Winters: Retail-Style Seasonal Data")
            .x_label("month").y_label("sales").background({250, 250, 252});
        plot.save_svg("build/org/hw_retail_forecast.svg");
        std::cout << "wrote hw_retail_forecast.svg\n";
    }

are not always separately interpretable against a known truth the way a
regression coefficient often is.

** Simple exponential smoothing versus Holt's linear trend

#+begin_example
SES alpha=0.0777
SES 5-step forecast: 50.1126 50.1126 50.1126 50.1126 50.1126
Holt alpha=0.3792 beta=0.9999
Holt 5-step forecast: 50.0182 49.9119 49.8056 49.6993 49.5930
#+end_example

Simple exponential smoothing forecasts a flat 50.1126 at every horizon --
exactly the correct behavior for a series with no real trend, since SES
has no trend component to (mis)estimate in the first place. Holt's model,
given the identical ten data points, drives its trend-smoothing parameter
$\beta$ to 0.9999 -- effectively its numerical upper boundary -- and
forecasts a small but steadily /declining/ trend (50.02 down to 49.59
over five steps) that does not exist in the true generating process.
§5 discusses why this happened and what it implies about model choice.

* Discussion

**A concrete warning about fitting unnecessary trend.** §4.4's comparison
is this report's central finding: given a genuinely flat series, adding a
trend component that the data does not need does not simply produce a
harmless zero estimate -- it can produce a spurious, confidently-stated
trend, with $\beta$ pinned at its search boundary as the optimizer tries
to use the trend term to explain ordinary sampling noise in a ten-point
series. Ten observations is little enough data that a few points'
random fluctuation can look, to an SSE-minimizing search, like the start
of a trend worth tracking closely. Simple exponential smoothing, by
having no trend parameter to (mis)fit at all, is immune to this
particular failure mode by construction.

**ARIMA's coefficients versus Holt-Winters' coefficients tell different
kinds of stories.** §4.1's ARIMA coefficients (0.3923, 0.0842) do not
individually correspond to any single parameter of the true generating
process, since ARIMA models autocorrelation structure abstractly rather
than named components; §4.3's Holt-Winters parameters ($\alpha$, $\beta$,
$\gamma$), by contrast, each map onto an interpretable question ("how
fast does the level move," "how fast does the trend move," "how fast does
the seasonal shape move") that this report could check directly against
how the simulation was actually built. Neither approach is more "correct"
-- §2.7 already frames them as two different traditions -- but a
practitioner choosing between them is also choosing between two very
different kinds of after-the-fact interpretability.

**Connection to the wider project.** This report's
simulated-data-with-known-truth approach follows directly from the
[[file:lmm_analysis.org][LMM]] and [[file:glmm_analysis.org][GLMM
companion reports]]' own use of simulation to check recovery against a
known answer; here, uniquely, the check is qualitative (does the forecast
shape and parameter story make sense) rather than a direct numeric
comparison against one "true" coefficient, since neither ARIMA's nor
Holt-Winters' parameters correspond one-to-one with the handful of
numbers used to generate any of this report's four series.

* Conclusion

Fitting seasonal ARIMA and exponential smoothing models with
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::ARIMA= and =stats::ExponentialSmoothing= to
four simulated time series reproduces the Box-Jenkins and Holt-Winters
traditions' textbook behaviors: forecast uncertainty growing with horizon
for a differenced random walk, a seasonal ARIMA model correctly
extrapolating a twelve-month cycle, and Holt-Winters recovering smoothing
parameters that tell a coherent story about a simulated retail series'
trend and seasonal structure. The report's most important single result
is a cautionary one: fitting Holt's linear trend model to a genuinely
flat series drives its trend parameter to its numerical boundary and
produces a real, spurious forecasted trend, while simple exponential
smoothing -- with no trend parameter available to misuse -- correctly
forecasts a flat line. As with its companions, this entire report is
reproducible end to end via =just org-example arima_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.3.6's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
