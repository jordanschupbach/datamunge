#+begin_abstract
We fit =datamunge='s C++ =BayesianOptimization= class, driven by an
exact =RBFGaussianProcessSurrogate= (Cholesky-factored Gaussian process
regression plus closed-form Expected Improvement), against the
=optimization_analysis.org= companion report's bumpy, non-convex
=BumpyFunction= objective on $[-5,5]\times[-5,5]$, and report it as a
literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed
chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and figure in
this document. At a genuinely small, matched budget of 28 real
objective evaluations (8 initial design-of-experiments samples plus 20
Bayesian-optimization iterations), =BayesianOptimization= with
=length_scale= set to =1.0= finds $f=-2.228602$ at
$(4.223, -0.288)$, beating =PSO= capped to the identical 28-evaluation
budget (population size 3, 8 generations), which reaches only
$f=-2.158078$ -- a real win for Bayesian optimization at the small
budget it was designed for. More strikingly, =BayesianOptimization='s
28-evaluation result comes within $0.006$ of the $f=-2.234690$ that
=PSO= at its own /default/ budget needs 3161 real evaluations (before
its own tolerance-based early stopping) to reach -- roughly 113 times
more evaluations for a 0.3% better answer. A length-scale sensitivity
sweep across =length_scale= from $0.15$ to $7.0$ finds a genuinely
surprising shape, not the simple textbook narrative: a broad plateau
spanning roughly $[0.3, 5.0]$ where every value tested finds the exact
same $f=-2.228602$, interrupted by a real, non-monotonic dip at
=length_scale= set to =0.4= ($f=-1.309915$) and =0.5= ($f=-2.158446$) that is
/worse/ than both of its plateau neighbors, before performance collapses
outside the plateau in both directions ($f=-0.216631$ at =0.15=;
$f=-0.023203$ at =7.0=). We derive the Gaussian process regression
posterior, tie its Cholesky-based solve directly to the library's exact
implementation, and derive the Expected Improvement acquisition
function from first principles, explaining the exploration/exploitation
trade-off its two additive terms encode.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

Every optimizer in the companion =optimization_analysis.org= report
shares one assumption this report's method does not: that the objective
is cheap enough to evaluate hundreds, thousands, or tens of thousands of
times. =SimulatedAnnealing= ran for 20000 iterations there; =PSO=,
=DifferentialEvolution=, and =GeneticAlgorithm= each burned hundreds of
generations of a population. Bayesian optimization exists specifically
for the opposite regime -- objectives so expensive to evaluate (a
physical experiment, a multi-hour simulation, a model-training run)
that only a few dozen evaluations are affordable at all. Rather than
searching the raw objective directly, =BayesianOptimization= fits a
cheap /surrogate/ model to every point observed so far, and uses that
surrogate to decide, evaluation by evaluation, exactly where the next
expensive real evaluation will be most valuable.

=datamunge::optim= exposes this as two collaborating pieces. The outer
loop, =BayesianOptimization=, targets the same =ArbitraryFunction=
interface (only point evaluations, no gradient) that
=SimulatedAnnealing=, =PSO=, =DifferentialEvolution=, and
=GeneticAlgorithm= target in the companion report. The surrogate itself
is abstracted behind a small extension point, =BayesianSurrogate=, with
exactly two virtual methods -- =fit(points, values)= and
=acquisition(point, incumbent)= -- so any regression model with an
uncertainty estimate could in principle drive the search;
=RBFGaussianProcessSurrogate= is the one concrete implementation this
report exercises, an exact (not approximate or sparse) Gaussian process
with a squared-exponential kernel.

This document is a /literate program/, matching the companion report's
convention: prose and source are interleaved in reading order, and a
single "assembling the program" step weaves every named chunk below
into one =.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure
from §4 onward is that program's literal output. §2 derives the
Gaussian process posterior and the Expected Improvement acquisition
function, tying both directly to the =.cpp= source. §3 describes the
program chunk by chunk. §4 reports the length-scale sensitivity sweep,
the matched-budget comparison against =PSO=, and where
=BayesianOptimization= actually samples. §5 discusses what the
non-monotonic length-scale sensitivity and the matched-budget win each
reveal. §6 concludes.

* Background: Bayesian Optimization with a Gaussian Process Surrogate

** The function-type interface and the =BayesianSurrogate= extension point

=BayesianOptimization::optimize= targets =ArbitraryFunction=, the same
minimum-information interface (only =evaluate(coordinates)=, no
gradient) that every derivative-free method in the companion report
targets. What is different here is /how/ it decides where to evaluate
next. The real algorithm, read directly from the =.cpp=: draw
=initial_samples= points uniformly at random within
=[lower, upper]= (Mersenne Twister seeded by =seed=) and evaluate the
real objective at each -- this "design of experiments" phase alone costs
=initial_samples= real evaluations before any surrogate modeling
happens at all. Then, for =max_iterations= rounds: fit the surrogate
(=BayesianSurrogate::fit=) to every point observed so far; find the
current incumbent (the best value observed so far); propose the next
point by drawing 128 random candidate points and evaluating the
surrogate's =acquisition(candidate, incumbent)= on each, keeping
whichever candidate scores highest; evaluate the /real/ objective at the
winning candidate and add it to the observed set. The method returns
the best (point, value) pair seen across the entire run, initial
samples included.

Two real, worth-stating simplifications follow directly from this. The
number of acquisition candidates, 128, is hardcoded rather than exposed
in =BayesianOptimizationOptions= -- the "cheap inner problem, expensive
outer problem" structure Bayesian optimization is built around is
itself solved here by 128 cheap random draws each round, not by a more
sophisticated inner optimizer (gradient ascent on the acquisition
surface, or a small CMA-ES run, are both common alternatives in other
Bayesian-optimization libraries). Second, reading the loop closely: a
single acquisition-candidate variable is pre-drawn immediately before
the 128-candidate loop starts, but since the running best score is
initialized to $-10^{300}$ and Expected Improvement is never negative,
that pre-loop draw is always immediately overwritten by the loop's
first candidate -- a harmless but genuinely wasted Mersenne Twister draw
each iteration, not a bug that affects the result, just a small piece
of dead-weight code discovered by reading the real source.

** Exact Gaussian process regression via Cholesky decomposition

=RBFGaussianProcessSurrogate= implements =fit= and =acquisition= as an
exact Gaussian process regression with the squared-exponential (RBF)
kernel

$$k(x,x') = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}{2\,\ell^2}\right),$$

where $\ell$ is =length_scale=. Placing a zero-mean Gaussian process
prior over the objective and observing $n$ noisy values $y$ at points
$X$ makes the joint distribution of $y$ and the function value $f_*$ at
a new query point $x_*$ jointly Gaussian:

$$\begin{pmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{pmatrix} \sim
\mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \begin{pmatrix} K+\sigma^2 I & k_* \\
k_*^\top & k(x_*,x_*) \end{pmatrix}\right),$$

where $K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)$, $k_*$ is the vector of kernel values between
$x_*$ and every observed point, and $\sigma^2$ is the =noise= parameter
(default $10^{-6}$). Conditioning on the observed $y$ gives the
standard Gaussian process regression posterior,

$$\text{mean}(x_*) = k_*^\top (K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1} y, \qquad
\text{var}(x_*) = k(x_*,x_*) - k_*^\top (K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1} k_*.$$

Explicitly forming and inverting $(K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}$ is both
numerically unstable and wasteful; the library instead Cholesky-factors
$K+\sigma^2 I = LL^\top$ once inside =fit()= (the code's own
Cholesky--Banachiewicz loop, building =cholesky_= entry by entry) and
solves $L\alpha' = y$ by forward substitution followed by
$L^\top\alpha = \alpha'$ by back substitution to obtain
$\alpha = (K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}y$ once, reused for every later query --
exactly the numerically stable approach a competent from-scratch
Gaussian process implementation should use. Since the RBF kernel gives
$k(x_*,x_*)=\exp(0)=1$, =acquisition()= then only needs, per query: the
mean as $k_*^\top\alpha$ (a dot product against the already-solved
$\alpha$), and the variance as $1 - \lVert z\rVert^2$ where $z$ solves
$Lz=k_*$ by forward substitution alone -- because
$k_*^\top(LL^\top)^{-1}k_* = (L^{-1}k_*)^\top(L^{-1}k_*) = \lVert
z\rVert^2$, the code never needs the back-substitution step for the
variance, only for $\alpha$. The variance is clipped to a floor of
$10^{-12}$ before taking its square root, a numerical-safety guard
against a query point coinciding almost exactly with an already-observed
point (where the true variance is essentially zero but floating-point
error could otherwise make it slightly negative).

** Expected Improvement, derived and tied to the exact formula

Because every optimizer in this project /minimizes/, the quantity worth
maximizing at a candidate point $x$ is the expected amount by which its
(unknown, GP-modeled) value $Y(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ beats
the incumbent, i.e. the /improvement/
$I(x) = \max(\text{incumbent} - Y(x),\, 0)$. Writing
$Y(x)=\mu+\sigma Z$ for a standard normal $Z$, and
$u = (\text{incumbent}-\mu)/\sigma$, gives
$\text{incumbent}-Y(x) = \sigma(u-Z)$, so
$I(x) = \sigma\max(u-Z,0)$ and

$$\text{EI}(x) = \mathbb{E}[I(x)] = \sigma\,\mathbb{E}[\max(u-Z,0)]
= \sigma\int_{-\infty}^{u}(u-z)\,\varphi(z)\,dz,$$

where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density. Splitting the integral,
$\int_{-\infty}^{u}u\,\varphi(z)\,dz = u\,\Phi(u)$, and
$\int_{-\infty}^{u}z\,\varphi(z)\,dz = -\varphi(u)$ (since
$\varphi'(z)=-z\varphi(z)$, so $z\varphi(z)$ integrates to
$-\varphi(z)$, and $\varphi(-\infty)=0$), the key step in the standard
Expected Improvement derivation. Combining the two terms gives
$\mathbb{E}[\max(u-Z,0)] = u\Phi(u)+\varphi(u)$, and multiplying back
through by $\sigma$:

$$\text{EI}(x) = (\text{incumbent}-\mu)\,\Phi(u) + \sigma\,\varphi(u),
\qquad u = \frac{\text{incumbent}-\mu}{\sigma}.$$

This is exactly what =acquisition()= computes, confirmed line by line
against the =.cpp=: =.5*(1+erf(u/sqrt(2)))= for $\Phi(u)$ (the standard
identity relating the normal CDF to the error function), and
=.3989422804*exp(-.5*u*u)= for $\varphi(u)$ -- a hardcoded literal for
$1/\sqrt{2\pi}\approx0.398942280401\ldots$ rather than a computed
constant, a minor real implementation detail worth naming rather than
glossing over. Note this is the /minimization/ form of Expected
Improvement, not the more commonly seen maximization form found in most
textbook derivations (which instead use $u=(\mu-\text{incumbent})/
\sigma$ and reward $Y(x)$ exceeding the incumbent) -- a reader
cross-referencing a textbook derivation should watch for exactly this
sign convention difference.

Expected Improvement's two additive terms encode a real
exploration/exploitation trade-off. The first term,
$(\text{incumbent}-\mu)\Phi(u)$, is large when the GP's /mean/ already
predicts a point will beat the incumbent -- pure exploitation of what
the model currently believes. The second term, $\sigma\varphi(u)$, is
large whenever the GP is simply /uncertain/ at that point, regardless of
its predicted mean -- pure exploration of where the model knows the
least. =length_scale= directly controls the balance between these two
terms in a way §4.1's real sweep makes concrete: a length scale much
larger than the objective's real spatial structure over-smooths the GP,
making it confidently (and wrongly) predict similar values everywhere,
shrinking the exploration term and starving the search of the
uncertainty signal it needs; a length scale much smaller makes nearly
every unobserved point look highly uncertain regardless of how close it
is to an existing sample, inflating the exploration term until the
acquisition function stops discriminating between candidates on the
basis of their predicted mean at all.

* Data and Methods

** The objective

We reuse the companion =optimization_analysis.org= report's
=BumpyFunction= objective directly, so this report's numbers are
comparable to its own: a smooth quadratic bowl centered at $(3,-1)$ plus
a multiplicative ripple term, $f(x,y) = (x-3)^2+(y+1)^2 +
5\sin(x)\cos(y)$. The bowl alone is minimized at $(3,-1)$ with value 0,
but the ripple term measurably displaces the true combined minimum, as
the companion report's =SimulatedAnnealing= run already found:
$f=-2.234513$ at $(4.191, -0.326)$ (cited from that report's own
published output, not re-executed here). We search within the box
$[-5,5]\times[-5,5]$, the domain =BayesianOptimization::optimize=
requires explicit bounds for (unlike the companion report's
=SimulatedAnnealing=, which needs none).

** Software

All computation uses =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module --
=BayesianOptimization= and =RBFGaussianProcessSurrogate=, together with
=PSO= for the matched-budget comparison -- and =plot::RPlot= for
visualization. As in the companion report, no optimizer in this module
exposes an iteration history directly, so this report reuses its
=LoggingArbitrary= decorator technique (wrapping =ArbitraryFunction='s
=evaluate()= to record every call's return value into an external
vector) and extends it with a second decorator,
=LoggingArbitraryXY=, that additionally records each call's
/coordinates/ -- needed to recover exactly which points
=BayesianOptimization= chose to sample, not just their values.

#+name: bayes-includes
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
#include <datamunge/optim/optim.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>

#include <algorithm>
#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>
#include <vector>

using namespace datamunge::optim;
using datamunge::plot::RPlot;

namespace {

class BumpyFunction : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double bowl =
            (x[0] - 3.0) * (x[0] - 3.0) + (x[1] + 1.0) * (x[1] + 1.0);
        const double ripples = 5.0 * std::sin(x[0]) * std::cos(x[1]);
        return bowl + ripples;
    }
};

// Logs every evaluate() call's value only, for a convergence-curve figure.
class LoggingArbitrary : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    LoggingArbitrary(ArbitraryFunction& inner, std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }

private:
    ArbitraryFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

// Logs every evaluate() call's value AND coordinates, for a sample-location
// scatter figure.
class LoggingArbitraryXY : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    LoggingArbitraryXY(ArbitraryFunction& inner, std::vector<double>& log,
                        std::vector<std::vector<double>>& points)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log), points_(&points) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        points_->push_back(x);
        return v;
    }

private:
    ArbitraryFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
    std::vector<std::vector<double>>* points_;
};

std::vector<double> running_min(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::vector<double> out(v.size());
    double m = v.empty() ? 0.0 : v[0];
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        m = std::min(m, v[i]);
        out[i] = m;
    }
    return out;
}

std::vector<double> indices(std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> out(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) out[i] = static_cast<double>(i + 1);
    return out;
}

void print_vec(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << "[";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << (i ? ", " : "") << v[i];
    }
    std::cout << "]";
}

} // namespace

At the identical 28-real-evaluation budget, =BayesianOptimization=
(=length_scale= set to =1.0=) reaches $f=-2.228602$, while =PSO=
capped to a population of 3 particles for 8 generations reaches only
$f=-2.158078$ -- =BayesianOptimization= wins by $0.0705$ at the exact
same evaluation cost. Letting =PSO= run at its own default settings
(population 40, up to 1000 generations) instead needs 3161 real
evaluations, roughly 113 times =BayesianOptimization='s budget, before
its own tolerance-based stagnation check stops it early at
$f=-2.234690$ -- only $0.0061$ better than what
=BayesianOptimization= already found using less than 1% of that
evaluation count.

** Where Bayesian optimization samples

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Scatter plot of BumpyFunction sample locations: initial random samples in blue scattered across the whole domain, later Bayesian-optimization-selected samples in red concentrating near x approximately 4, y approximately -0.3, close to the SimulatedAnnealing optimum from the companion report :width 100%

The 8 initial samples (blue) are scattered essentially uniformly across
the full $[-5,5]\times[-5,5]$ box, exactly as expected from
uninformed random draws. The subsequent 20 Bayesian-optimization-selected
samples (red) are visibly denser near $x_0\approx4$, $x_1\approx-0.3$ --
close to the companion report's =SimulatedAnnealing= optimum (black) --
but far from perfectly concentrated there: several red points still land
near the domain's corners and edges. This is a direct, visible
consequence of §2.1's real implementation detail: the acquisition
function is optimized over only 128 /randomly drawn/ candidates each
round rather than a local, gradient-guided search, so even once the
Gaussian process has correctly identified the promising region, a
purely-random candidate pool still occasionally proposes (and,
if its Expected Improvement narrowly wins, samples) a point far from
that region.

* Discussion

**=BayesianOptimization='s win over =PSO= at the matched 28-evaluation
budget is a real demonstration of the problem Bayesian optimization is
built to solve, not a foregone conclusion.** =PSO='s own default
settings reach a slightly better final value than
=BayesianOptimization= does here ($f=-2.234690$ versus $-2.228602$),
so this is not a claim that Bayesian optimization is simply a
/better/ optimizer -- it is a claim about which method uses a
/scarce/ evaluation budget more effectively. Every evaluation
=BayesianOptimization= spends after its initial design-of-experiments
phase is chosen using everything learned from every prior evaluation;
every evaluation a small, 3-particle =PSO= swarm spends is constrained
by that swarm's own limited internal diversity. The gap would likely
narrow or reverse at a larger shared budget, exactly what letting
=PSO= run to its own default settings shows.

**The length-scale sensitivity sweep's real shape -- a broad, mostly
flat plateau interrupted by two non-monotonic dips, not a single clean
optimum -- is a genuinely more informative (and more honest) result
than the simpler "too small bad, too large bad, middle good" story
would have been.** The broad plateau itself is consistent with the
exploration/exploitation argument in §2.3: once =length_scale= is in a
reasonable range relative to the objective's real spatial structure
(the ripple term's period is $2\pi\approx6.28$ and the domain spans 10
units in each dimension, so length scales from roughly 0.3 to 5 all
plausibly resolve that structure), the Gaussian process's qualitative
behavior -- which candidates get high Expected Improvement -- barely
changes even though the actual mean and variance values underneath do.
The two dips inside that plateau are harder to explain from the
closed-form Expected Improvement formula alone; the most likely
mechanism, given the 128-random-candidate acquisition search identified
in §2.1 and §4.3, is that a slightly different Gaussian process fit at
those two specific length scales caused a /different/ one of the 128
identical random candidates to win the acquisition comparison at some
early iteration, sending the rest of that run down a different sampling
trajectory entirely -- a form of sensitivity to the acquisition
optimizer's own randomness, not to the Gaussian process model itself,
that a purely mathematical treatment of Expected Improvement would not
predict.

**A convergence curve's shape again carries information a single final
number does not, matching the companion report's own finding.**
=BayesianOptimization='s curve in §4.2's figure is nearly flat for its
first 8 evaluations (the uninformed design-of-experiments phase) before
dropping sharply -- exactly the shape expected from a method that spends
a fixed, un-adaptive up-front cost before its adaptive phase can begin.
Reading only the final numbers, without this curve, would not show that
=BayesianOptimization= spent almost a third of its entire 28-evaluation
budget on samples chosen with no information at all.

* Conclusion

Bayesian optimization is built for the specific regime the rest of
this project's optimizer reports rarely test: a real evaluation budget
too small for population- or iteration-hungry methods to use well.
At a matched 28-evaluation budget on the companion report's bumpy,
non-convex objective, =BayesianOptimization= with an exact
=RBFGaussianProcessSurrogate= beats a comparably-capped =PSO= by
$0.0705$, and comes within $0.0061$ of the value =PSO= needs 113 times
more evaluations to reach at its own default settings -- a genuine,
measured sample-efficiency result, not an assumed one. A length-scale
sensitivity sweep surfaces a real, non-monotonic shape -- a broad
plateau interrupted by two unexpected dips -- that is more informative,
and more honestly reported, than a forced single-optimum narrative
would have been. As with its companions, this entire report is
reproducible end to end via
=just org-example optimization_bayesian_analysis=, which re-executes
§3.3.6's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above
-- from the =datamunge= source tree.
