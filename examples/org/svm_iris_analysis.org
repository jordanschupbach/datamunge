#+begin_abstract
We fit a soft-margin, multi-class support vector machine to Fisher's (1936)
iris data using =datamunge='s C++ =stats::SVM= class, and report it as a
literate program: every code fragment below is a real, compiled, executed
chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and figure in this
document. The default radial-basis-function (RBF) model separates the three
species with 97.33% training accuracy using 52 of 150 training points as
support vectors. A four-way kernel comparison ranks radial best (97.33%),
ahead of linear (96.67%), polynomial (95.33%), and sigmoid (89.33%); a
cost-parameter sweep reproduces the classic underfit/overfit tradeoff
directly, collapsing to 33.33% accuracy at $C=0.01$ before plateauing at
98.67% for $C \geq 10$. We derive the maximal-margin classifier, its soft-margin
relaxation, the dual problem, the kernel trick, and Sequential Minimal
Optimization (SMO) from first principles, connect each to the corresponding
library call, and close with a direct comparison to the companion LDA report
on the same dataset.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The support vector machine (SVM) answers the same three-species iris
classification question as the companion report,
[[file:lda_iris_analysis.org][=lda_iris_analysis.org=]], but from a completely different starting
point. Where linear discriminant analysis begins with a probabilistic model
of each class and derives a boundary from it, the SVM begins with a purely
geometric question -- *which separating boundary leaves the largest possible
margin between the classes?* -- and only later, through the kernel trick,
gains the ability to draw boundaries that aren't straight lines at all. This
report builds that idea up from scratch, in C++, against the same 150
flowers and four measurements used throughout this project's other
worked examples.

As with the companion report, this document is a /literate program/ in
Knuth's sense: prose and source are interleaved in reading order, and a
single "assembling the program" step (§3.3.10) weaves every named chunk
below into one =.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and
figure from §4 onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives the maximal-margin classifier, its soft-margin relaxation, the
dual problem and support vectors, the kernel trick, and the SMO algorithm
this library uses to solve it, plus the one-vs-one scheme that extends a
binary method to three species. §3 describes the program chunk by chunk.
§4 reports the fitted model, a four-kernel comparison, a cost-parameter
sweep, and a 2-D decision-boundary picture. §5 discusses what the results
mean and compares them directly to the LDA report's findings on the same
data. §6 concludes.

* Background: Support Vector Machines

** Setup and notation

Consider first the binary case: $n$ labeled points $(x_i, y_i)$,
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$, $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$. A linear classifier is a
hyperplane $w^\top x + b = 0$; a new point is classified by the sign of
$w^\top x + b$. Section 2.7 extends this to the three iris species.

** The maximal margin classifier

If the two classes are linearly separable, infinitely many hyperplanes
separate them perfectly, and not all generalize equally well. The SVM picks
the one furthest from both classes: the perpendicular distance from the
hyperplane to the nearest point of either class -- the /margin/ -- is
exactly $1/\lVert w \rVert$ once $w, b$ are scaled so that the nearest points
satisfy $y_i(w^\top x_i + b) = 1$, so the full margin width is
$2/\lVert w \rVert$. Maximizing that margin is therefore the same as
/minimizing/ $\lVert w \rVert$, giving the hard-margin problem

\[
\min_{w,b} \ \tfrac12 \lVert w \rVert^2
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
y_i (w^\top x_i + b) \geq 1, \ \ i = 1, \dots, n .
\]

** Soft margin and the cost parameter

Real data is rarely perfectly separable -- §4.1's exploratory plot already
shows /versicolor/ and /virginica/ overlapping -- so a hard constraint that
every point sit on the correct side of the margin is too rigid to be
useful. The soft-margin relaxation adds a per-point slack $\xi_i \geq 0$
that lets a point violate its margin, at a cost:

\[
\min_{w,b,\xi} \ \tfrac12 \lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
y_i (w^\top x_i + b) \geq 1 - \xi_i, \ \ \xi_i \geq 0 .
\]

$C$ -- =SVMOptions::cost= -- is the knob that trades margin width against
training fit: a small $C$ tolerates many violations in exchange for a wide,
simple margin (risking underfitting), while a large $C$ punishes violations
heavily, narrowing the margin to fit the training data more exactly (risking
overfitting). §4.5 measures this tradeoff directly by refitting across seven
values of $C$.

** The dual problem and support vectors

The soft-margin primal above is a constrained quadratic program; introducing
Lagrange multipliers $\alpha_i$ for the margin constraints and eliminating
$w$ and $\xi$ gives the /dual/ problem,

\[
\max_{\alpha} \ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \tfrac12 \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j \, x_i^\top x_j
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
0 \leq \alpha_i \leq C, \ \ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0 ,
\]

and the decision function becomes
$f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \, x_i^\top x + b$. At the optimum, the KKT
conditions force $\alpha_i = 0$ for every point safely on the correct side
of its margin -- only points sitting exactly on the margin or violating it
have $\alpha_i > 0$, and only /those/ points -- the /support vectors/ --
appear in $f(x)$ at all. This is exactly =SVM::num_support_vectors()= and
the per-pair =PairModel::support_indices=/=support_alpha_y= cached in
[[file:../../include/datamunge/stats/svm.hpp][=include/datamunge/stats/svm.hpp=]]: a fitted SVM only needs to remember its
support vectors, not the full training set.

** The kernel trick

Both the dual objective and $f(x)$ above depend on the data only through
inner products $x_i^\top x_j$. Replacing that inner product everywhere with
a /kernel/ $K(x_i, x_j) = \phi(x_i)^\top \phi(x_j)$, for some (possibly very
high-dimensional) feature map $\phi$, lets the same linear-margin machinery
produce a nonlinear boundary in the original space -- without ever computing
$\phi$ explicitly. =datamunge::stats::SVM= implements four kernels
(=SVMKernel=), matching their definitions in
[[file:../../src/datamunge/stats/svm.cpp][=src/datamunge/stats/svm.cpp=]] exactly:

\begin{align*}
\text{Linear:} \quad & K(x, x') = x^\top x' \\
\text{Polynomial:} \quad & K(x, x') = (\gamma \, x^\top x' + r)^{d} \\
\text{Radial (RBF):} \quad & K(x, x') = \exp(-\gamma \lVert x - x' \rVert^2) \\
\text{Sigmoid:} \quad & K(x, x') = \tanh(\gamma \, x^\top x' + r)
\end{align*}

where $\gamma$ (=SVMOptions::gamma=, defaulting to $1/p$ when left
$\leq 0$), $r$ (=coef0=), and $d$ (=degree=) are the kernel's own tunable
parameters. §4.4 fits and compares all four.

** Sequential Minimal Optimization

The dual is a quadratic program with $n=150$ variables here, small enough
for an off-the-shelf QP solver -- but the classic, highly influential
approach (Platt, 1998) instead solves it with almost no linear algebra at
all: /Sequential Minimal Optimization/ repeatedly picks the smallest
possible subproblem that can move while preserving
$\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$ -- exactly /two/ $\alpha$'s at a time -- and
updates that pair in closed form. Sweeping this update over the whole
dataset repeatedly, until a full pass finds no update larger than a
tolerance (=SVMOptions::tolerance=) or a fixed number of consecutive
no-change passes is reached (=SVMOptions::max_passes=), converges to the
dual optimum without ever forming the full $n \times n$ kernel matrix's
inverse. This is exactly what =datamunge='s (simplified) SMO solver
implements internally.

** Multi-class extension: one-vs-one voting

An SVM is inherently a two-class method, but the iris problem has three.
=datamunge::stats::SVM= extends to $K$ classes the same way R's
=e1071::svm= does: fit one binary SVM per /pair/ of classes -- $K(K-1)/2 = 3$
pairwise classifiers here -- and classify a new point by running it through
all three and taking a majority vote. This is exactly
=SVMPrediction::votes=, used directly in §4.7. Unlike LDA's posterior
probabilities, a one-vs-one vote is a small integer count (out of 3 here),
not a graded confidence -- §5 returns to what that difference means in
practice.

** Feature scaling and what we will report

Because every kernel above is a function of raw dot products or Euclidean
distances, a predictor measured on a much larger numeric scale than the
others would dominate the kernel regardless of its true discriminative
value. =SVMOptions::scale= (default =true=, matching =e1071::svm=)
standardizes every numeric predictor to zero mean and unit variance before
fitting, and is left on throughout this report. With the model fit, we
report: the group/kernel summary and training diagnostics =print_summary=
already exposes; a training confusion matrix and accuracy; support vectors
per class; a comparison across all four kernels; a sweep across the cost
parameter $C$; and a 2-D decision-boundary picture -- fit on only two of the
four predictors purely for visualization, since a decision surface in more
than two dimensions can't be drawn directly (the same caveat the LDA report
makes about its discriminant-space plot, though there the 2-D view is an
/exact/ projection of the full model -- here it is a genuinely different,
separately-fit model, and §4.6 is explicit about that).

* Data and Methods

** The data

As in the companion report: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns Fisher's
150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ -- measured on
=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width= (cm), with no
missing values.

** Software

Once again, the entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, the formula-driven =stats::SVM= for every
model in this report, and =plot::RPlot=/=plot::RLayout= for every figure.
No external plotting or statistics package is used.

Refit with each of the four kernels from §2.5 in turn, at the default cost

    {
        struct KernelRun { std::string name; SVMKernel kernel; };
        const std::vector<KernelRun> runs = {
            {"linear", SVMKernel::Linear},
            {"polynomial", SVMKernel::Polynomial},
            {"radial", SVMKernel::Radial},
            {"sigmoid", SVMKernel::Sigmoid},
        };
        std::vector<double> accs, svs;
        std::vector<std::string> names;
        std::cout << "\nKernel comparison (cost=1, default gamma):\n";

*** Visualizing the decision boundary

A decision surface over four predictors can't be drawn, so this section
fits a /separate/ RBF model using only =Petal.Length= and =Petal.Width= --
the two most discriminative measurements per §4.1 -- purely to draw it.
Every point on a fine grid is classified with that 2-predictor model and
plotted in a pale, class-colored background; the real training points are
then layered on top in the same colors used throughout this report.

#+name: svm-decision-boundary
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    {
        SVM boundary_model(iris, "Species ~ Petal.Length + Petal.Width");

        const double x_lo = 0.5, x_hi = 7.5;
        const double y_lo = 0.0, y_hi = 3.0;
        constexpr int nx = 70, ny = 70;

        auto region = RPlot::create();
        for (std::size_t k = 0; k < kSpecies.size(); ++k) {
            std::vector<double> gx, gy;
            for (int i = 0; i < nx; ++i) {
                const double x = x_lo + (x_hi - x_lo) * i / (nx - 1);
                for (int j = 0; j < ny; ++j) {
                    const double y = y_lo + (y_hi - y_lo) * j / (ny - 1);
                    DataFrame pt;
                    pt.add_column("Petal.Length", std::vector<double>{x});
                    pt.add_column("Petal.Width", std::vector<double>{y});
                    const auto pred = boundary_model.predict(pt);
                    if (pred[0] == kSpecies[k]) {
                        gx.push_back(x);
                        gy.push_back(y);
                    }
                }
            }
            region.points(gx, gy, kSpecies[k] + " region",
                          kSpeciesRegionColor[k], 3.2);
        }
        for (std::size_t k = 0; k < kSpecies.size(); ++k) {
            const auto subset = iris.filter([&](const DataFrame::Row& r) {
                return r.get_string("Species") == kSpecies[k];
            });
            region.points(subset.numeric_column("Petal.Length").values(),
                          subset.numeric_column("Petal.Width").values(),
                          kSpecies[k], kSpeciesColor[k], 5.5);
        }
        region.title("SVM Decision Regions (RBF, Petal.Length x Petal.Width)")
            .x_label("Petal.Length (cm)")
            .y_label("Petal.Width (cm)")
            .background({250, 250, 252});
        region.save_svg("build/org/svm_iris_decision_boundary.svg");
        std::cout << "wrote svm_iris_decision_boundary.svg\n";
    }

Training accuracy is 97.33% (146/150), with four errors, all -- again --
between /versicolor/ and /virginica/, split evenly in both directions (2
misclassified each way). This is very slightly worse than the LDA report's
98.00% (147/150, 3 errors) on identical data; §5 discusses why that's
unsurprising rather than a sign either method is "wrong."

Radial wins, but only narrowly over linear -- consistent with §4.1's

curved boundary buys little. Sigmoid is a clear outlier, both least accurate

visibly the weakest fit.

** Cost sensitivity

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Line plots of training accuracy and support-vector count against log10(cost) :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/svm_iris_cost_sensitivity.svg]]

| $C$   | Accuracy | Support vectors |
|-------+----------+------------------|
|  0.01 |   33.33% |              150 |
|  0.10 |   94.67% |              115 |
|  1.00 |   97.33% |               52 |
|  3.00 |   97.33% |               40 |
| 10.00 |   98.67% |               33 |
| 30.00 |   98.67% |               30 |
|100.00 |   98.67% |               29 |

$C=0.01$ is a textbook underfit: accuracy collapses to 33.33% -- exactly
chance performance for three balanced classes -- and every single training
point becomes a support vector, meaning the margin is so wide and so
heavily penalized for narrowing that the model effectively cannot separate
the classes at all. Accuracy recovers almost immediately by $C=0.1$ and
plateaus at 98.67% for $C \geq 10$, while the support-vector count keeps
/falling/ as $C$ grows, from 150 down to 29: a larger cost tolerates fewer
margin violations, so the solver needs progressively fewer boundary-defining
points to satisfy the tighter constraint. Both curves match §2.3's
prediction exactly.

* Discussion

**Comparing kernels and the cost sweep.** Both §4.4 and §4.5 tell the same
underlying story from different angles: this dataset is close enough to
linearly separable that a straight boundary (linear kernel, or a tightly
constrained RBF at high $C$) already does most of the work, and the
remaining errors are concentrated entirely in the small region where
/versicolor/ and /virginica/ genuinely overlap in measurement space. No
kernel or cost setting can do better than that overlap allows without
overfitting to noise -- which is exactly what the flat accuracy plateau
above $C = 10$ in §4.5 shows happening.

**SVM versus LDA on identical data.** The companion LDA report gets 98.00%
training accuracy (3 errors) on these same 150 flowers and this same
formula; the default SVM here gets 97.33% (4 errors) -- a difference of one
flower, well within noise for a 150-row resubstitution estimate, and not
grounds to prefer one method over the other on accuracy alone. The more
interesting difference is in what each model /says/ about its own
uncertainty. LDA's Bayes posterior for the boundary-case flower in its
report was 81.16% /virginica/ against 18.84% /versicolor/ -- a graded,
directly interpretable confidence that the case was close. The identical
flower here (§4.7's fourth row) gets exactly the same 2-1 vote split as an
unambiguous /virginica/ example one row above it: one-vs-one voting is a
discrete count out of $K(K-1)/2$ pairwise outcomes, and with only 3 pairs
for 3 classes, there simply isn't enough resolution to distinguish "clearly
virginica" from "barely virginica." A practitioner who needs calibrated
confidence, not just a label, has a real reason here to prefer LDA's
posterior probabilities -- or, if the geometric flexibility of a kernel
method is what's needed, to look at each pairwise decision value directly
rather than the collapsed vote count this report used.

**Limitations.** As with the LDA report, every accuracy figure above is a
resubstitution (training-set) estimate, optimistic by construction; unlike
LDA, the SVM here has multiple hyperparameters ($C$, $\gamma$, and per-kernel
$r$/$d$) that were swept or fixed by convention rather than tuned by
cross-validation, so the "best" kernel and cost identified in §4.4-4.5
should be read as illustrative of the tradeoffs involved, not as a properly
validated model-selection result.

* Conclusion

A soft-margin, one-vs-one support vector machine, fit with =datamunge='s
C++ =stats::SVM=, separates Fisher's three iris species with 97.33% training
accuracy using an RBF kernel and 52 support vectors, essentially matching
the companion LDA report's 98.00% on the same data. A four-kernel comparison
and a cost-parameter sweep both trace directly back to the margin/violation
tradeoff derived in §2.3, down to a textbook underfitting collapse at
$C=0.01$. The one qualitative difference from the LDA report worth
remembering: this SVM's one-vs-one votes are a coarse count, not a
calibrated probability, and §4.7 shows a genuinely ambiguous flower getting
exactly the same vote tally as an unambiguous one. As with its companion,
this entire document is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example svm_iris_analysis=, which re-executes §3.3.10's assembly
block -- and therefore every number and figure above -- from the
=datamunge= source tree.
