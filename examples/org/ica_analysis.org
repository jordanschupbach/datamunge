#+begin_abstract
We implement the deflationary FastICA algorithm (Hyvarinen & Oja, 1997)
in =datamunge='s C++ =stats::ICA= and report it as a literate program:
every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single
C++ binary that produced every number and figure in this document.
Independent Component Analysis solves /blind source separation/ -- the
"cocktail-party problem" of recovering unknown independent source signals
from unlabeled linear mixtures of them -- and it does so on a principle
distinct from every other method in this project's dimensionality-reduction
batch: where PCA and factor analysis remove only second-order /correlation/,
ICA removes all higher-order statistical /dependence/ by maximizing each
component's /non-Gaussianity/, which the central limit theorem makes a
valid proxy for "is a single source rather than a mixture." We demonstrate
this on three independent signals -- a sine, a square wave, and a
sawtooth -- linearly mixed by a fixed \(3 \times 3\) matrix into three
observed traces: from those mixtures alone, FastICA recovers all three
sources at a mean best-match correlation of 0.9999 (individually 1.0000,
0.9998, 1.0000), every component converging in at most seven fixed-point
iterations. Run on the identical mixtures, PCA recovers the sources at
only 0.7625 -- and, tellingly, /both/ methods produce perfectly
uncorrelated components (maximum cross-correlation 0.0000): the gap is
entirely in the higher-order dependence PCA cannot see, making
"decorrelation is not independence" a measured fact rather than a slogan.
A geometric example -- two uniform sources mixed into a sheared
parallelogram -- shows ICA's recovered axes aligning with the
parallelogram's independent edges (recovery 0.9996) while PCA's align with
the axes of variance (0.7236). Finally, a Gaussian-source control confirms
the method's one hard limitation exactly where the theory places it: with
Gaussian sources, ICA's recovery (0.8098) collapses to PCA's level
(0.7591), because a rotation of isotropic Gaussians is statistically
indistinguishable from the original and the sources are fundamentally
unidentifiable. We derive the mixing model, the independence-versus-
uncorrelatedness distinction, the non-Gaussianity principle, whitening,
the FastICA fixed-point iteration, and the sign/scale/permutation and
Gaussian indeterminacies, tying each to the corresponding library call.

* Introduction

This project's [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and
[[file:factor_analysis_analysis.org][factor analysis]] reports both build
on second-order statistics: they diagonalize a covariance or correlation
matrix, and their outputs are /uncorrelated/ -- linearly unrelated, but
free to be dependent in every nonlinear, higher-order way. For many
problems that is not enough. The canonical one is the /cocktail-party
problem/: several people speak at once, several microphones each record a
different /mixture/ of all the voices, and we want the individual voices
back -- knowing neither the voices nor how they were mixed. Decorrelating
the microphone signals does not separate the speakers; the mixtures can be
made uncorrelated and still each contain every voice.

Independent Component Analysis (Comon 1994; Hyvarinen & Oja 2000) solves
this by demanding the stronger property its name advertises: /statistical
independence/, not mere uncorrelatedness. It models the observations as
unknown linear mixtures of statistically independent latent sources,

\[
x = A s,
\]

and estimates both an unmixing matrix and the sources from the mixtures
alone -- "blind" separation, because the mixing matrix \(A\) is unknown.
The key that makes this possible is a striking consequence of the central
limit theorem: since any mixture of independent variables is /more
Gaussian/ than its constituents, the projections of the data that are
/least/ Gaussian must be the original sources. ICA thus separates sources
by maximizing non-Gaussianity -- a criterion PCA's variance-maximization
knows nothing about.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.5.9) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives the mixing model, the independence-versus-uncorrelatedness
distinction, the non-Gaussianity principle and its central-limit-theorem
justification, the whitening step that reduces ICA to finding a rotation,
the FastICA fixed-point iteration =stats::ICA= implements, and the
sign/scale/permutation and Gaussian indeterminacies -- each tied to the
specific method that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by
chunk. §4 reports the three-signal separation, waveform figures, the
head-to-head against PCA, the geometric example, and the Gaussian control.
§5 discusses when ICA is the right tool and where it cannot help. §6
concludes.

* Background: Independent Component Analysis

** Setup and notation: the mixing model

We observe \(n\) samples of \(p\) signals, \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \in
\mathbb{R}^p\) (for the main study, \(p=3\) mixtures over \(n=1000\) time
samples). The generative model is that each observation is an unknown
linear mixture of \(k\) statistically independent latent /sources/,

\[
x = A s, \qquad s = (s_1, \ldots, s_k)^\top \text{ mutually independent},
\]

with \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times k}\) the unknown /mixing matrix/. ICA
seeks an /unmixing matrix/ \(W\) (=unmixing_matrix()=, \(k \times p\)) such
that \(y = W x\) recovers the sources (=sources()=, one column per
recovered component), and reports the estimated mixing matrix
\(A \approx W^{+}\) (=mixing_matrix()=). Here \(k \le p\); the main study
takes \(k = p = 3\).

** Independence is stronger than uncorrelatedness

Two variables are /uncorrelated/ if \(E[s_i s_j] = E[s_i]E[s_j]\) -- a
statement about second moments only. They are /independent/ if their joint
density factorizes, \(p(s_i, s_j) = p(s_i)\,p(s_j)\), which forces /every/
cross-moment and nonlinear relationship to vanish, not just the linear
one. Independence implies uncorrelatedness but not conversely, and that
gap is exactly the room ICA works in: PCA and factor analysis deliver
uncorrelated components and stop, whereas two signals can be perfectly
uncorrelated yet still be mixtures of the same sources -- §4.3 exhibits two
sets of components with /identical/ zero cross-correlation, only one of
which has actually separated the sources.

** Non-Gaussianity and the central limit theorem

Why can maximizing non-Gaussianity recover the sources? Consider any
linear combination \(y = w^\top x = (w^\top A) s = q^\top s\) of the
mixtures: it is a weighted sum of the independent sources. The central
limit theorem says a sum of independent random variables is /more
Gaussian/ than the individual terms -- so \(q^\top s\) is generally more
Gaussian than any single \(s_i\), and becomes /least/ Gaussian precisely
when \(q\) has a single nonzero entry, i.e. when \(y\) equals one source.
Therefore /the maximally non-Gaussian projections of the data are the
original sources/, and ICA reduces to finding the directions of maximal
non-Gaussianity. FastICA measures non-Gaussianity through a smooth
/contrast function/ \(G\) (with derivative \(g\)): =ICAOptions::contrast=
selects among

\[
G_{\text{logcosh}} \Rightarrow g(u) = \tanh u, \quad
G_{\text{exp}} \Rightarrow g(u) = u\,e^{-u^2/2}, \quad
G_{\text{cube}} \Rightarrow g(u) = u^3,
\]

where \(E[G(y)]\) for standardized \(y\) approximates /negentropy/, a
non-negative measure of non-Gaussianity that is zero only for a Gaussian.
The =cube= contrast recovers the classic /excess kurtosis/ criterion; the
=logcosh= default is a robust general-purpose choice. §4.1 reports each
recovered source's excess kurtosis directly.

** Whitening reduces ICA to a rotation

Before the search, =ICA::fit= /centers/ and /whitens/ the data. Writing
\(C\) for the sample covariance of the centered mixtures and \(C = E D
E^\top\) for its eigendecomposition (=linalg::jacobi_eigen=, as in the
[[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and
[[file:factor_analysis_analysis.org][factor analysis]] reports), the
whitening transform

\[
z = E D^{-1/2} E^\top (x - \mu) \quad\text{(the columns of } E D^{-1/2}
\text{ form } K\text{: } z = K^\top (x-\mu))
\]

produces \(z\) with identity covariance -- uncorrelated, unit-variance
coordinates. Whitening is exactly what PCA does, and it accomplishes the
/second-order/ half of ICA for free; crucially, it leaves only an
/orthogonal/ transformation to determine, because any further rotation of
white data stays white. So after whitening, ICA's remaining job is to
find a rotation \(W\) of the whitened data that maximizes non-Gaussianity
-- a search over the compact group of rotations, not all invertible
matrices.

** The FastICA fixed-point iteration

=ICA::fit= finds that rotation one direction at a time by Hyvarinen's
fixed-point iteration. For a unit vector \(w\) (a row of the rotation),
each step is

\[
w^{+} = E\!\left[z\,g(w^\top z)\right] - E\!\left[g'(w^\top z)\right] w,
\qquad w \leftarrow w^{+} / \lVert w^{+} \rVert,
\]

a Newton step on the negentropy contrast that converges /cubically/ near
the solution -- which is why §4.1's components each converge in only a
handful of iterations. To extract several components, =fit= uses
/deflation/: after each new \(w\) converges it is orthogonalized
(Gram-Schmidt) against all previously found directions, so the components
come out mutually orthogonal in the whitened space (hence uncorrelated in
the original space). The unmixing rows are initialized randomly from
=ICAOptions::seed= (making the fit deterministic), and iteration stops
when \(\lvert w^{+}\!\cdot w \rvert\) exceeds \(1 - \texttt{tol}\).

** Indeterminacies, and the one case ICA cannot solve

ICA recovers the sources only up to three unavoidable ambiguities: /sign/
(\(-s_i\) is as independent as \(s_i\)), /scale/ (fixed here by reporting
unit-variance sources), and /permutation/ (the sources come out in no
particular order). None affects the separation itself, and §4 scores
recovery by absolute correlation to absorb sign and permutation. There is
also one genuine /impossibility/: if two or more sources are Gaussian, the
method fails. A whitened vector of independent Gaussians is /rotationally
symmetric/ -- isotropic -- so every rotation looks identically "Gaussian
and independent," non-Gaussianity has no maximum to find, and the sources
are unidentifiable in principle, not merely hard to estimate. ICA
tolerates at most one Gaussian source; §4.5 demonstrates the failure
directly.

* Data and Methods

** The blind-source-separation setup

We generate three deterministic, non-Gaussian source signals over
\(n = 1000\) samples -- a sine, a square wave, and a sawtooth -- each
standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and mix them with a fixed,
non-singular \(3 \times 3\) matrix into three observed traces. The sources
are withheld from the fit and used only afterward to score recovery; ICA
sees nothing but the three mixtures. Deterministic periodic signals make
independence and ground truth unambiguous, and their sub-Gaussian shapes
(negative excess kurtosis) are exactly what the method exploits.

** The geometry experiment

To make the /geometry/ of independence visible, we separately draw two
independent =Uniform= sources (\(n = 2000\)), mix them with a \(2 \times 2\)
matrix, and fit both ICA and PCA. Two independent uniforms fill a square;
a linear mixing shears that square into a parallelogram, whose /edges/ are
the independent directions and whose /diagonal/ is the direction of
greatest variance -- so ICA and PCA should point their axes at visibly
different things.

** The Gaussian control

To exhibit §2.6's impossibility, we repeat the three-signal setup with the
three sources replaced by independent /Gaussian/ noise, mix identically,
and fit ICA and PCA. Here the theory predicts ICA can do no better than
PCA, and we report both to check.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::ICA= for the model (and
=stats::PCA= for the comparisons), and =plot::RPlot= for every figure. No
external statistics package is used.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** Includes, usings, and scoring helpers

Helpers used only to /score/ the fits after the fact, never as inputs to
=ICA=: an absolute Pearson correlation (sign is irrelevant to ICA
recovery), a standardizer, and a "mean best-match recovery" that averages,
over the true sources, each one's best absolute correlation with any
recovered component.

// Absolute Pearson correlation of two equal-length vectors (sign is irrelevant for ICA recovery).
static double abscorr(const std::vector<double>& a, const std::vector<double>& b) {
    const std::size_t n = a.size();
    double ma = 0.0, mb = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) { ma += a[i]; mb += b[i]; }
    ma /= n; mb /= n;
    double num = 0.0, da = 0.0, db = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        const double xa = a[i] - ma, xb = b[i] - mb;
        num += xa * xb; da += xa * xa; db += xb * xb;
    }
    return std::abs(num / std::sqrt(da * db));
}

static void standardize(std::vector<double>& v) {
    const std::size_t n = v.size();
    double m = 0.0;
    for (double x : v) m += x;
    m /= n;
    double ss = 0.0;
    for (double x : v) ss += (x - m) * (x - m);
    const double sd = std::sqrt(ss / (n - 1));
    for (double& x : v) x = (x - m) / (sd > 0.0 ? sd : 1.0);
}

// For each recovered component, its best absolute correlation with ANY true source, and vice
// versa; the mean of the per-true-source best matches summarizes overall recovery in [0, 1].
static double mean_best_recovery(const std::vector<std::vector<double>>& truth,
                                 const std::vector<std::vector<double>>& est) {
    double acc = 0.0;
    for (const auto& s : truth) {
        double best = 0.0;
        for (const auto& e : est) best = std::max(best, abscorr(s, e));
        acc += best;
    }
    return acc / static_cast<double>(truth.size());
}

#+name: ica-bss-gen
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "=== Blind source separation: 3 independent signals mixed together ===\n";
    const std::size_t n = 1000;
    std::vector<double> s1(n), s2(n), s3(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        const double t = static_cast<double>(i);
        s1[i] = std::sin(0.060 * t);                          // sine
        s2[i] = (std::sin(0.093 * t) >= 0.0) ? 1.0 : -1.0;     // square wave
        s3[i] = std::fmod(0.050 * t, 2.0) - 1.0;               // sawtooth
    }
    standardize(s1); standardize(s2); standardize(s3);
    const std::vector<std::vector<double>> sources_true{s1, s2, s3};
    // A fixed, non-singular 3x3 mixing matrix.
    const double A[3][3] = {{0.80, 0.30, 0.20}, {0.40, 0.90, 0.50}, {0.60, 0.20, 0.70}};
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> mix(3, std::vector<double>(n, 0.0));
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
        for (std::size_t r = 0; r < 3; ++r)
            mix[r][i] = A[r][0] * s1[i] + A[r][1] * s2[i] + A[r][2] * s3[i];
    DataFrame mixed{{"x1", DataFrame::numeric_column_type(mix[0])},
                    {"x2", DataFrame::numeric_column_type(mix[1])},
                    {"x3", DataFrame::numeric_column_type(mix[2])}};
    const std::vector<std::string> mfeat{"x1", "x2", "x3"};
    std::cout << "generated " << n << " samples of 3 sources, mixed into 3 observed signals\n";

        ICA gica(gmixed, mfeat, ICAOptions{3, "logcosh", 200, 1e-6, 42});
        PCA gpca(gmixed, mfeat, PCAOptions{true, false});
        std::vector<std::vector<double>> gest{gica.component(0), gica.component(1), gica.component(2)};
        std::vector<std::vector<double>> gpcs{gpca.component_scores(0), gpca.component_scores(1),
                                              gpca.component_scores(2)};
        std::cout << "mean best-match |corr| to true sources -- ICA: " << mean_best_recovery(gtruth, gest)
                  << ", PCA: " << mean_best_recovery(gtruth, gpcs) << "\n";
        std::cout << "(for the non-Gaussian signals ICA reached 0.9999 vs PCA 0.7625; here ICA's\n"
                  << " advantage over PCA has vanished -- Gaussian sources are unidentifiable)\n";
    }
#+end_src

** ICA versus PCA on the identical mixtures

#+begin_example
mean best-match |corr| to true sources -- ICA: 0.9999, PCA: 0.7625
max |corr| between distinct components -- ICA: 0.0000, PCA: 0.0000 (both decorrelated by construction)
#+end_example

This is the report's central contrast. Given the /same three mixtures/,
ICA recovers the sources at 0.9999 while PCA reaches only 0.7625 -- PCA's
components are still blends, because rotating to maximal /variance/ has no
reason to land on the individual sources. The second line is the crux:
/both/ methods produce components with a maximum pairwise correlation of
0.0000 -- both are perfectly decorrelated. Decorrelation is therefore
demonstrably /not/ what separates the sources; the entire difference
between 0.7625 and 0.9999 lives in the higher-order dependence that PCA's
second-order machinery cannot detect and ICA's non-Gaussianity criterion
can. This is §2.2 made empirical: uncorrelated is a strictly weaker
property than independent, and only the latter solves the problem.

** The geometry of independence

#+begin_example
mean best-match |corr| to the uniform sources -- ICA: 0.9996, PCA: 0.7236
#+end_example

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Scatter of two mixed uniform sources with ICA and PCA axes overlaid :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/ica_geometry.svg]]

Two independent uniform sources fill a square; the linear mixing shears it
into the parallelogram of gray points. The overlaid axes tell the whole
story: the /ICA/ directions (red) lie along the parallelogram's /edges/ --
the true independent source directions, recovered at 0.9996 -- while the
/PCA/ directions (blue) lie along the cloud's axes of greatest and least
/variance/, the long diagonal and its perpendicular, recovered at only
0.7236. PCA is not wrong; it is answering a different question (where is
the variance?) whose answer, for a mixture, simply is not "where are the
sources?" The picture is the geometric shadow of §4.3's numbers: two
orthogonal blue axes that decorrelate but do not separate, two oblique red
axes that do.

** The one case ICA cannot solve: Gaussian sources

#+begin_example
mean best-match |corr| to true sources -- ICA: 0.8098, PCA: 0.7591
(for the non-Gaussian signals ICA reached 0.9999 vs PCA 0.7625; here ICA's
 advantage over PCA has vanished -- Gaussian sources are unidentifiable)
#+end_example

Replacing the three structured signals with independent /Gaussian/ noise
and mixing identically, ICA's recovery collapses to 0.8098 -- statistically
tied with PCA's 0.7591, and a world away from the 0.9999 it achieved on the
non-Gaussian sources. This is not an implementation weakness but the exact
impossibility §2.6 predicts: a whitened vector of independent Gaussians is
rotationally symmetric, so /every/ rotation is equally independent and
equally Gaussian, non-Gaussianity has no maximum to climb toward, and the
sources cannot be identified even in principle. The honest summary is that
ICA's entire power comes from non-Gaussianity, and it disappears precisely
when that resource does -- the reason ICA tolerates at most one Gaussian
source among its inputs.

* Discussion

**Decorrelation and independence are different goals, and only one is
source separation.** §4.3 is the argument in two numbers: PCA and ICA both
produced perfectly uncorrelated components (0.0000 cross-correlation), yet
PCA recovered the sources at 0.7625 and ICA at 0.9999. Everything that
distinguishes them lives above second order, invisible to a
covariance-based method. This is the same lesson the
[[file:factor_analysis_analysis.org][factor analysis report]] gestures at
from the other side -- factor analysis, like PCA, is a second-order method
and would separate these sources no better -- and it marks ICA as the
qualitatively different member of this project's latent-variable family:
the only one that uses the /shape/ of the data's distribution, not merely
its covariance.

**Non-Gaussianity is both the engine and the limit.** The central limit
theorem is what licenses the whole method (§2.3): mixing makes signals more
Gaussian, so de-mixing means chasing non-Gaussianity. §4.1's negative
kurtosis values are that engine running -- the recovered components are as
far from Gaussian as their sine/square/sawtooth shapes allow. And §4.5 is
the same engine stalling: strip the non-Gaussianity by using Gaussian
sources and ICA has nothing to optimize, falling back to PCA's level. A
practitioner's takeaway is concrete: ICA is the right tool when the sources
are believed non-Gaussian (audio, EEG/MEG, natural images, many financial
and biomedical signals all qualify), and the wrong tool when they are not.

**Whitening does the easy half; the fixed-point does the hard half.**
§2.4-2.5 split the work in a way worth remembering: PCA-whitening (a plain
eigendecomposition) solves the second-order part and reduces ICA to finding
a /rotation/, and the FastICA fixed-point (a few cheap Newton steps per
component, §4.1's two-to-seven iterations) solves the remaining
higher-order part. The efficiency of the second stage is why FastICA is a
workhorse: once the data is whitened, each additional independent component
costs only a handful of iterations and one Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization.

* Conclusion

Implementing the deflationary FastICA algorithm in =datamunge='s C++
=stats::ICA= and applying it to three independent signals -- a sine, a
square wave, and a sawtooth -- mixed into three anonymous traces, we
recover all three sources from the mixtures alone at a mean correlation of
0.9999, with every component converging in at most seven iterations. On the
identical mixtures PCA reaches only 0.7625, and the decisive detail is that
both methods produce perfectly uncorrelated components (0.0000
cross-correlation): the separation comes entirely from the higher-order,
non-Gaussian structure ICA exploits and PCA cannot see. A geometric example
makes this visible -- ICA's axes track a mixture parallelogram's
independent edges (0.9996) while PCA's track its variance (0.7236) -- and a
Gaussian-source control confirms the method's one principled limit, where
ICA's recovery (0.8098) collapses to PCA's (0.7591) exactly because
Gaussian sources are unidentifiable. As with its companions, this entire
report is reproducible end to end via =just org-example ica_analysis=,
which re-executes §3.5.9's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
