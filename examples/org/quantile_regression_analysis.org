#+begin_abstract
Ordinary least squares describes how the conditional /mean/ of a response
changes with predictors. Quantile regression asks the richer question:
how do the lower tail, median, and upper tail change? We implement linear
quantile regression in =datamunge::stats::QuantileRegression= and exercise
it as a literate C++ program. The implementation minimizes the convex,
nondifferentiable check (pinball) loss with the alternating direction
method of multipliers (ADMM), decomposing each iteration into one
QR-based least-squares update and an exact asymmetric soft-thresholding
update. Three analyses show why that matters: synthetic heteroskedastic
data produce genuinely different lower/median/upper slopes; median
regression remains stable when a single extreme outlier pulls OLS away;
and 10th, 50th, and 90th percentile models of Palmer penguin body mass
show how a predictor can affect the shape of a real conditional
distribution rather than only its center.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

A regression line is often described as "the relationship" between \(x\)
and \(y\), but an OLS line is only the conditional mean
\(\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]\). Two populations can have the same mean and
radically different tails, asymmetry, or spread. In risk analysis the
upper tail may be the estimand; in growth studies the lower tail may be
the vulnerable population; and under heteroskedasticity a single mean
line deliberately erases how variability changes with \(x\).

Quantile regression, introduced by Koenker and Bassett (1978), estimates
the conditional quantile

\[
Q_Y(\tau\mid x)=\inf\{y:\Pr(Y\le y\mid X=x)\ge\tau\},
\qquad 0<\tau<1.
\]

The special case \(\tau=0.5\) is median regression. Fitting several
values of \(\tau\) traces the conditional distribution without assuming
Gaussian, constant-variance errors.

This document is a /literate program/: the code fragments are woven into
one C++ program, compiled, and executed during export. The figures and
numerical diagnostics below are produced by that program.

* Mathematics

** From a sample quantile to regression

The sample mean minimizes squared error. Analogously, a sample
\(\tau\)-quantile minimizes the asymmetric absolute-error objective

\[
\hat q_\tau=\arg\min_q\sum_{i=1}^n\rho_\tau(y_i-q),
\]

where the /check loss/ is

\[
\rho_\tau(r)=r\bigl(\tau-\mathbf 1\{r<0\}\bigr)
=
\begin{cases}
\tau r, & r\ge0,\\
(\tau-1)r, & r<0.
\end{cases}
\]

Positive residuals cost \(\tau\) per unit and negative residuals cost
\(1-\tau\) per unit. At \(\tau=0.9\), underprediction is nine times as
expensive as overprediction, pushing the fitted value to the 90th
percentile. At \(\tau=0.5\), the objective is one half of absolute error,
so the solution is a median.

Replacing the constant \(q\) by a linear predictor \(x_i^\top\beta\)
gives linear quantile regression:

\[
\hat\beta_\tau
=\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb R^p}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\rho_\tau(y_i-x_i^\top\beta).
\]

No Gaussian likelihood or constant variance is required. The linearity
assumption applies to the chosen conditional quantile, not necessarily
to the conditional mean.

** Why ordinary smooth optimization is a poor fit

The check loss is convex but has a kink at zero. Its subgradient with
respect to a residual is \(\tau\) above zero, \(\tau-1\) below zero, and
the interval \([\tau-1,\tau]\) at zero. A smooth optimizer can approximate
that kink, but doing so changes the problem and introduces a smoothing
parameter. The classical alternative is a linear program. The
implementation here uses ADMM, which preserves the exact objective while
reducing every iteration to operations already native to =datamunge='s
dense linear algebra.

** ADMM splitting

Introduce a residual vector \(r=y-X\beta\):

\[
\min_{\beta,r}\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau(r_i)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad y-X\beta-r=0.
\]

For penalty \(\varrho>0\) and scaled dual variable \(u\), ADMM alternates

\[
\begin{aligned}
\beta^{k+1}
&=\arg\min_\beta\|y-r^k+u^k-X\beta\|_2^2,\\
r^{k+1}
&=\operatorname{prox}_{\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau/\varrho}
  (y-X\beta^{k+1}+u^k),\\
u^{k+1}&=u^k+y-X\beta^{k+1}-r^{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The first line is least squares. =QuantileRegression= factorizes \(X\)
with Householder QR once, then reuses that factorization for every
right-hand side. The second line separates by observation and has the
closed form asymmetric soft threshold

\[
\operatorname{prox}(v_i)=
\begin{cases}
v_i-w_i\tau/\varrho,&v_i>w_i\tau/\varrho,\\
v_i+w_i(1-\tau)/\varrho,&v_i<-w_i(1-\tau)/\varrho,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

** Pseudocode

#+begin_example
input: design X, response y, quantile tau, weights w, penalty rho
check 0 < tau < 1, rho > 0, and that X has full column rank
factor X = QR once
initialize beta with least squares; r = 0; u = 0

repeat until the primal and dual residual tests pass:
    beta = least_squares_QR(X, y - r + u)
    v = y - X beta + u
    for each observation i:
        if v[i] > w[i] * tau / rho:
            r[i] = v[i] - w[i] * tau / rho
        else if v[i] < -w[i] * (1 - tau) / rho:
            r[i] = v[i] + w[i] * (1 - tau) / rho
        else:
            r[i] = 0
    u = u + y - X beta - r

return beta, fitted values, residuals, check loss, convergence diagnostics
#+end_example

The primal residual measures violation of \(y-X\beta-r=0\). The dual
residual is \(\varrho X^\top(r^k-r^{k-1})\), the change in the split
residual projected into coefficient space. Absolute and relative
tolerances are used for both, following standard ADMM stopping rules.

** Goodness of fit

Squared-error \(R^2\) is tied to a mean model and is inappropriate here.
We report Koenker--Machado pseudo-\(R^2\):

\[
R^1_\tau
=1-\frac{\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau(y_i-x_i^\top\hat\beta_\tau)}
{\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau(y_i-\hat q_\tau)}.
\]

The denominator is the check loss of an intercept-only model at the same
quantile. It measures improvement over that baseline, but it should not
be interpreted as a fraction of variance explained.

* Program

** Includes and helpers

#+name: qr-includes
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
#include <datamunge/datasets/datasets.hpp>
#include <datamunge/dstruct/dstruct.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>
#include <datamunge/stats/lm.hpp>
#include <datamunge/stats/quantile_regression.hpp>

#include <algorithm>
#include <array>
#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>
#include <string>
#include <vector>

using datamunge::dstruct::DataFrame;
using datamunge::plot::RPlot;
using datamunge::stats::LM;
using datamunge::stats::QuantileRegression;
using datamunge::stats::QuantileRegressionOptions;

QuantileRegression fit_quantile(const DataFrame& data,
                                const std::string& formula,
                                double tau) {
    QuantileRegressionOptions options;
    options.quantile = tau;
    // The response is measured in grams in the real-data analysis; a smaller
    // fixed penalty is a better scale match than the library's neutral default.
    options.rho = 0.1;
    options.max_iterations = 20000;
    // Report-scale fits do not need machine-precision optimization. This
    // standard ADMM tolerance retains stable displayed coefficients while
    // avoiding thousands of iterations at the nonsmooth optimum.
    options.absolute_tolerance = 1e-5;
    options.relative_tolerance = 1e-3;
    return QuantileRegression(data, formula, options);
}

void print_fit(const std::string& label, const QuantileRegression& model) {
    std::cout << label << ": beta = [";
    for (std::size_t j = 0; j < model.coefficients().size(); ++j)
        std::cout << (j ? ", " : "") << model.coefficients()[j];
    std::cout << "], loss = " << model.objective()
              << ", pseudo-R1 = " << model.pseudo_r_squared()
              << ", iterations = " << model.iterations()
              << ", converged = " << (model.converged() ? "yes" : "no")
              << ", primal residual = " << model.primal_residual_norm()
              << ", dual residual = " << model.dual_residual_norm()
              << "\n";
}
#+end_src

** Analysis 1: heteroskedastic synthetic data

The first dataset is deterministic so the report is reproducible. Its
center follows \(10+2x\), while deviations are multiplied by
\(1+0.12x\). The spread therefore fans out with \(x\). If quantile
regression is doing more than moving a mean line vertically, the lower
quantile should have a shallower slope and the upper quantile a steeper
one.

#+name: qr-heteroskedastic
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
const auto make_heteroskedastic_data = [] {
    std::vector<double> x, y;
    const std::array<double, 9> pattern{
        -1.8, -1.1, -0.6, -0.2, 0.0, 0.2, 0.6, 1.1, 1.8
    };
    for (int xi = 0; xi <= 40; ++xi) {
        for (const double z : pattern) {
            x.push_back(static_cast<double>(xi));
            y.push_back(10.0 + 2.0 * xi + (1.0 + 0.12 * xi) * z);
        }
    }
    DataFrame data;
    data.add_column("x", x);
    data.add_column("y", y);
    return data;
};

const auto hetero = make_heteroskedastic_data();
const auto q10 = fit_quantile(hetero, "y ~ x", 0.10);
const auto q50 = fit_quantile(hetero, "y ~ x", 0.50);
const auto q90 = fit_quantile(hetero, "y ~ x", 0.90);
print_fit("tau=0.10", q10);
print_fit("tau=0.50", q50);
print_fit("tau=0.90", q90);

{
    const auto& xs = hetero.numeric_column("x").values();
    const auto& ys = hetero.numeric_column("y").values();
    auto plot = RPlot::create();
    plot.points(xs, ys, "observations", {148, 163, 184}, 2.5);
    const std::vector<double> grid{0.0, 40.0};
    const auto line = [&](const QuantileRegression& model) {
        return std::vector<double>{
            model.coefficients()[0],
            model.coefficients()[0] + 40.0 * model.coefficients()[1]
        };
    };
    plot.line(grid, line(q10), "tau = 0.10", {37, 99, 235}, 2.2);
    plot.lines(grid, line(q50), "tau = 0.50", {22, 163, 74}, 2.2);
    plot.lines(grid, line(q90), "tau = 0.90", {220, 38, 38}, 2.2);
    plot.legend({"tau = 0.10", "tau = 0.50", "tau = 0.90"},
                {{37, 99, 235}, {22, 163, 74}, {220, 38, 38}});
    plot.title("Heteroskedastic data: conditional quantile lines")
        .x_label("x").y_label("y");
    plot.save_svg("build/org/quantile_heteroskedastic.svg");
}
#+end_src

** Analysis 2: resistance to an extreme response

Absolute loss gives median regression bounded response influence in a
way squared loss does not. We fit a nearly exact line, replace one
response with an extreme value, and compare slopes. This is not a claim
that quantile regression is immune to leverage points in predictor
space; the contamination here is specifically vertical.

#+name: qr-outlier
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
DataFrame outlier_data;
std::vector<double> outlier_x, outlier_y;
for (int i = 0; i <= 20; ++i) {
    outlier_x.push_back(i);
    outlier_y.push_back(5.0 + 1.5 * i + ((i % 3) - 1) * 0.2);
}
outlier_y.back() = 250.0;
outlier_data.add_column("x", outlier_x);
outlier_data.add_column("y", outlier_y);

const LM outlier_ols(outlier_data, "y ~ x");
const auto outlier_median = fit_quantile(outlier_data, "y ~ x", 0.50);
std::cout << "outlier OLS:    beta = [" << outlier_ols.coefficients()[0]
          << ", " << outlier_ols.coefficients()[1] << "]\n";
print_fit("outlier median", outlier_median);
#+end_src

** Analysis 3: Palmer penguin body-mass quantiles

The real-data analysis models =body_mass_g= from =flipper_length_mm= and
=species=. The formula engine applies the same null omission and
alphabetical-reference dummy encoding used by =LM=. Separate 10th,
median, and 90th percentile fits show how the flipper-length association
and species shifts vary across the conditional body-mass distribution.

#+name: qr-penguins
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
const auto penguins = datamunge::datasets::penguins();
const std::string penguin_formula =
    "body_mass_g ~ flipper_length_mm + species";
const auto penguin_q10 = fit_quantile(penguins, penguin_formula, 0.10);
const auto penguin_q50 = fit_quantile(penguins, penguin_formula, 0.50);
const auto penguin_q90 = fit_quantile(penguins, penguin_formula, 0.90);
print_fit("penguins tau=0.10", penguin_q10);
print_fit("penguins tau=0.50", penguin_q50);
print_fit("penguins tau=0.90", penguin_q90);

{
    DataFrame grid;
    std::vector<double> flipper;
    std::vector<std::string> species;
    for (int length = 170; length <= 230; ++length) {
        flipper.push_back(length);
        species.push_back("Adelie");
    }
    grid.add_column("flipper_length_mm", flipper);
    grid.add_column("species", species);

    const auto p10 = penguin_q10.predict(grid);
    const auto p50 = penguin_q50.predict(grid);
    const auto p90 = penguin_q90.predict(grid);
    auto plot = RPlot::create();
    plot.line(flipper, p10, "tau = 0.10", {37, 99, 235}, 2.2);
    plot.lines(flipper, p50, "tau = 0.50", {22, 163, 74}, 2.2);
    plot.lines(flipper, p90, "tau = 0.90", {220, 38, 38}, 2.2);
    plot.legend({"tau = 0.10", "tau = 0.50", "tau = 0.90"},
                {{37, 99, 235}, {22, 163, 74}, {220, 38, 38}});
    plot.title("Adelie body-mass quantiles vs. flipper length")
        .x_label("flipper length (mm)").y_label("conditional body mass (g)");
    plot.save_svg("build/org/quantile_penguins.svg");
}

* Results and interpretation

** Heteroskedasticity changes slopes, not only intercepts

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Synthetic fan-shaped data with fitted 10th, 50th, and 90th percentile regression lines :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/quantile_heteroskedastic.svg]]

The three lines spread apart as \(x\) increases. That geometry is the
central result: a mean model could describe the center, and a
heteroskedastic standard-error correction could repair inference about
that mean, but neither would estimate these distinct tail relationships.
The median line follows the constructed center near slope \(2\); the
lower slope is smaller and the upper slope larger.

** Median regression resists a vertical outlier

The uncontaminated slope is \(1.5\). Because OLS squares the final
observation's enormous residual, it rotates toward that point. Median
regression pays only linearly for the same residual and remains close to
the relationship supported by the other twenty observations. This
robustness is a consequence of the objective, not an after-the-fact
diagnostic adjustment.

** Real conditional body-mass distributions

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Fitted 10th, median, and 90th percentile Adelie penguin body-mass curves across flipper length :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/quantile_penguins.svg]]

The vertical distance between the curves is an estimated conditional
spread for Adelie penguins. Nonparallel curves mean that spread changes
with flipper length. The categorical coefficients should be interpreted
at a fixed flipper length and relative to the formula engine's Adelie
reference level; fitting each quantile separately allows those species
shifts to differ across the distribution.

* Practical considerations

- Quantile regression estimates conditional quantiles. They are not
  obtained by taking quantiles of OLS predictions.
- Coefficients at different \(\tau\) values may cross, especially with
  small samples or extrapolation. Independent linear fits do not impose
  global noncrossing constraints.
- The current API reports point estimates and optimization diagnostics,
  not coefficient standard errors. Bootstrap inference is a natural
  extension because the check loss is nondifferentiable and analytic
  sandwich estimators require estimating the conditional density near
  zero residual.
- ADMM's \(\varrho\) affects convergence speed, not the optimum. Fixed
  penalties are reproducible and remain the default; optional residual
  balancing can adapt the penalty on harder problems. =rho=,
  =adaptive_rho=, iteration limits, and tolerances are exposed through
  =QuantileRegressionOptions=.
- The design must have full column rank. As in =LM=, categorical
  predictors and formula transformations are resolved before fitting.

* Conclusion

=QuantileRegression= adds a distribution-aware regression primitive to
the canonical C++ core. Its formula interface matches the existing model
layer, while its solver respects the exact convex, nonsmooth pinball
objective. The examples demonstrate three distinct uses: discovering
heterogeneous effects hidden by a mean, obtaining a response-outlier-
resistant median relationship, and describing lower/central/upper
patterns in real data. The main conceptual shift is simple but powerful:
there need not be one regression line when the scientific question is
about an entire conditional distribution.
