#+begin_abstract
How many configurations of a system have each possible energy? That count -- the *density of states*
\(g(E)\) -- determines all of a system's thermodynamics, but ordinary Monte Carlo, which samples with
Boltzmann weight, almost never visits the rare high- or low-energy states. The *Wang-Landau* algorithm
(2001) estimates \(g(E)\) directly by performing a random walk that *flattens its own energy histogram*,
building \(g\) on the fly. This report drives =datamunge='s =algorithms::wang_landau_dos=, verified to
recover an exactly-known binomial density of states, and shows the estimate matching the truth.
#+end_abstract

* A random walk that flattens its histogram

The idea is to sample configurations with probability proportional to \(1/g(E)\), which makes every
energy equally likely -- a *flat* energy histogram. Since \(g\) is unknown, Wang-Landau *learns* it while
walking. It proposes a move (here, flipping one spin) and accepts it with

\[ P_{\text{acc}}=\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{g(E_{\text{old}})}{g(E_{\text{new}})}\right), \]

using the *current estimate* of \(g\). After every step it multiplies \(g(E)\) at the current energy by a
modification factor \(f>1\) (added in the log domain) and increments a histogram bin. This self-correcting
bias pushes the walk away from energies it has already visited often. Once the histogram is *flat* --
every bin within a fraction of the mean -- the factor is reduced, \(f\to\sqrt f\), the histogram is
reset, and the walk continues with finer updates. As \(f\to1\), \(g(E)\) converges to the true density of
states (up to an overall normalization).

For a toy system of \(N\) spins whose energy is the number of up-spins, the exact answer is the binomial
coefficient \(g(E)=\binom{N}{E}\), giving a clean target: \(\log g(E)=\log\binom{N}{E}\). Wang-Landau
recovers it without ever enumerating the \(2^N\) configurations -- the reason it is a standard tool for
the thermodynamics of spin systems, polymers, and other models with rugged energy landscapes.

* Algorithm

#+begin_example
WANG-LANDAU(N):
  log g[E] = 0;  histogram H[E] = 0;  log_f = 1
  while log_f > threshold:
      repeat until H is flat:
          propose a spin flip: E -> E'
          accept with prob min(1, exp(log g[E] - log g[E']))
          log g[current E] += log_f;  H[current E] += 1
      log_f /= 2                          # f -> sqrt(f)
      reset H
  return log g normalized so log g(0) = 0
#+end_example

* Worked example: recovering a binomial density of states

We run Wang-Landau on \(N=30\) spins and compare the estimated \(\log g(E)\) against the exact
\(\log\binom{N}{E}\).

#+name: wl-run
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    const int  N    = 30;
    const auto logg = wang_landau_dos(N, 0.85, 1e-6, 1);

    std::cout << "=== Wang-Landau density of states (N = " << N << ") ===\n";
    std::cout << "  E    WL log g(E)   exact log C(N,E)   |diff|\n";
    double maxerr = 0;
    std::vector<double> es, wl, exact;
    for (int E = 0; E <= N; ++E) {
        const double ex = std::lgamma(N + 1) - std::lgamma(E + 1) - std::lgamma(N - E + 1);
        maxerr = std::max(maxerr, std::fabs(logg[E] - ex));
        es.push_back(E); wl.push_back(logg[E]); exact.push_back(ex);
        if (E % 5 == 0) std::printf("  %2d    %9.4f     %9.4f      %.4f\n", E, logg[E], ex, std::fabs(logg[E] - ex));
    }
    std::printf("max |WL - exact| over all E: %.4f\n", maxerr);

    auto plot = RPlot::create();
    plot.line(es, exact, "exact", RGB{30, 30, 30}, 2.5);
    plot.points(es, wl, "wl", RGB{37, 99, 235}, 5.0);
    plot.legend({"exact log C(N,E)", "Wang-Landau estimate"}, {{30, 30, 30}, {37, 99, 235}});
    plot.title("Wang-Landau recovers the exact log density of states")
        .x_label("energy E (up-spins)").y_label("log g(E)").background({250, 250, 252});
    plot.save_svg("build/org/wang_landau.svg");
    std::cout << "\nwrote wang_landau.svg\n";
#+end_src

The estimated \(\log g(E)\) tracks the exact binomial curve across all energies to a few tenths -- the
residual statistical error left by a finite final modification factor -- without ever enumerating the
\(2^{30}\) configurations.

The blue Wang-Landau points sit right on the black exact-density curve, peaked at the middle energy where
the most configurations live. Learning that entire curve from a single self-adjusting random walk -- with
no prior knowledge of where the states are -- is what makes Wang-Landau sampling so powerful for systems
whose energy landscape is too rugged for ordinary Monte Carlo to explore.
