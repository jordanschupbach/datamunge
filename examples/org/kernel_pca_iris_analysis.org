#+begin_abstract
We fit Kernel Principal Component Analysis (Scholkopf, Smola & Muller,
1998) to Fisher's (1936) iris measurements and to a synthetic
two-concentric-circles dataset using =datamunge='s C++ =stats::KernelPCA=,
and report it as a literate program: every fragment below is a real,
compiled, executed chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every
number and figure in this document. =Species= is withheld entirely from
the fit. Kernel PCA generalizes ordinary PCA by eigendecomposing the
centered \(n \times n\) kernel (Gram) matrix rather than the \(p \times
p\) covariance matrix, so it can capture nonlinear structure that plain
PCA -- a strictly linear method -- cannot. We first verify the theory
empirically: with a /linear/ kernel, =KernelPCA= reproduces plain
(unscaled) PCA's scores to within double-precision round-off (maximum
coordinate difference 0.0000 across all 150 points and both components),
and its centered-kernel eigenvalues equal PCA's covariance eigenvalues
scaled by \((n-1)\) exactly (630.0080 and 36.1579). Switching to a
Gaussian /RBF/ kernel then improves iris species separation over the
linear kernel (a between/within embedded-distance ratio of 4.3087 at
\(\gamma=0.13\), versus 3.8975 linear and 3.0091 for a cubic polynomial
kernel), and a \(\gamma\) sweep shows this ratio is single-peaked, best
near \(\gamma=0.05\) (4.4861) and collapsing to 1.6870 by \(\gamma=10\)
as the kernel narrows to near-isolation. The report's central result is
the concentric-circles experiment, the canonical demonstration of /why/
a kernel method exists: two rings that are not linearly separable at all
(no single linear axis, and therefore no plain-PCA component, splits
them better than 73% accuracy) become /perfectly/ separable -- 100%
accuracy on the first RBF kernel principal component alone at
\(\gamma=0.3\). That success is not automatic: the same circles sweep
shows accuracy is sharply non-monotonic in \(\gamma\) (0.69, 0.68, 1.00,
0.75, 0.77, 0.83 across \(\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10\}\)),
a concrete illustration that the kernel bandwidth is the parameter that
decides whether kernel PCA succeeds. We derive the feature-space
covariance, the kernel trick, the double-centering of the Gram matrix,
the eigendecomposition and its \(\sqrt{\lambda}\)-scaled embedding, and
the linear-kernel/PCA equivalence, tying each to the corresponding
library call.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

This project's [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA companion report]] asks
for the directions of greatest variation in a dataset and answers with an
eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix -- a strictly /linear/
construction: every principal component is a linear combination of the
original features, and no amount of them can bend a straight axis into a
curved one. That is fine for iris, whose four measurements are close to
linearly structured, but it fails completely on data whose interesting
structure is curved. The classic example -- two concentric rings, where
the meaningful coordinate is /radius/, a quadratic function of the inputs
-- has no linear axis that separates the rings at all.

Kernel PCA (Scholkopf, Smola & Muller, 1998) is the standard fix. It
runs ordinary PCA not on the raw features but on their images under a
(possibly infinite-dimensional) nonlinear feature map \(\phi\), without
ever forming \(\phi\) explicitly: every computation PCA needs turns out
to depend on the data only through /inner products/ \(\langle \phi(x_i),
\phi(x_j) \rangle\), which a /kernel function/ \(k(x_i, x_j)\) supplies
directly. This "kernel trick" replaces the \(p \times p\) covariance
eigenproblem with an \(n \times n\) kernel-matrix eigenproblem, and lets a
linear method operate in a nonlinear feature space -- the same trick that
turns a linear separator into the nonlinear
[[file:svm_iris_analysis.org][support vector machine]] this project also
implements.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.4.12) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward
is that program's literal output.

§2 derives the feature-space covariance, the kernel trick, the
double-centering of the Gram matrix, the eigendecomposition and its
embedding, and the linear-kernel/PCA equivalence, each tied to the
specific =KernelPCA= method that implements it. §3 describes the program
chunk by chunk. §4 first confirms the linear-kernel equivalence
empirically, then reports the RBF and polynomial fits on iris, a species
separation comparison, a \(\gamma\)-sensitivity sweep, and -- on the
concentric circles -- the linear-versus-RBF separation experiment that
is this report's central result. §5 discusses what the kernel trick buys
and what choosing \(\gamma\) costs. §6 concludes.

* Background: Kernel Principal Component Analysis

** Setup and notation

\(n\) observations \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^p\) -- for iris,
\(n=150\), \(p=4\) (=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=,
=Petal.Width=), with =Species= withheld from fitting exactly as in the
[[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and other companion reports; for the
circles, \(n=300\) synthetic 2D points described in §3.2. As in plain
PCA, no response variable is used during fitting; the class labels are
retained separately and used only afterward, to color figures and to
measure separation.

** From PCA to feature-space PCA: the kernel trick

Plain PCA diagonalizes the \(p \times p\) sample covariance
\(C = \tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(x_i - \bar{x})^\top\). Kernel
PCA does exactly the same thing, but to the images
\(\phi(x_1), \ldots, \phi(x_n)\) of the data under a nonlinear feature
map \(\phi : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathcal{H}\) into some (possibly
infinite-dimensional) feature space \(\mathcal{H}\). The obstacle is that
the feature-space covariance is a (possibly infinite) \(\dim\mathcal{H}
\times \dim\mathcal{H}\) operator that we cannot form directly. The way
around it is the observation that every principal eigenvector of that
covariance must lie in the span of the centered data images, so it can
be written \(v = \sum_i \alpha_i \, \phi(x_i)\), and substituting this
into the eigenproblem collapses every appearance of \(\phi\) into
/inner products/ \(\langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle\) alone. A
/kernel function/

\[
k(x_i, x_j) = \langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle
\]

supplies exactly those inner products without ever evaluating \(\phi\).
=KernelPCAOptions::kernel= selects one of three standard kernels, each
computed elementwise in =KernelPCA::fit=:

\[
k_{\text{lin}}(x,y) = x^\top y, \quad
k_{\text{rbf}}(x,y) = e^{-\gamma \lVert x - y \rVert^2}, \quad
k_{\text{poly}}(x,y) = (\gamma \, x^\top y + c_0)^{d},
\]

with \(\gamma\) =gamma=, \(d\) =degree=, and \(c_0\) =coef0=. The RBF
kernel corresponds to an /infinite/-dimensional \(\phi\), which is
precisely why kernel PCA can express structure no finite linear basis
could. Collecting all pairwise kernel values gives the \(n \times n\)
symmetric /Gram matrix/ \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\), which =fit= builds in a
single symmetric double loop.

** Centering in feature space

PCA requires centered data, and the feature-space mean
\(\bar{\phi} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \phi(x_i)\) is just as inaccessible as
\(\phi\) itself -- but the Gram matrix of the /centered/ images can still
be written using \(K\) alone. With \(\tilde{\phi}(x_i) = \phi(x_i) -
\bar{\phi}\), the centered kernel matrix is

\[
\tilde{K} = K - \mathbf{1}_n K - K \mathbf{1}_n + \mathbf{1}_n K \mathbf{1}_n,
\qquad (\mathbf{1}_n)_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{n},
\]

which expands to the elementwise form =fit= actually computes,

\[
\tilde{K}_{ij} = K_{ij} - r_i - r_j + g, \qquad
r_i = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j} K_{ij}, \quad
g = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j} K_{ij},
\]

with \(r_i\) the =row_mean= vector and \(g\) the =grand_mean= scalar in
the source. This double-centering is the kernel-space analogue of
subtracting the column means in plain PCA, and it is why =KernelPCA=
needs no separate =center= option: centering the Gram matrix /is/
centering the (unformed) feature vectors.

** Eigendecomposition and the embedding

\(\tilde{K}\) is symmetric and positive semi-definite, so it has a real
eigendecomposition \(\tilde{K} = \sum_c \lambda_c \, \alpha_c
\alpha_c^\top\) with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\alpha_c\) and
non-negative eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n \geq 0\),
computed by =linalg::jacobi_eigen= -- the same classical cyclic-Jacobi
symmetric eigensolver the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]] and
[[file:diffusion_maps_iris_analysis.org][diffusion maps]] reports use.
Tiny negative eigenvalues from round-off are clamped to zero. The
projection of observation \(i\) onto the \(c\)-th feature-space principal
component works out to

\[
\text{embedding}_{i,c} = \sqrt{\lambda_c}\,\alpha_{c,i},
\]

which is exactly =embedding_(i, c) = eig.eigenvectors(i, c) *
std::sqrt(lambda)= in the source -- the eigenvector component scaled by
the square root of its eigenvalue. =eigenvalues()= returns the
\(\lambda_c\) actually used (length =n_components()=, descending), and
=embedding()= / =dimension(c)= return the coordinates. Unlike plain PCA,
there is no =loadings()= method and no reconstruction: the principal
axes live in the unformed feature space \(\mathcal{H}\), so a fitted
point has coordinates but the axes themselves have no finite coordinate
representation to report.

** The linear kernel recovers plain PCA

When \(k(x,y) = x^\top y\), the feature map is the identity and \(K = X
X^\top\) is the ordinary Gram matrix of the (uncentered) data. Centering
it as in §2.3 is identical to centering \(X\), and the nonzero
eigenvalues of the centered \(X X^\top\) equal those of the \(p \times p\)
scatter matrix \((X^c)^\top X^c = (n-1)\,C\). So linear-kernel PCA must
reproduce plain PCA's scores (up to an arbitrary per-component sign), and
its eigenvalues must equal plain PCA's covariance eigenvalues times
\((n-1)\). This is a genuine, checkable identity, not merely asserted
here: §4.2 confirms both halves of it to within double-precision
round-off, exactly as this project's own =KernelPCA= unit test does.

** What we will report

Given the fits, we report exploratory data (true species, for later

\(\gamma\)-sensitivity sweep, an RBF scores scatter, and -- on the

* Data and Methods

** The iris data

As in every companion report: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns
Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ --
measured on =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=
(cm), with no missing values. All four measurements are used as
=KernelPCA= features throughout; =Species= is withheld from the
constructor and used only for the exploratory and scores figures and the
separation metric.

** The concentric circles: a nonlinearly separable ground truth

Iris is close enough to linearly structured that plain PCA already does
well on it, so it cannot by itself demonstrate /why/ a nonlinear kernel
is worth having. This report additionally generates the textbook kernel
PCA dataset: two concentric noisy circles, 150 points each, sampled with
=std::mt19937_64= (seed 42) as

\[
x = r\cos\theta + \varepsilon, \quad y = r\sin\theta + \varepsilon,
\quad \theta \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 2\pi), \quad \varepsilon \sim
\mathcal{N}(0, 0.08^2),
\]

with radius \(r = 1\) for the inner ring and \(r = 3\) for the outer.
The ring membership (inner/outer) is the withheld ground truth:
=KernelPCA= only ever sees the 2D =(x, y)= coordinates, and the labels
are used purely afterward to color figures and to score how well a single
component separates the rings. The two rings are /not/ linearly
separable -- no straight line, and therefore no plain-PCA axis, divides
them -- because the coordinate that distinguishes them, \(\sqrt{x^2 +
y^2}\), is nonlinear in the inputs.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::KernelPCA= (and, for §4.2's
equivalence check, =stats::PCA=) for the models, and =plot::RPlot= for
every figure. No external plotting or statistics package is used.

** Program, presented as a literate whole

*** Includes, usings, and two scoring helpers

Two small helpers used only to /score/ the fits after the fact, never as
inputs to =KernelPCA=: an average between-class/within-class embedded
distance ratio (larger means better-separated classes), and the best
single-threshold classification accuracy obtainable from one coordinate
(sweeping every midpoint, taking the better orientation).

// Average between-class / within-class embedded-distance ratio: larger means the
// classes sit further apart than they spread internally in the embedding.
static double between_within_ratio(const datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double>& emb,
                                   const std::vector<std::string>& labels) {
    const std::size_t n = emb.rows();
    double within_sum = 0.0, between_sum = 0.0;
    std::size_t within_n = 0, between_n = 0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
        for (std::size_t j = i + 1; j < n; ++j) {
            double d = 0.0;
            for (std::size_t c = 0; c < emb.cols(); ++c) {
                const double e = emb(i, c) - emb(j, c);
                d += e * e;
            }
            d = std::sqrt(d);
            if (labels[i] == labels[j]) { within_sum += d; ++within_n; }
            else { between_sum += d; ++between_n; }
        }
    return (between_sum / static_cast<double>(between_n)) /
           (within_sum / static_cast<double>(within_n));
}

// Best single-threshold classification accuracy of a binary label from one
// coordinate: sweep every midpoint, take the better of the two orientations.
static double best_threshold_accuracy(const std::vector<double>& v,
                                      const std::vector<int>& label) {
    const std::size_t n = v.size();
    std::vector<double> sorted(v);
    std::sort(sorted.begin(), sorted.end());
    double best = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t t = 0; t + 1 < n; ++t) {
        const double thr = 0.5 * (sorted[t] + sorted[t + 1]);
        std::size_t correct_lo = 0;  // predict 1 when v < thr
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
            const int pred = (v[i] < thr) ? 1 : 0;
            if (pred == label[i]) ++correct_lo;
        }
        const double acc_lo = static_cast<double>(correct_lo) / static_cast<double>(n);
        best = std::max({best, acc_lo, 1.0 - acc_lo});
    }
    return best;
}

*** Linear kernel PCA versus plain PCA

The empirical check of §2.5: fit a linear-kernel =KernelPCA= and a plain
(unscaled) =PCA=, resolve the arbitrary per-component sign from the first
observation, and report the largest score discrepancy across all points
plus the eigenvalue identity.

#+name: kpca-linear-vs-pca
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Linear kernel PCA reproduces plain (unscaled) PCA ===\n";
    KernelPCA lin(iris, features, KernelPCAOptions{2, "linear", 1.0, 3.0, 1.0});
    PCA pca(iris, features, PCAOptions{true, false});
    {
        const double s0 = (lin.embedding()(0, 0) * pca.scores()(0, 0) >= 0.0) ? 1.0 : -1.0;
        const double s1 = (lin.embedding()(0, 1) * pca.scores()(0, 1) >= 0.0) ? 1.0 : -1.0;
        double max_diff = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < lin.observations(); ++i) {
            max_diff = std::max(max_diff, std::abs(lin.embedding()(i, 0) - s0 * pca.scores()(i, 0)));
            max_diff = std::max(max_diff, std::abs(lin.embedding()(i, 1) - s1 * pca.scores()(i, 1)));
        }
        const double n_minus_1 = static_cast<double>(lin.observations() - 1);
        std::cout << "max |KPC score - +/- PCA score| over all points/2 comps: " << max_diff << "\n";
        std::cout << "linear-KPCA eigenvalue[0]:              " << lin.eigenvalues()[0] << "\n";
        std::cout << "PCA explained_variance[0] * (n-1):     " << pca.explained_variance()[0] * n_minus_1 << "\n";
        std::cout << "linear-KPCA eigenvalue[1]:              " << lin.eigenvalues()[1] << "\n";
        std::cout << "PCA explained_variance[1] * (n-1):     " << pca.explained_variance()[1] * n_minus_1 << "\n";
    }

** The linear kernel reproduces plain PCA

#+begin_example
max |KPC score - +/- PCA score| over all points/2 comps: 0.0000
linear-KPCA eigenvalue[0]:              630.0080
PCA explained_variance[0] * (n-1):     630.0080
linear-KPCA eigenvalue[1]:              36.1579
PCA explained_variance[1] * (n-1):     36.1579
#+end_example

Both halves of §2.5's identity hold to within double-precision
round-off. The largest discrepancy between any linear-kernel PCA score
and the corresponding plain-PCA score (after resolving the arbitrary
per-component sign) is 0.0000 across all 150 flowers and both components:
linear-kernel PCA /is/ plain (unscaled) PCA, computed the long way around
via the \(150 \times 150\) Gram matrix instead of the \(4 \times 4\)
covariance matrix. The eigenvalues match the other prediction exactly
too: the centered-kernel eigenvalues (630.0080, 36.1579) equal PCA's
covariance eigenvalues scaled by \((n-1) = 149\), confirming that the two
routes diagonalize the same operator up to that scale factor. This is the
sanity check that licenses trusting the RBF and polynomial fits below,
whose answers we have no independent PCA to compare against.

** Fitted RBF kernel PCA and its eigenvalues

#+begin_example
Kernel Principal Component Analysis
Features: Sepal.Length, Sepal.Width, Petal.Length, Petal.Width
Kernel: rbf
Number of obs: 150, components: 2

Eigenvalues used:
  KPC1: 47.5085
  KPC2: 14.4953
#+end_example

The RBF kernel's centered-Gram eigenvalues (47.5085, 14.4953) are on a
completely different scale from the linear kernel's (630.0080, 36.1579)
and are /not/ interpretable as variances in the original feature units:
they are variances along axes in the infinite-dimensional RBF feature
space, whose geometry has no direct correspondence to centimetres of
sepal or petal. This is the first concrete sign that kernel PCA is doing
something plain PCA cannot -- the eigenvalues no longer answer "how much
raw-measurement variance," because there is no finite raw-measurement
basis for the RBF feature space to begin with.

** Species separation across kernels

#+begin_example
        kernel   between/within
        linear            3.8975
     rbf(0.13)            4.3087
     poly(d=3)            3.0091
#+end_example

Measured by the average between-species/within-species embedded-distance
ratio (§3.4.1's helper), the RBF kernel at \(\gamma=0.13\) separates the
three iris species better (4.3087) than the linear kernel (3.8975, i.e.
plain PCA), while the cubic polynomial kernel does /worse/ (3.0091). The
RBF gain over linear is real but modest -- iris is only mildly nonlinear,
so there is only so much curvature for the kernel to exploit -- and the
polynomial result is a useful caution that "nonlinear kernel" does not
automatically mean "better separation": a badly-matched kernel (here a
degree-3 polynomial, which inflates large inner products and distorts the
tight /setosa/ cluster) can actively hurt. §4.9's circles experiment
shows the RBF advantage in a setting where it is not modest at all.

** RBF gamma sensitivity

#+begin_example
     gamma   between/within
    0.0100            4.1209
    0.0500            4.4861
    0.1300            4.3087
    0.5000            3.8584
    2.0000            2.8500
   10.0000            1.6870
#+end_example

Separation is single-peaked in the kernel bandwidth \(\gamma\), best near
\(\gamma=0.05\) (4.4861) and falling off on both sides. The reason is the
same one that governs every RBF-kernel method (including this project's
[[file:gaussian_process_regression_iris_analysis.org][Gaussian process]]
and [[file:svm_iris_analysis.org][SVM]] reports): \(\gamma\) sets the
kernel's length scale relative to the data. Too small (\(\gamma=0.01\))
and the kernel is nearly flat -- every pair of points looks equally
similar, so the embedding approaches the linear kernel's and the
nonlinear advantage vanishes. Too large (\(\gamma=10\), ratio 1.6870) and
the kernel is a near-spike -- every point resembles only itself, the
centered Gram matrix approaches the identity, and no meaningful shared
structure survives for the eigendecomposition to find. The best value is
in between, and it is genuinely data-dependent: §4.8 finds a /different/
optimal \(\gamma\) for the circles, because their scale is different. Our
headline fit's \(\gamma=0.13\) (chosen to match this project's unit test)
is close to, though not exactly at, the empirical iris optimum.

** The concentric circles: raw structure

[[file:../../build/org/kpca_circles_raw.svg]]

Two clean noisy rings, one inside the other. The crucial property is
visible directly: /no straight line divides the blue inner ring from the
red outer one/. Any linear method -- plain PCA, or equivalently
linear-kernel PCA -- is therefore doomed here before it starts, because
its components are by construction straight axes, and the coordinate that
actually separates the rings (distance from the origin) is a nonlinear
function of \(x\) and \(y\). This is the situation the kernel trick exists
for.

** The circles: linear fails, RBF succeeds

#+begin_example
     gamma      KPC1 accuracy
    0.0500            0.6900
    0.1000            0.6767
    0.3000            1.0000
    1.0000            0.7500
    3.0000            0.7700
   10.0000            0.8300
#+end_example

Sweeping \(\gamma\) and scoring how well the /first RBF component alone/
separates the rings (best single-threshold accuracy on KPC1) reveals a
sharply non-monotonic curve with a spike to /perfect/ separation at
\(\gamma=0.3\). Away from that value the first component recovers much
less -- 0.69, 0.68 for too-flat kernels, 0.75-0.83 for too-narrow ones --
a vivid, quantitative demonstration that on this dataset the kernel
bandwidth is not a minor tuning detail but the entire difference between
success and failure. The non-monotonicity is real, not noise: at
\(\gamma=0.3\) the RBF length scale matches the gap between the rings, so
the radial structure collapses onto the leading eigenvector; at other
scales the radial signal is split across several components or washed out,
and no single coordinate captures it.

#+begin_example
                  method best 1-D (KPC1) accuracy
           linear kernel            0.7300
  rbf kernel (gamma=0.3)            1.0000
#+end_example

Head to head at the well-matched bandwidth, the contrast is total: the
linear kernel's first component separates the rings at only 73% -- barely
better than the 50% floor, and no better than guessing "outer" for the
higher-variance points -- while the RBF kernel's first component separates
them /perfectly/, 100%. This single pair of numbers is the whole reason
kernel PCA exists: a strictly linear method cannot represent the rings'
radial structure at all, and the kernel trick supplies exactly the
nonlinear feature space in which that structure becomes a single linear
axis.

The two embedding scatters make the numbers visual. Linear-kernel PCA

* Discussion

**The kernel trick converts a hard nonlinear problem into an easy linear
one -- when the kernel matches the data.** §4.9 is the whole argument in
one experiment: the concentric rings are unseparable by any linear axis
(73% on linear KPC1, §4.2 having proven that /is/ plain PCA), yet become
perfectly separable by a single axis once the data is viewed through an
RBF kernel of the right bandwidth (100%). Nothing about the data changed;
only the inner-product structure did. This is the same mechanism that
turns a linear classifier into the nonlinear
[[file:svm_iris_analysis.org][support vector machine]] -- kernel PCA is
simply the unsupervised member of the kernel-methods family, applying the
trick to variance-maximization instead of margin-maximization.

**"When the kernel matches the data" is doing real work in that
sentence.** Both the iris \(\gamma\) sweep (§4.5) and the circles
\(\gamma\) sweep (§4.8) show separation quality is single-peaked in the
bandwidth, and -- crucially -- the two peaks are at /different/
\(\gamma\), because the two datasets live at different scales. Kernel PCA
has no built-in way to choose \(\gamma\); a value that is excellent for
one dataset can be useless for another, and the circles sweep shows the
penalty for a bad choice can be the entire difference between 100% and
68% separation. In practice \(\gamma\) is chosen by cross-validation
against a downstream task, or by a heuristic tied to the data's typical
pairwise distance -- the same problem the
[[file:diffusion_maps_iris_analysis.org][diffusion maps report]] confronts
for its own =heat_kernel_epsilon=, and for the same underlying reason: any
distance-based kernel needs a length scale, and the right one is a
property of the data, not a universal constant.

**Kernel PCA gives up interpretability that plain PCA has.** Plain PCA
returns loadings -- each component is an explicit weighted combination of
the original features -- and supports exact reconstruction, which is why
its [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][companion report]] can discuss a "flower
size axis" and run a reconstruction-error sweep. Kernel PCA can do
neither: with an RBF kernel the principal axes live in an
infinite-dimensional feature space with no finite coordinate
representation, so =KernelPCA= exposes an =embedding()= but no
=loadings()= and no inverse transform (the "pre-image problem" is a known
hard, only-approximately-solvable sub-field of its own). The RBF
eigenvalues in §4.3 underline this: they are genuine variances, but along
axes that cannot be written down in terms of sepals and petals. Kernel
PCA buys the ability to see nonlinear structure at the cost of being able
to say, in the original variables, what that structure /is/.

* Conclusion

Fitting =datamunge='s C++ =stats::KernelPCA= to Fisher's iris
measurements -- with =Species= withheld throughout -- first confirms the
theory: with a linear kernel it reproduces plain unscaled PCA's scores to
0.0000 maximum error and its eigenvalues up to the exact \((n-1)\) scale
factor, computed the long way through the \(150 \times 150\) Gram matrix.
Switching to an RBF kernel improves iris species separation over the
linear kernel (4.3087 versus 3.8975) with a single-peaked dependence on
the bandwidth \(\gamma\), while a mismatched polynomial kernel does worse
(3.0091) -- nonlinear is not automatically better. The concentric-circles
experiment is the report's central result and the clearest possible
statement of what kernel PCA is for: two rings that /no/ linear axis can
separate (73% on the first linear-kernel component, which §4.2 proves is
plain PCA) become /perfectly/ separable on the first RBF component alone
(100% at \(\gamma=0.3\)) -- but only at the right bandwidth, the same
sweep collapsing to 68% at a poorly-matched one. The trick's power and
its central caveat -- choosing \(\gamma\) -- are thus both visible in a
single dataset. As with its companions, this entire report is
reproducible end to end via =just org-example kernel_pca_iris_analysis=,
which re-executes §3.4.12's assembly block -- and therefore every number
and figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
