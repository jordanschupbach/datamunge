#+begin_abstract
We fit a bagged ensemble of 100 CART decision trees -- a random forest --
to Fisher's (1936) iris data using =datamunge='s C++
=stats::RandomForestClassifier=, and report it as a literate program: every
fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++ binary
that produced every number and figure in this document. The forest reaches
100.00% /training/ accuracy -- a textbook overfitting warning sign -- but
its out-of-bag estimate, computed without holding out any data at all, is a
much more credible 95.33%, resolving the exact "every accuracy figure here
is optimistic" caveat every one of this project's other classifier reports
has had to raise. Feature importance, averaged across all 100 trees, gives
both sepal measurements small but /nonzero/ credit (8.52% and 1.80%) where
the companion decision-tree report's single tree gave them exactly zero --
a direct, visible consequence of randomly restricting each split to a
feature subset. We derive bootstrap aggregating, random feature selection,
out-of-bag estimation, and ensemble feature importance from first
principles, connect each to the corresponding library call, and close by
placing this fourth classification method alongside its three companions
on the same 150 flowers.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

This project now has three companion reports fitting three different
classifiers to the same 150 iris flowers --
[[file:lda_iris_analysis.org][=lda_iris_analysis.org=]],
[[file:svm_iris_analysis.org][=svm_iris_analysis.org=]], and
[[file:dt_iris_analysis.org][=dt_iris_analysis.org=]] -- and the decision
tree report's own discussion ends by naming this one as its natural
sequel: a single tree is fast to fit and easy to read, but is also a
notoriously /high-variance/ method, meaning a small change to the training
data can produce a visibly different tree. A random forest's answer to
that instability is almost embarrassingly simple -- fit /many/ trees on
randomized variations of the data and let them vote -- and yet it is one of
the most reliably strong off-the-shelf classifiers in practice. This report
fits exactly that ensemble, using =datamunge='s own
=DecisionTreeClassifier= as its building block, and spends real attention
on the one diagnostic a single tree can't offer at all: an accuracy
estimate that doesn't require pretending the training data is also test
data.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.8) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp= file,
compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is that
program's literal output.

§2 derives bootstrap aggregating, random feature selection at each split,
out-of-bag error estimation, and ensemble feature importance, each tied to
the specific library call that implements it. §3 describes the program
chunk by chunk. §4 reports the fitted forest, its training-versus-OOB
accuracy gap, feature importance, a tree-count sweep, and the decision-
region picture this method produces. §5 places all four companion reports'
methods side by side. §6 concludes.

* Background: Random Forests

** Setup and notation

As in the companion reports: $n = 150$ flowers, $p = 4$ measurements
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$, and $y_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, $K = 3$, labeling
one of the three species.

** Bootstrap aggregating

The decision-tree companion report's real limitation is variance: a
greedy, single tree fit on the full training set is exactly one particular
tree among many that could have resulted from a slightly different
sample. /Bagging/ (bootstrap aggregating; Breiman, 1996) addresses this
directly: draw $B$ bootstrap samples -- each one $n$ rows drawn /with
replacement/ from the original $n$ training rows -- fit one full decision
tree per sample, and predict a new point by majority vote across all $B$
trees. If each tree's prediction were independent noise around the true
answer, averaging $B$ of them would shrink prediction variance by a factor
of $B$; in reality the trees are correlated (they're fit on overlapping
data), so the benefit is real but smaller than that best case, which is
exactly the motivation for §2.3. This is
=RandomForestClassifierOptions::n_trees= and =bootstrap= in
=datamunge=, fit fresh per tree inside =RandomForestClassifier::fit=.

** Random feature selection

Bagging alone still leaves correlated trees: if one predictor is
overwhelmingly the best splitter (as =Petal.Length= is here, per the
decision-tree companion report), nearly every bootstrap sample will
produce a tree whose root splits on it, and highly correlated trees don't
reduce variance nearly as much as independent ones would. The "random" in
random forest fixes this by restricting /each individual split/, not just
each tree, to a random subset of =max_features= predictors -- drawn fresh
at every node -- so a tree is sometimes forced to split on a weaker
predictor simply because the current best one wasn't offered as a
candidate this time. =datamunge= defaults =max_features= for
classification to $\lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor$, at least 1 -- with $p=4$
predictors here, that's 2, confirmed directly in §4.2's =Call:= line. This
single mechanism is the entire difference between a random forest and
plain bagged trees, and is exactly why §4.3's feature importance looks
different from the single-tree companion report's.

** Out-of-bag estimation

Because each tree trains on an $n$-row sample drawn /with replacement/
from $n$ rows, a given row has probability $(1 - 1/n)^n \approx e^{-1}
\approx 36.8\%$ of never being drawn into any one tree's bootstrap sample
-- that row is /out-of-bag/ for that tree. Averaged over all $n$ rows and
$B$ trees, roughly a third of the ensemble never saw any given training
row during fitting, which is enough to do something a single tree cannot:
for each row, take a majority vote using /only/ the trees that didn't
train on it, and compare that vote to the row's true label. The resulting
=oob_accuracy()= is a near-unbiased estimate of test-set accuracy,
computed entirely from the training data, without ever holding out a
separate validation split. §4.2 reports it directly alongside (optimistic)
training accuracy, and the gap between the two is this report's headline
number.

** Ensemble feature importance

Exactly as in the decision-tree companion report, every split anywhere in
the forest credits its weighted impurity decrease to whichever predictor
it split on; =RandomForestClassifier::feature_importance()= simply
averages that per-tree credit across all $B$ trees and renormalizes to sum
to 1. Because §2.3's random feature restriction forces even a weak
predictor to occasionally be the /only/ available choice at some split, a
predictor that a single tree would never touch (and therefore score
/exactly/ zero, as both sepal measurements did in the decision-tree
companion report) typically earns some small but nonzero credit here --
§4.3 confirms this happens on this exact data.

** Prediction

A new point is classified by running it down every tree in the forest and
taking a majority vote; =predict_detail()='s =vote_share= field reports,
per class, what fraction of the $B$ trees voted for it. This is a fourth
distinct notion of "confidence" alongside the three companion reports'
Bayes posterior (LDA), one-vs-one vote out of very few pairs (SVM), and
single-leaf empirical frequency (a lone decision tree): with $B=100$ trees
here, =vote_share= can take up to 101 distinct values between 0 and 1,
far finer-grained than a single tree's leaf-count fractions or an SVM's
out-of-3 vote, without requiring any distributional assumption the way
LDA's posterior does.

** What we will report

Given the fit, we report the forest's own summary (training /and/
out-of-bag accuracy side by side, plus feature importance), a confusion
matrix, a classification plot marking misclassified points directly,
feature importance, a sweep over the number of trees, and -- reduced to
two predictors purely for visualization, the same caveat every companion
report makes about its own 2-D view -- the forest's decision regions.

* Data and Methods

** The data

As in all three companion reports: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns
Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ --
measured on =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=
(cm), with no missing values.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, the formula-driven
=stats::RandomForestClassifier= for every model in this report (itself
built internally from =stats::DecisionTreeClassifier=, the companion
report's own class), and =plot::RPlot=/=plot::RLayout= for every figure.
No external plotting or statistics package is used.

*** Number-of-trees sensitivity

Refit at =n_trees= 1 through 400, tracking training accuracy alongside

#+name: rf-ntrees-sensitivity
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    {
        const std::vector<std::size_t> tree_counts =
            {1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400};
        std::vector<double> tree_x, oob_accs, train_accs;
        std::cout << "\nn_trees sensitivity:\n";
        std::cout << std::setw(10) << "n_trees" << std::setw(12) << "train"
                   << std::setw(10) << "OOB\n";
        for (const auto nt : tree_counts) {
            RandomForestClassifierOptions opts;
            opts.n_trees = nt;
            RandomForestClassifier m(iris, kFormula, opts);
            const double train_acc = m.training_accuracy() * 100.0;
            const double oob_acc = m.oob_accuracy() * 100.0;
            tree_x.push_back(static_cast<double>(nt));
            train_accs.push_back(train_acc);
            oob_accs.push_back(oob_acc);
            std::cout << std::setw(10) << nt << std::setw(11) << std::fixed
                       << std::setprecision(2) << train_acc << "%"
                       << std::setw(9) << oob_acc << "%\n";
        }
        auto oob_plot = RPlot::plot(tree_x, oob_accs, "b", "OOB accuracy",
                                     {220, 38, 38});
        oob_plot.line(tree_x, train_accs, "training accuracy",
                      {37, 99, 235}, 2.0);
        oob_plot.title("Accuracy vs Number of Trees")
            .x_label("n_trees").y_label("accuracy (%)")
            .background({250, 250, 252});
        oob_plot.save_svg("build/org/rf_iris_ntrees_sensitivity.svg");

* Discussion

**Four methods on one dataset.** With this report, four genuinely
different classifiers have now been fit to the identical 150 flowers:
LDA's single global eigenproblem, SVM's convex quadratic program, one
greedy decision tree, and this greedy tree's bagged, decorrelated
ensemble. All four land within a couple of points of each other on
accuracy (LDA 98.00%, SVM 97.33%, single tree 97.33%, this forest's OOB
95.33%), because -- as every report in this series has now said in its own
words -- the one genuine difficulty in this data is a single overlapping
region between /versicolor/ and /virginica/ that no amount of additional
modeling sophistication removes, since it reflects real biological
similarity between those two species, not a shortcoming of any one method.

**Why the forest's own accuracy figure looks lower.** It would be a
mistake to read 95.33% as "the random forest is worse than the other three
methods": it is the only figure among all four reports computed the way a
genuine test-set accuracy would be, rather than by asking a model to grade
its own training data. Read that way, the single tree's 97.33%
/training/ accuracy and this forest's 95.33% /out-of-bag/ accuracy are not
directly comparable at all -- the fairer comparison is the forest's own
100.00% training figure (matching or beating every companion report) against
its 95.33% out-of-bag figure, and that gap is the real, quantified cost of
the single tree's overfitting risk that the decision-tree report could only
describe qualitatively.

**Interpretability, once more.** The decision-tree companion report's
entire fitted model fit in eight lines a person could read end to end;
this report's model is 100 such trees, and no single one of them is "the"
model the way the lone tree was. That trade -- give up a single readable
structure in exchange for a materially more reliable accuracy estimate and
(per §4.2) less brittle, better-distributed feature importance -- is the
central trade this whole report has been making explicit.

* Conclusion

A 100-tree random forest, fit with =datamunge='s C++
=stats::RandomForestClassifier=, reaches 100.00% training accuracy but a
more trustworthy 95.33% out-of-bag accuracy on Fisher's three iris
species -- resolving, with an honest number rather than a caveat, the
resubstitution-accuracy limitation every other report in this series has
had to flag. Random feature selection at each split gives both sepal
measurements small but nonzero importance where a single tree gave them
none, and a number-of-trees sweep shows out-of-bag accuracy stabilizing by
roughly 25-50 trees even as training accuracy saturates almost
immediately. Placed alongside its three companions, this report completes
a four-way comparison spanning a global eigenproblem, a convex program, one
greedy tree, and this bagged ensemble of many -- four different roads that
all arrive within a couple of points of each other on the same 150
flowers. As with its companions, this entire report is reproducible end to
end via =just org-example rf_iris_analysis=, which re-executes §3.3.8's
assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above -- from the
=datamunge= source tree.
