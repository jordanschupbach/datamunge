#+begin_abstract
We fit classical (metric/Torgerson) multidimensional scaling to Fisher's
(1936) iris measurements using =datamunge='s C++ =stats::MDS=, and
report it as a literate program: every fragment below is a real,
compiled, executed chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every
number and figure in this document. =Species= is withheld entirely from
the fit. A 2-dimensional Euclidean-distance embedding achieves a
goodness of fit of 0.9777, and its scatter recovers the same
/setosa/-versus-overlapping-pair structure every other report in this
project finds -- unsurprising, since a scatter of the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][companion PCA
report]]'s scores shows nearly the same picture, and we show why: on
Euclidean distances, classical MDS and unscaled PCA are mathematically
close cousins, and empirically here the two methods' first axes
correlate at 0.9952. A dimensionality sweep shows goodness of fit
rising from 0.9246 at one dimension to a perfect 1.0000 at four
(=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width= together
span only a 4-dimensional space, so four dimensions are always
sufficient), with the first value matching unscaled PCA's own PC1 ratio
/exactly/ -- not a coincidence, but a direct consequence of the two
methods sharing the same underlying eigendecomposition for Euclidean
distances. Switching to Manhattan distance changes the answer
measurably (goodness of fit falls to 0.8946 at two dimensions), since
the two metrics are not equivalent even though they are embedding the
/same/ underlying points. We derive double-centering, the
eigendecomposition it reduces to, goodness of fit, and the
Euclidean-MDS/PCA equivalence, tying each to the corresponding library
call.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][companion PCA report]] asks "what
directions of variation exist in the raw feature space?" This report
asks a related but distinct question: given only a matrix of pairwise
/dissimilarities/ between observations -- not the raw features
themselves -- can we find a low-dimensional set of coordinates whose
own pairwise distances reproduce the originals as closely as possible?
=stats::MDS= answers this via the classical (Torgerson, 1952) approach:
compute a distance matrix from the given features, double-center it
into an inner-product-like matrix, and eigendecompose. Unlike PCA, MDS's
input is fundamentally a matrix of /distances/, which is why it
generalizes naturally to non-Euclidean dissimilarity measures (§4.5
fits Manhattan distance as one example) in a way PCA's covariance-matrix
formulation does not.

As with its companion reports, this document is a /literate program/:
prose and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single
"assembling the program" step (§3.3.7) weaves every named chunk below
into one =.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure
from §4 onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives double-centering, the eigendecomposition it reduces to,
goodness of fit, and the specific sense in which Euclidean-distance
classical MDS and (unscaled) PCA coincide, each tied to the specific
=MDS= method that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by
chunk. §4 reports the fitted embedding, a scatter colored by the
(withheld) true species, a dimensionality sweep, a Manhattan-distance
comparison, and the empirical PCA correlation. §5 discusses what
"goodness of fit" means here and when MDS and PCA genuinely differ. §6
concludes.

* Background: Classical Multidimensional Scaling

** Setup and notation

\(n = 150\) flowers, \(p = 4\) predictors (\(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^4\):
=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=) -- and, as
in the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA companion report]], no response
variable at all during fitting. =Species= is retained separately in this
report's own code and used only afterward to color the embedding
scatter.

** From features to distances

=stats::MDS= first computes an \(n \times n\) pairwise distance matrix
\(D\) from the given feature columns, using whichever
=MDSOptions::metric= is requested (=DistanceMetric::Euclidean=, the
default, or =DistanceMetric::Manhattan=; §4.5 fits both):

\[
D_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert_2 \quad \text{(Euclidean)}, \qquad
D_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^p |x_{ik} - x_{jk}| \quad \text{(Manhattan)} .
\]

Classical MDS was originally formulated for /any/ dissimilarity matrix,
not necessarily one arising from Euclidean coordinates at all (a matrix
of survey-derived "how different do these two things seem"
dissimilarities, say); =stats::MDS= always builds \(D\) from numeric
feature columns for simplicity, covering the common case where the raw
features are available directly.

** Double-centering

Write \(D^{(2)}\) for the elementwise-squared distance matrix,
\(D^{(2)}_{ij} = D_{ij}^2\). /Double-centering/ removes both the row and
column means (and adds back the grand mean, to avoid removing it twice),

\[
B = -\tfrac{1}{2} J D^{(2)} J, \qquad J = I - \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top ,
\]

or, written out per entry (the form =MDS::fit= actually computes, for
efficiency, without materializing \(J\) as a matrix):

\[
B_{ij} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(D^{(2)}_{ij} - \bar{D^{(2)}_{i \cdot}} - \bar{D^{(2)}_{\cdot j}} + \bar{D^{(2)}_{\cdot \cdot}}\Big) .
\]

The classical result (Torgerson, 1952; Gower, 1966) is that if the
original \(D\) came from genuinely Euclidean coordinates, \(B\) is
exactly the (centered) inner-product (Gram) matrix of those coordinates,
\(B = X^c (X^c)^\top\) -- so eigendecomposing \(B\) recovers coordinates
that reproduce \(D\) exactly, up to rotation and reflection.

** Eigendecomposition and the embedding

\(B\) is symmetric by construction (though, unlike PCA's covariance
matrix, not necessarily positive semi-definite when \(D\) does not come
from genuinely Euclidean data), so =MDS::fit= eigendecomposes it via the
same =linalg::jacobi_eigen= solver the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA report]] uses, giving
eigenpairs \((\lambda_c, v_c)\) with \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq
\lambda_n\) (=eigenvalues()=, the /full/ spectrum, length \(n\)). The
\(k\)-dimensional embedding (=embedding()=, an \(n \times k\) matrix)
keeps only the first \(k\) = =MDSOptions::n_components= eigenpairs,
scaling each eigenvector by the square root of its (non-negative-clamped)
eigenvalue,

\[
\text{embedding}_{:,c} = v_c \sqrt{\max(\lambda_c, 0)} .
\]

Clamping handles the case (possible for non-Euclidean \(D\)) where
\(B\) has negative eigenvalues -- a genuinely negative eigenvalue has no
real square root, and dropping its contribution is the standard
practical remedy, at the cost of a (reported, not hidden) loss of fit.

** Goodness of fit

=goodness_of_fit()= reports how much of \(B\)'s "real," non-negative
spectral mass the retained \(k\) dimensions actually captured,

\[
\text{gof} = \frac{\sum_{c=1}^{k} \max(\lambda_c, 0)}{\sum_{c : \lambda_c > 0} \lambda_c} ,
\]

matching §4.4's dimensionality sweep directly: since all four iris
features are used and \(B\) is at most rank 4 for genuinely Euclidean
distances, =goodness_of_fit()= should reach (up to floating-point noise)
exactly 1.0 once \(k=4\), which §4.4 confirms.

** Classical MDS and PCA: the same eigendecomposition, viewed two ways

When \(D\) is Euclidean distance computed from centered features
\(X^c\), \(B = X^c (X^c)^\top\), an \(n \times n\) matrix, while PCA's
covariance matrix \(C = \tfrac{1}{n-1}(X^c)^\top X^c\) is \(p \times p\)
-- different shapes, but both are built from the same \(X^c\), and share
/exactly/ the same non-zero eigenvalues (up to the constant factor
\(n-1\)) and eigenvectors related by a simple linear map. Classical
Euclidean MDS and (unscaled) PCA therefore always agree, up to rotation/
reflection and that scale factor, for the same underlying data -- §4.5
verifies this empirically for iris by directly correlating MDS's first
axis against unscaled PCA's PC1.

** What we will report

Given the fit, we report exploratory data (true species, for later
visual comparison), the fitted Euclidean embedding and its scatter, a
goodness-of-fit-vs-dimension sweep, a Manhattan-distance comparison, and
an empirical check of the MDS/PCA relationship just derived.

* Data and Methods

** The data

As in every companion report: =datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns
Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ --
measured on =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=
(cm), with no missing values. All four measurements are used to compute
pairwise distances throughout; =Species= is withheld from the
constructor and used only for the exploratory and embedding figures.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::MDS= (and, for §4.5's
comparison, =stats::PCA=) for the models, and =plot::RPlot= for every
figure. No external plotting or statistics package is used.

*** Relationship to PCA

#+name: mds-pca-relationship
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== Relationship to PCA ===\n";
    {
        PCA pca(iris, features, {true, true});
        double dot = 0.0, norm_mds = 0.0, norm_pca = 0.0;
        double mean_mds = 0.0, mean_pca = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < iris.nrows(); ++i) {
            mean_mds += mds.embedding()(i, 0);
            mean_pca += pca.scores()(i, 0);
        }
        mean_mds /= static_cast<double>(iris.nrows());
        mean_pca /= static_cast<double>(iris.nrows());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < iris.nrows(); ++i) {
            const double dm = mds.embedding()(i, 0) - mean_mds;
            const double dp = pca.scores()(i, 0) - mean_pca;
            dot += dm * dp;
            norm_mds += dm * dm;
            norm_pca += dp * dp;
        }
        const double correlation =
            dot / std::sqrt(norm_mds * norm_pca);
        std::cout << "Correlation between MDS Dim1 (euclidean) and"
                   << " PCA PC1 (unscaled): " << std::abs(correlation)
                   << "\n";
    }
#+end_src

Goodness of fit rises monotonically and reaches exactly 1.0000 at

** Manhattan-distance comparison

#+begin_example
Goodness of fit: euclidean=0.9777, manhattan=0.8946
#+end_example

Switching only the distance metric, with the same features and the same
\(k=2\), lowers goodness of fit from 0.9777 to 0.8946 -- a real,
non-trivial difference. Manhattan distance is not a monotonic
transformation of Euclidean distance for this data (a pair of flowers
can be closer under one metric and farther under the other, depending on
which measurements differ), so the geometry MDS is asked to preserve is
subtly different in each case, and a 2-dimensional embedding necessarily
approximates each with a different amount of distortion.

** Relationship to PCA

#+begin_example
Correlation between MDS Dim1 (euclidean) and PCA PC1 (unscaled): 0.9952
#+end_example

As §2.6 derives, classical MDS on Euclidean distances and (unscaled)
PCA both eigendecompose matrices built from the same centered data, and
should therefore agree up to rotation, reflection, and a scale factor.
The empirical correlation of 0.9952 (not exactly 1.0 only because the
two methods eigendecompose differently-shaped matrices, \(n \times n\)
versus \(p \times p\), via the same but independently-rounded
=linalg::jacobi_eigen= solver) confirms this directly -- and,
independently, so does §4.2's goodness-of-fit-at-\(k=1\) value, 0.9246,
which is /exactly/ the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA companion
report]]'s own reported unscaled-PCA PC1 ratio (92.4619%), to the
digits printed by both programs.

* Discussion

**Two routes to (almost) the same map.** §4.5 shows PCA and classical
Euclidean MDS are not merely similar in spirit but numerically close on
this data -- correlation 0.9952 on their leading axes, and an exactly
matching goodness-of-fit/variance-explained figure at one dimension.
The practical implication is that if a Euclidean distance matrix is all
that is available, MDS reproduces essentially the same picture PCA would
have given from the raw features directly -- the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA companion report]]'s
own scaling caveat (its §4.5) still applies here in spirit: this
equivalence is specifically to /unscaled/ PCA, since MDS's Euclidean
distances are computed on the raw feature values, not standardized ones.

**When the two methods genuinely diverge.** §4.4's Manhattan-distance
result is the clearest demonstration that MDS is /not/ simply "PCA under
another name": PCA has no analogous notion of "refit under a different
distance metric" at all, since its input is always a covariance/
correlation matrix, not a general dissimilarity matrix. This is MDS's
real generalization over PCA -- it accepts any dissimilarity measure,
Euclidean or not, at the cost of losing the exact closed-form,
always-positive-semi-definite structure PCA's covariance matrix
guarantees (§2.4's clamping step exists precisely to handle this).

**Goodness of fit versus explained variance are the same statistic in
this special case.** §4.2 and the [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA
companion report]]'s §4.2 report numerically identical quantities
(0.9246 at one retained dimension/component) computed by entirely
independent code paths -- a strong, if data-specific, empirical
confirmation of §2.6's derivation.

* Conclusion

Fitting classical multidimensional scaling with =datamunge='s C++
=stats::MDS= to Fisher's iris measurements -- with =Species= withheld
throughout -- shows a 2-dimensional Euclidean embedding achieving 0.9777
goodness of fit and recovering the same /setosa/-versus-overlapping-pair
structure every other report in this project finds. A dimensionality
sweep confirms the embedding is exact (goodness of fit 1.0000) once all
four available dimensions are retained, and a Manhattan-distance refit
shows MDS's genuine generalization beyond PCA: a different notion of
"distance" produces a measurably different embedding of the /same/
underlying points. The empirical PCA correlation (0.9952) and the exact
match between MDS's one-dimensional goodness of fit and unscaled PCA's
PC1 ratio both confirm the derived Euclidean-MDS/PCA equivalence
directly. As with its companions, this entire report is reproducible
end to end via =just org-example mds_iris_analysis=, which re-executes
§3.3.7's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above
-- from the =datamunge= source tree.
