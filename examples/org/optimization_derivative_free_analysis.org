#+begin_abstract
We compare two derivative-free optimizers in =datamunge='s C++ =optim=
module -- =NelderMead=, a strictly local downhill simplex search, and
=CMAES=, the population-based Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution
Strategy -- on the same two objectives and starting points as this
project's companion report, =optimization_analysis.org=, as a literate
program: every number and figure below comes from a single compiled,
executed C++ binary. On the bowl-plus-ripple =BumpyFunction= (true
combined minimum $f=-2.235$ at $(4.191,-0.326)$, found by the companion
report's =SimulatedAnnealing=), =NelderMead= started at the origin
collapses into a much shallower nearby basin, $f=-0.414926$ at
$(2.079,-2.428)$ -- and =CMAES=, run from the same start, converges to
the *identical* point to six decimal places, a real confirmation that
both methods, once fully converged, land on the same well-defined local
minimum rather than disagreeing about where the basin bottom is. On the
=RastriginFunction=, started from $(3,-4)$ exactly as in the companion
report, =NelderMead= gets trapped almost immediately: 109 evaluations
move it only from $f=25.000000$ (the starting value) to $f=24.873845$,
never escaping the single grid cell its tiny initial simplex was born
into. =CMAES= with its library default population (six candidates per
generation for this two-dimensional problem) does much better but is
/seed-dependent/: across five seeds it ranges from an exact
$f=0.000000$ (seed 7) down to $f=24.873845$ (seed 2 -- indistinguishable
from =NelderMead='s failure). Enlarging the population to =40=
(matching =PSO='s default population size) makes =CMAES= converge to
$f=0.000000$ reliably across every seed tested, matching the companion
report's population-method success on this benchmark. A same-run
reproduction of the companion report's =PSO= (global topology, constant
inertia) confirms its published $f=0.000000$ result and adds a new
number the companion report did not report: 6081 function evaluations
to get there, letting us plot all three methods' best-so-far
convergence curves on one real, shared figure.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The companion report, =optimization_analysis.org=, organizes
=datamunge='s C++ =optim= module around the amount of information an
optimizer assumes is available about the objective: an exact gradient
(=DifferentiableFunction=), a separable sum of per-example gradients
(=DifferentiableSeparableFunction=), or nothing beyond point evaluation
(=ArbitraryFunction=). This report stays entirely within that last,
most general case, but draws out a further distinction the companion
report's own =ArbitraryFunction= consumers (=SimulatedAnnealing=, then
=PSO=, =DifferentialEvolution=, and =GeneticAlgorithm=) already
illustrate implicitly: whether an optimizer tracks a *single* candidate
solution or an entire *population* of them at once. =NelderMead=
maintains exactly one object, a simplex of $n+1$ points that deforms
and moves as a whole; =CMAES= maintains a full generation of $\lambda$
sampled candidates plus an adapted Gaussian search distribution (mean,
step size, and full covariance) summarizing where the next generation
should be drawn from. Both consume only =ArbitraryFunction::evaluate()=
-- neither ever asks for, or could use, a gradient -- but the
population/no-population distinction turns out to matter enormously for
which of §4's two objectives each method can actually solve, exactly
the way it did for =SimulatedAnnealing= (single-point, and correctly
displaced by the =BumpyFunction='s ripple term) versus the companion
report's population methods (reliably exact on =RastriginFunction=) in
the earlier report.

As with its companion, this document is a /literate program/: prose
and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling
the program" step (§3.3.7) weaves every named chunk below into one
=.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4
onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives =NelderMead='s four simplex operations and =CMAES='s
log-rank weighting and cumulative step-size adaptation, each tied to
the real formula in its =.cpp= file. §3 describes the program
chunk by chunk, reusing the companion report's exact =BumpyFunction=
and =RastriginFunction= objective classes and its =LoggingArbitrary=
convergence-logging decorator. §4 reports both methods' results on both
objectives, a seed/population-size sensitivity sweep for =CMAES= on
Rastrigin, and a same-run reproduction of the companion report's =PSO=
for a true shared convergence figure. §5 discusses what the sensitivity
sweep and the bumpy-function's identical convergence point reveal. §6
concludes.

* Background: Optimization Methods

** The ArbitraryFunction interface, revisited

Both methods in this report target =ArbitraryFunction=, supplying only
=evaluate(coordinates)= -- no gradient, ever. This is the same
interface the companion report's =SimulatedAnnealing=, =PSO=,
=DifferentialEvolution=, and =GeneticAlgorithm= all target; this
report's contribution is two /different/ ways of using nothing but
evaluations, one that tracks a single deforming simplex and one that
tracks and adapts an entire generation's search distribution.

** Nelder-Mead: local simplex search

=NelderMead= (Nelder & Mead, 1965) maintains a simplex of $n+1$ points
in $n$ dimensions -- a triangle for $n=2$, the case both of §4's
objectives use. Given a starting point $x_0$, the initial simplex sets
vertex $0$ to $x_0$ and displaces vertex $j+1$ along coordinate $j$ by

$$\Delta_j = \texttt{initial\_simplex\_scale} \cdot \max(1, |x_{0,j}|),$$

a displacement /relative to the starting coordinate's own magnitude/
(=NelderMeadOptions::initial_simplex_scale= defaults to $0.05$) rather
than a fixed absolute step -- sensible for a starting point far from
the origin, but, as §4.2 shows, it also means a simplex born near a
small-magnitude starting point (any Rastrigin start close to an
integer coordinate) is tiny relative to that landscape's own ripple
period.

Each iteration sorts the $n+1$ vertices by objective value and computes
the centroid $c$ of every vertex /except/ the worst, then tries, in
order:

1. *Reflection*: $x_r = c + \texttt{reflection}\cdot(c - x_{\text{worst}})$
   (=reflection= defaults to $1.0$). If $x_r$ beats the best vertex,
   try *expansion*, $x_e = c + \texttt{expansion}\cdot(c -
   x_{\text{worst}})$ (=expansion= defaults to $2.0$), and keep
   whichever of $x_r$, $x_e$ is better.
2. Otherwise, if $x_r$ beats the /second/-worst vertex, accept the
   plain reflection $x_r$.
3. Otherwise *contract*: if $x_r$ still beat the worst vertex (the
   "outside" case), contract toward $c$ from $x_r$,
   $x_c = c + \texttt{contraction}\cdot(x_r - c)$; otherwise (the
   "inside" case) contract from the worst vertex itself,
   $x_c = c + \texttt{contraction}\cdot(x_{\text{worst}} - c)$
   (=contraction= defaults to $0.5$ either way). Accept $x_c$ if it
   beats the corresponding comparison point.
4. If even contraction fails, *shrink* the whole simplex toward the
   best vertex, $x_i \leftarrow x_{\text{best}} + \texttt{shrink}\cdot
   (x_i - x_{\text{best}})$ for every vertex but the best (=shrink=
   defaults to $0.5$) -- the only one of the four operations that costs
   $n$ evaluations rather than one.

The search stops once both the simplex's diameter (max distance from
the best vertex) and its value spread (worst minus best) fall below
=tolerance=. Every one of these four operations compares \(f\)-values
directly and never differences or scales them, which is exactly why
=NelderMead= needs no gradient -- but it is also why it is a *strictly
local* method: once every vertex has collapsed into one basin, nothing
in the algorithm can pull it back out, in sharp contrast to the
companion report's population methods (=PSO=, =DifferentialEvolution=,
=GeneticAlgorithm=), which maintain many independently-moving points
rather than one deforming simplex specifically so that some of them can
keep exploring other basins while others exploit a promising one.

** CMA-ES: adapting a global Gaussian search distribution

=CMAES= (Hansen & Ostermeier, Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution
Strategy) instead maintains an explicit multivariate Gaussian search
distribution, $N(\text{mean}, \sigma^2 C)$, and reshapes all three of
its parameters -- the mean, the global step size $\sigma$, and the full
covariance matrix $C$ -- generation by generation from the
best-performing sampled candidates. This report describes the
algorithm's structure and intuition at a level tied to the real
=datamunge= implementation; the full coefficient-by-coefficient
derivation belongs in Hansen's own tutorial (Hansen, 2016), cited
below rather than reproduced symbol-for-symbol here, since the
=datamunge= implementation follows it almost variable-for-variable.

Each generation samples $\lambda$ candidates from the current
distribution (via the covariance's eigendecomposition -- computed here
by a hand-rolled cyclic Jacobi eigensolver, since this project avoids
external linear-algebra dependencies entirely), evaluates all
$\lambda$, and selects the best $\mu=\lfloor\lambda/2\rfloor$. When
=population_size= is left at its default (set to =0=), =CMAES=
computes $\lambda = 4 + \lfloor 3\ln n\rfloor$ -- for both of §4's
two-dimensional objectives, $\lambda = 4 + \lfloor 3\ln 2\rfloor = 6$,
so $\mu=3$.

*Log-rank weighting.* The selected $\mu$ points are combined into the
new mean using weights that fall off with rank rather than treating
all $\mu$ survivors equally:

$$w_i \propto \ln(\mu+0.5) - \ln(i+1), \qquad i=1,\dots,\mu$$

(normalized so $\sum_i w_i = 1$). This is a log, not linear, decay --
the best-ranked survivor is weighted much more heavily than a small
linear taper would give it, while still letting every selected point
contribute something. Concretely, for this report's $\mu=3$ case, the
raw (pre-normalization) weights are $\ln(3.5)-\ln(1)=1.252763$,
$\ln(3.5)-\ln(2)=0.559616$, and $\ln(3.5)-\ln(3)=0.154151$, summing to
$1.966529$ and normalizing to $w_1=0.637043$, $w_2=0.284570$,
$w_3=0.078387$ -- the best of the three survivors gets roughly eight
times the weight of the worst. These weights also fix the "variance
effective selection mass" $\mu_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sum_i w_i^2 \approx
2.028611$, which in turn scales several of the adaptation-rate
constants (=c_sigma=, =c_c=, =c1=, =c_mu= in the source) that this
report does not re-derive in full.

*Cumulative step-size adaptation (CSA).* Beyond just moving the mean,
=CMAES= tracks an /evolution path/ $p_\sigma$: an exponentially-decayed
running sum of the (whitened) steps actually taken,

$$p_\sigma \leftarrow (1-c_\sigma)\,p_\sigma +
\sqrt{c_\sigma(2-c_\sigma)\mu_{\text{eff}}}\; C^{-1/2}\frac{\Delta
\text{mean}}{\sigma}.$$

The intuition this encodes: compare the /realized/ length of this
accumulated path to the length it would have under pure random
selection (no consistent good direction to move in). A
longer-than-expected path means consecutive generations' steps are
reinforcing each other -- the search is moving consistently in a good
direction -- so $\sigma$ should grow to cover more ground faster. A
shorter-than-expected path means steps are working against each other,
i.e. oscillating back and forth, so $\sigma$ should shrink. The source
implements this directly as $\sigma \leftarrow \sigma \exp\!\left(
\frac{c_\sigma}{d_\sigma}\left(\frac{\lVert p_\sigma\rVert}
{\texttt{expected\_norm}} - 1\right)\right)$, where =expected_norm= is
the closed-form expected norm of an \(n\)-dimensional standard Gaussian
vector, $\sqrt{n}(1 - \tfrac{1}{4n} + \tfrac{1}{21n^2})$. A second,
separate evolution path (=path_covariance=) drives the covariance
update itself, combining a rank-one term from its own path with a
rank-$\mu$ term built from the selected steps -- letting =CMAES= adapt
not just how far to step, but along which /correlated/ directions,
which is what makes it effective on rotated or ill-conditioned
landscapes that defeat axis-aligned population methods. The run stops
once $\sigma$ times the largest covariance axis length drops below
=tolerance=.

Unlike =NelderMead=, =CMAES= is a *population-based* method -- closer
in spirit to the companion report's =PSO= / =DifferentialEvolution= /
=GeneticAlgorithm= than to simplex search -- specifically because it
samples and evaluates $\lambda$ independent candidates every
generation rather than deforming one simplex. But, as §4.3's
sensitivity sweep shows directly, being population-based does not by
itself guarantee global behavior: it depends on the population being
large enough relative to the landscape's basin density.

* Data and Methods

** The objectives

This report deliberately reuses, unmodified, the companion report's
exact =BumpyFunction= ($f(x,y)=(x-3)^2+(y+1)^2+5\sin(x)\cos(y)$, whose
smooth bowl component alone is minimized at $(3,-1)$ but whose true
combined minimum, found by the companion report's
=SimulatedAnnealing=, is $f=-2.234513$ at $(4.191,-0.326)$) and
=RastriginFunction= ($f(x)=10n+\sum_i (x_i^2 - 10\cos(2\pi x_i))$,
global minimum $f=0$ at the origin, surrounded by a dense regular grid
of local minima) so that every number this report produces is directly
comparable to a number the companion report has already published,
without re-deriving or re-tuning either objective. Both methods run
from the identical starting points the companion report used: the
origin region for the bumpy function (this report uses $(0,0)$
exactly, matching =SimulatedAnnealing='s own start), and $(3,-4)$ for
Rastrigin, matching every one of the companion report's six
population-method configurations.

** Software

All computation uses =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module -- =NelderMead=,
=CMAES=, and (for the same-run comparison series) =PSO= -- together
with =optim::ArbitraryFunction=, the single interface every objective
in this report implements, and =plot::RPlot= for visualization.

#+name: optim-df-includes

#include <datamunge/optim/optim.hpp>
#include <datamunge/plot/plot.hpp>

#include <cmath>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>
#include <vector>

using namespace datamunge::optim;
using datamunge::plot::RPlot;

namespace {

class BumpyFunction : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double bowl =
            (x[0] - 3.0) * (x[0] - 3.0) + (x[1] + 1.0) * (x[1] + 1.0);
        const double ripples = 5.0 * std::sin(x[0]) * std::cos(x[1]);
        return bowl + ripples;
    }
};

class RastriginFunction : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        double total = 10.0 * static_cast<double>(x.size());
        for (const double xi : x) {
            total += xi * xi - 10.0 * std::cos(2.0 * M_PI * xi);
        }
        return total;
    }
};

// Logs every evaluate() call's objective value, for building a
// convergence-curve figure. Verbatim from optimization_analysis.org.
class LoggingArbitrary : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    LoggingArbitrary(ArbitraryFunction& inner, std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }

private:
    ArbitraryFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

std::vector<double> running_min(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::vector<double> out(v.size());
    double m = v.empty() ? 0.0 : v[0];
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        m = std::min(m, v[i]);
        out[i] = m;
    }
    return out;
}

std::vector<double> indices(std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> out(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) out[i] = static_cast<double>(i + 1);
    return out;
}

void print_vec(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << "[";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << (i ? ", " : "") << v[i];
    }
    std::cout << "]";
}

} // namespace
#+end_src

* Results

** NelderMead and CMAES on the bumpy function

#+begin_example
=== ArbitraryFunction: NelderMead on the bumpy function ===
NelderMead: f=-0.414926 x=[2.079339, -2.428494] (123 evaluations)

=== ArbitraryFunction: CMAES on the bumpy function ===
CMAES: f=-0.414926 x=[2.079339, -2.428494] (679 evaluations)
#+end_example

Started from the same point $(0,0)$ that the companion report's
=SimulatedAnnealing= used, both derivative-free methods here converge
to $f=-0.414926$ at $(2.079,-2.428)$ -- a real local minimum of the
bumpy function, but *not* the true combined minimum, $f=-2.234513$ at
$(4.191,-0.326)$, that =SimulatedAnnealing= found from the same start
in the companion report. What stands out is not that both methods miss
the global minimum (unsurprising -- neither has any mechanism
comparable to =SimulatedAnnealing='s Metropolis-driven willingness to
accept temporarily worsening moves), but that they land on the
/identical/ point to six decimal places: strong direct evidence that
both a strictly local simplex search and a population-sampling
covariance-adapting search, once each has fully converged, find the
same well-defined basin bottom precisely.

configuration in the companion report was, gets trapped almost
immediately. $f(3,-4)=25.000000$ exactly (both coordinates are integers,
so $\cos(2\pi x_i)=1$ and each term degenerates to $x_i^2-10$); 109
evaluations move the search only to $f=24.873845$ at
$(2.985,-3.980)$, a shift of a few hundredths in each coordinate. This
is the local-method failure mode §2.2 predicted: =initial_simplex_scale=
gives an initial simplex of size roughly $0.15\times0.2$ here, tiny
relative to Rastrigin's unit-period ripple grid, so the simplex is born
already confined to a single cell and every one of its four operations
can only ever compare points inside that cell.

=CMAES=, from the identical start and using its library-default
population, does substantially better -- reaching $(0.115,0.044)$,
inside the same grid cell as the true global minimum at the origin --
but does not reach $f=0$ exactly. §4.3 shows this default-population
result is highly sensitive to the random seed.

With the library-default population ($\lambda=6$, $\mu=3$ for this
two-dimensional problem), =CMAES='s outcome on Rastrigin swings across
the entire range this report has seen: seed 7 reaches the exact global
minimum, seed 2 stalls at $f=24.873845$ -- indistinguishable from
=NelderMead='s own failure in §4.2 -- and the other three seeds land
at intermediate values. Raising =population_size= to =40= (matching
=PSO='s own default population size in the companion report) removes
the sensitivity entirely: all three seeds tested reach $f=0.000000$
exactly. This is a real, seed-verified demonstration that =CMAES='s
default population-size formula, $4+\lfloor 3\ln n\rfloor$, is tuned
for efficiency on smooth, unimodal landscapes and is simply too small
to reliably cover Rastrigin's dense grid of local minima at $n=2$ --
not a defect in the adaptation mechanism itself, but a sizing choice
that needs revisiting for landscapes like this one.

* Discussion

**A strictly local method and a population-based method can converge
to the exact same point, which is informative rather than
surprising.** §4.1's six-decimal agreement between =NelderMead= and
=CMAES= on the bumpy function is not evidence that either method is
secretly more global than described in §2 -- both started in the same
basin of attraction relative to $(0,0)$, and a basin of a smooth
function has exactly one bottom, so any method that fully converges
within it necessarily finds the same point. The real information is in
what differs: =NelderMead= gets there in 123 evaluations against
=CMAES='s 679 -- about a fifth as many -- because deforming a single
simplex is simply cheaper per unit of local progress than resampling
and re-adapting an entire covariance-parameterized population, when
there is only one basin to find.

**Population-based does not automatically mean globally reliable --
population /size/ relative to the landscape's basin density is what
matters.** §4.3's sensitivity sweep is this report's central finding:
the same algorithm, same objective, same starting point, differing
only in random seed, ranges from a perfect global solve to a failure
indistinguishable from =NelderMead='s strictly local one. This directly
qualifies the framing in §2.3 and in the companion report -- being
"closer in spirit to =PSO= / =DifferentialEvolution= /
=GeneticAlgorithm=" than to simplex search is a statement about
/mechanism/ (sampling many
points, adapting a distribution), not a guarantee of the same
reliability those methods showed on Rastrigin at their own, larger,
default population sizes. =CMAES='s default population formula, tuned
primarily for per-generation efficiency on well-behaved landscapes, is
the direct cause: §4.3 shows that simply matching =PSO='s population
size removes the failure mode entirely, across every seed tested.

**A convergence curve plotted on a shared linear axis can visually
hide a real result if the methods' costs differ by orders of
magnitude.** §4.4's three-way figure is honest about every method's
real evaluation count, but =PSO='s 6081-evaluation budget compresses
=NelderMead='s 109 and =CMAES='s 637 into a sliver near the axis
origin -- exactly the same log-scale-versus-linear-scale tradeoff the
companion report's own bowl-convergence figure navigated with a
log-log axis. This report did not log-transform the axis here, since
zero and near-zero =f= values (the whole point of comparing final
accuracy on Rastrigin) do not survive a log transform the way the
companion report's strictly-positive quadratic-bowl values did; the
tradeoff is a genuinely harder one on this objective, not an oversight.

* Conclusion

Running =datamunge='s two derivative-free =ArbitraryFunction=
optimizers on the companion report's own =BumpyFunction= and
=RastriginFunction=, from its own starting points, produces real,
directly comparable numbers: =NelderMead= and =CMAES= converge to the
identical bumpy-function local minimum, $f=-0.414926$, roughly five
times apart in evaluation cost (123 versus 679); =NelderMead= gets
trapped in Rastrigin's grid almost immediately ($f=24.873845$ after
109 evaluations, barely moved
from the $f=25$ starting value); =CMAES= with its default population is
seed-dependent, ranging from an exact solve to a =NelderMead=-level
failure across five seeds; and enlarging =CMAES='s population to match
=PSO='s own default population size removes that sensitivity entirely,
reaching $f=0.000000$ on every seed tested. A same-run reproduction of
the companion report's =PSO= confirms its published $f=0.000000$ result
and adds its previously-unpublished evaluation count, 6081. As with its
companion, this entire report is reproducible end to end via =just
org-example optimization_derivative_free_analysis=, which re-executes
§3.3.7's assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above
-- from the =datamunge= source tree.
