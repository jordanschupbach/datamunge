#+begin_abstract
We fit Isomap (Tenenbaum, de Silva & Langford, 2000) to Fisher's (1936)
iris measurements and to a synthetic Swiss-roll dataset using
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::Isomap=, and report it as a literate program:
every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single
C++ binary that produced every number and figure in this document. On
iris, sweeping =n_neighbors= from 3 to 30 shows the symmetric
=k=-nearest-neighbor graph is /disconnected/ -- and =Isomap='s
constructor throws -- for every value from 3 through 24; it first
becomes connected once =n_neighbors= reaches =25=, where the fit
reaches a goodness of fit of 0.9449 and its first embedding dimension
correlates with the
[[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][companion MDS report]]'s own Euclidean
embedding at 0.9973 -- on this data, once Isomap is finally able to fit
at all, it recovers almost exactly the same picture straight-line MDS
does. The Swiss roll tells a sharper story: with =n_neighbors= set to
=5=, Isomap achieves a goodness of fit of 0.9191 and near-perfect
recovery of the withheld ground truth (\(|r|=0.9928\) between embedding
Dim1 and the true manifold position \(t\), \(|r|=0.9598\) between Dim2
and the true height), cleanly unrolling the roll into a flat rectangle
-- while plain PCA and classical Euclidean MDS, fit on the identical 3D
coordinates, both max out at \(|r|=0.2426\) against \(t\), never
exceeding what a straight line through the coils can see. But this
success is fragile: a full sweep from =n_neighbors= set to =4= up to
=25= shows goodness of fit and ground-truth correlation both falling
sharply and monotonically past =n_neighbors= set to =6=, collapsing to
\(|r|=0.12\)-\(0.15\) once =n_neighbors= reaches =20= -- a real,
direct demonstration of Isomap's classic "short-circuit" failure mode,
where a merely slightly-too-generous neighborhood starts stitching
together points on opposite sides of the roll that are close in
straight-line distance but far apart along the manifold. We derive the
geodesic-distance approximation, the symmetric \(k\)-nearest-neighbor
graph, all-pairs shortest paths, and the classical-MDS step Isomap
applies to the resulting geodesic distance matrix, tying each to the
corresponding library call.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The [[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][companion PCA report]] and the
[[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][companion MDS report]] both embed data
using distances measured in a /straight line/ through the original
feature space -- Euclidean distance for PCA's covariance matrix,
Euclidean or Manhattan distance for MDS. That choice is silently
optimistic: it assumes the data's "natural" distances really are the
straight-line ones. For data that lies on (or near) a curved surface
embedded in a higher-dimensional space -- a manifold -- straight-line
distance can be badly misleading: two points can sit close together in
raw coordinates while being far apart /along/ the surface, the way two
points on opposite windings of a rolled-up sheet of paper are close in
3D but far apart if you have to walk along the paper to get from one to
the other. =stats::Isomap= (Tenenbaum, de Silva & Langford, 2000)
addresses this directly: it approximates the geodesic (along-the-manifold)
distance between every pair of points using shortest paths
over a \(k\)-nearest-neighbor graph, then hands the resulting geodesic
distance matrix to exactly the same classical (Torgerson) MDS machinery
the [[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][MDS companion report]] already
derives -- Isomap is, precisely, "MDS on a smarter distance matrix."

As with its companion reports, this document is a /literate program/:
prose and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single
"assembling the program" step (§3.3.13) weaves every named chunk below
into one =.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure
from §4 onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives the geodesic-distance approximation, the symmetric \(k\)-
nearest-neighbor graph, Floyd-Warshall all-pairs shortest paths, and the
classical-MDS step applied to the resulting distances, each tied to the
specific =Isomap= method that implements it. §3 describes the program
chunk by chunk. §4 reports the iris =n_neighbors= sweep and its
disconnection failure, the resulting embedding and its relationship to
MDS, the Swiss-roll =n_neighbors= sweep and its short-circuit failure,
and the headline Swiss-roll comparison against PCA and MDS. §5 discusses
what these two failure modes have in common and why =n_neighbors= is
Isomap's single most consequential tuning parameter. §6 concludes.

* Background: Isomap

** Setup and notation

For iris: \(n = 150\) flowers, \(p = 4\) predictors (\(x_i \in
\mathbb{R}^4\): =Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=,
=Petal.Width=) -- and, as in the
[[file:pca_iris_analysis.org][PCA]]/[[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][MDS]]
companion reports, no response variable at all during fitting.
=Species= is retained separately in this report's own code and used
only afterward to color the embedding scatter. For the Swiss roll
(§3.3.7): \(n = 400\) points, \(p = 3\) coordinates (=x=, =y=, =z=), with
the /true/ 1-dimensional manifold position \(t\) and the true "height"
coordinate withheld from fitting entirely, used only afterward to check
whether the embedding recovered them.

** From straight-line distance to a \(k\)-nearest-neighbor graph

Like =stats::MDS=, =stats::Isomap= starts from an \(n \times n\)
pairwise distance matrix \(D\) computed directly from the given feature
columns (=IsomapOptions::metric=, Euclidean or Manhattan, Euclidean
throughout this report),

\[
D_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert_2 .
\]

Unlike MDS, Isomap does not hand \(D\) itself to the embedding step.
Instead, for every point \(i\) it keeps only the
=IsomapOptions::n_neighbors= smallest entries of row \(i\), building a
/symmetric/ \(k\)-nearest-neighbor graph \(G\): an edge \((i, j)\) with weight
\(D_{ij}\) is added whenever \(j\) is among \(i\)'s =n_neighbors=
nearest points (or vice versa, since the graph is built by adding an
edge from each point to each of its own nearest neighbors, which can
make a point's in-degree exceed =n_neighbors= if other points also
picked it). This is exactly =Isomap::fit='s own
=dstruct::WeightedGraph<std::size_t, double>= construction (§3.3, and
verified directly against =src/datamunge/stats/isomap.cpp=): each
vertex is connected to its =n_neighbors= closest /other/ points by
straight-line distance, and nothing else -- \(G\) only ever contains
edges between points that are already close in the raw feature space,
never a long-range edge.

** Geodesic distance via all-pairs shortest paths

The key idea: if =n_neighbors= is chosen well, a short hop in \(G\)
only ever connects two points that really are close /along/ the
manifold, so a multi-hop /path/ through \(G\) approximates walking
along the manifold's surface -- exactly the "along the rolled-up paper,"
not "through it," distance the Swiss roll's own geometry needs (§4.4
and §4.5 make this concrete: for the Swiss roll's coiled geometry,
straight-line distance frequently connects two points on entirely
different windings that are actually far apart along the sheet).
Isomap's
geodesic-distance estimate is the shortest-path distance over \(G\),

\[
D^{\text{geo}}_{ij} = \text{shortest path length from } i \text{ to } j
\text{ in } G ,
\]

computed by =Isomap::fit= via a direct call to
=dstruct::WeightedGraph::floyd_warshall()=, the same all-pairs
shortest-path solver this project's graph-algorithms code already
provides generally, not anything Isomap-specific. If \(G\) is
/disconnected/ --
some pair of points has no path between them at all, because
=n_neighbors= was too small to bridge every part of the data -- no
finite shortest-path distance exists for that pair, and =Isomap='s
constructor throws =std::invalid_argument= rather than silently
producing a meaningless embedding. §4.2 gives a real, concrete example
of exactly this happening; §4.4 shows a second, milder failure mode
that does not throw at all -- an =n_neighbors= that keeps the graph
connected but too large for the data's local curvature, silently
producing a poor embedding instead of an exception.

** Classical MDS on the geodesic distances

Once every pairwise geodesic distance is known, Isomap's remaining step
is /identical/ to the
[[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][MDS companion report]]'s own derivation
(its §2.3-2.4, double-centering and eigendecomposition), just applied
to \(D^{\text{geo}}\) instead of the raw straight-line \(D\): square
the geodesic distances, double-center,

\[
B_{ij} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\Big((D^{\text{geo}}_{ij})^2 -
\overline{(D^{\text{geo}})^2_{i \cdot}} -
\overline{(D^{\text{geo}})^2_{\cdot j}} +
\overline{(D^{\text{geo}})^2_{\cdot \cdot}}\Big) ,
\]

and eigendecompose \(B = V \Lambda V^\top\) via the same
=linalg::jacobi_eigen= solver the MDS and PCA reports both use, giving
eigenpairs \((\lambda_c, v_c)\) with \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq
\lambda_n\) (=eigenvalues()=, the full spectrum). The \(k\)-dimensional
embedding keeps the first =IsomapOptions::n_components= eigenpairs,

\[
\text{embedding}_{:,c} = v_c \sqrt{\max(\lambda_c, 0)} ,
\]

with goodness of fit defined exactly as in the MDS report,

\[
\text{gof} = \frac{\sum_{c=1}^{k} \max(\lambda_c, 0)}{\sum_{c :
\lambda_c > 0} \lambda_c} .
\]

The only real difference from classical MDS, mathematically, is /which
distance matrix/ goes into this pipeline -- but that one difference is
Isomap's entire point: geodesic distances can capture curvature that
straight-line distances cannot, at the cost of a new failure mode
(disconnection, and the short-circuit degradation of §4.4) straight-line
MDS never has to worry about.

** What we will report

We report the iris exploratory data (true species, for later visual
comparison), an =n_neighbors= sweep on iris showing the disconnection
failure directly, the fitted iris embedding and its relationship to
MDS, a synthetic Swiss-roll dataset with withheld ground truth, an
=n_neighbors= sweep on the roll showing goodness of fit and ground-truth
correlation both rising then collapsing, the headline successful
unrolling at the best =n_neighbors=, and a comparison against PCA and
MDS fit on the identical roll data.

* Data and Methods

** The data

=datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns Fisher's 150 flowers -- 50 each of
/setosa/, /versicolor/, /virginica/ -- measured on =Sepal.Length=,
=Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width= (cm), with no missing
values; =Species= is withheld from every =Isomap= constructor call and
used only for the exploratory and embedding figures. The Swiss roll is
generated in-program (§3.3.7) using the exact seeded formula shared
across this report series: \(n=400\) points, =std::mt19937_64= seeded
with 42, \(t \sim 1.5\pi(1 + 2u)\) for \(u \sim
\text{Uniform}(0,1)\) (the /true/ 1D manifold position), a true
"height" coordinate \(\sim 21u\), and observed 3D coordinates
\(x = t\cos t\), \(y = \text{height}\), \(z = t\sin t\). Only \(x, y,
z\) are given to any model; \(t\) and =height= are withheld and used
only afterward, in §4.4-4.5, to check recovery.

** Software

The entire analysis is one C++ program against =datamunge=:
=dstruct::DataFrame= for the data, =stats::Isomap= for the model (and,
for §4.5's comparison, =stats::PCA= and =stats::MDS=), and =plot::RPlot=
for every figure. No external plotting or statistics package is used.

// Pearson correlation between two equal-length vectors.
static double correlation(const std::vector<double>& a,
                           const std::vector<double>& b) {
    const std::size_t n = a.size();
    double ma = 0.0, mb = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) { ma += a[i]; mb += b[i]; }
    ma /= n; mb /= n;
    double dot = 0.0, na = 0.0, nb = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        const double da = a[i] - ma, db = b[i] - mb;
        dot += da * db; na += da * da; nb += db * db;
    }
    return dot / std::sqrt(na * nb);
}

// |r| of each of the embedding's first two dims against a ground-truth
// vector, allowing for the axis-swap/sign-flip ambiguity any eigen-
// decomposition has -- returns {|r| for dim 0, |r| for dim 1}.
static std::pair<double, double> axis_correlations(
        const datamunge::linalg::DenseMatrix<double>& emb,
        const std::vector<std::size_t>& kept,
        const std::vector<double>& truth) {
    std::vector<double> d0(kept.size()), d1(kept.size()), tv(kept.size());
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < kept.size(); ++i) {
        d0[i] = emb(i, 0);
        d1[i] = emb(i, 1);
        tv[i] = truth[kept[i]];
    }
    return {std::abs(correlation(d0, tv)), std::abs(correlation(d1, tv))};
}

    std::cout << "\n=== Swiss roll (n=400, seed 42) ===\n";
    const std::size_t n = 400;
    std::mt19937_64 rng(42);
    std::uniform_real_distribution<double> u(0.0, 1.0);
    std::vector<double> t(n), height(n), x(n), y(n), z(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        t[i] = 1.5 * M_PI * (1.0 + 2.0 * u(rng));
        height[i] = 21.0 * u(rng);
        x[i] = t[i] * std::cos(t[i]);
        y[i] = height[i];
        z[i] = t[i] * std::sin(t[i]);
    }
    DataFrame roll;
    roll.add_column("x", x);
    roll.add_column("y", y);
    roll.add_column("z", z);
    const std::vector<std::string> roll_features{"x", "y", "z"};

** Swiss roll: =n_neighbors= sweep and the short-circuit failure mode

#+begin_example
=== Swiss roll (n=400, seed 42) ===

=== Isomap n_neighbors sweep on Swiss roll (ground-truth correlations) ===
     k       gof    |r| vs t  |r| vs height
     4    0.8915      0.9913          0.8843
     5    0.9191      0.9928          0.9598
     6    0.7723      0.8947          0.2171
     7    0.6320      0.7146          0.1056
     8    0.6318      0.6770          0.0747
    10    0.5811      0.4441          0.0697
    12    0.5682      0.4120          0.0713
    15    0.5488      0.2018          0.0379
    20    0.5650      0.1499          0.0363
    25    0.6003      0.1203          0.0415
#+end_example

Unlike iris, every one of these =n_neighbors= values keeps the roll's
graph connected -- but goodness of fit and ground-truth correlation both
tell a strikingly different story than "larger =n_neighbors= is safer."
Performance /peaks/ when =n_neighbors= is =5= (goodness of fit 0.9191,
\(|r|=0.9928\) against \(t\)) and then falls off a cliff: once
=n_neighbors= reaches =6=, correlation against =height= has already
collapsed from 0.9598 to 0.2171, and by =n_neighbors= set to =15= or
=25= correlation against \(t\) itself has fallen to 0.12-0.20, little
better than noise. This is
Isomap's classic /short-circuit/ failure mode, and it is the mirror
image of §4.2's iris disconnection failure: with too few neighbors, the
graph cannot connect at all; with even a modestly /larger/ neighborhood
than the roll's local curvature can tolerate, some of the new edges
silently jump across adjacent windings of the roll -- geometrically
short in 3D, but nowhere close to short along the actual sheet -- and
every shortest path that uses one of those edges reports a geodesic
distance that is far too small. A handful of bad edges is enough to
corrupt the entire distance matrix, since Floyd-Warshall's shortest
paths route through whichever edges are cheapest, short-circuit or not.

* Discussion

**Two mirror-image failure modes, one root cause.** §4.2's iris
disconnection and §4.4's Swiss-roll short-circuit look like opposite
problems -- one from too few neighbors, one from (relatively) too many
-- but both trace back to the same thing: =n_neighbors= controls a
tradeoff between /connectivity/ (can every point reach every other
point at all) and /local validity/ (does every edge in the graph
actually respect the manifold's true local geometry), and there is no
universal "safe" value. Iris needed 25 neighbors just to connect; the
Swiss roll needed fewer than 6 to avoid corrupting its own geometry. A
practitioner cannot know in advance which regime a new dataset falls
into without doing exactly the kind of sweep §4.2 and §4.4 both run.

**When Isomap and MDS agree, it is because Isomap's own geodesic
distances have quietly become straight-line distances.** §4.3's high
Isomap/MDS correlation (0.9973) is not evidence that Isomap and MDS are
"basically the same method" -- it is evidence that, with =n_neighbors=
set to =25= on a compact 150-point dataset, the \(k\)-nearest-neighbor
graph is so
dense that almost every shortest path is a single direct edge, so
\(D^{\text{geo}} \approx D\). §4.5's Swiss-roll comparison is the
control case that shows what Isomap can do when that approximation
/isn't/ trivially true: a small, well-chosen =n_neighbors= keeps
\(D^{\text{geo}}\) genuinely different from \(D\), and only then does
Isomap outperform MDS by the wide margin (\(|r|=0.99\) vs. \(|r|=0.24\))
the Swiss roll is specifically designed to reveal.

**Goodness of fit alone would not have caught the short-circuit
failure as clearly as the ground-truth correlation did.** §4.4's
goodness-of-fit column falls from 0.92 to about 0.55-0.60 as
=n_neighbors= grows, a real signal -- but a practitioner without access
to ground truth (the normal case) might read 0.55-0.60 as "a moderately
successful embedding" rather than "an embedding that has lost almost
all its ability to recover the true manifold coordinates," which is
what the correlation columns show far more starkly. This is a genuine
limitation of goodness of fit as Isomap's only self-reported diagnostic:
it measures how much of \(B\)'s spectral mass the retained dimensions
capture, not whether \(B\) itself was built from valid geodesic
distances in the first place.

* Conclusion

Fitting =datamunge='s C++ =stats::Isomap= to Fisher's iris measurements
shows a real, direct disconnection failure across essentially the
entire practical range of =n_neighbors= (3 through 24), first resolving
only once =n_neighbors= reaches =25=, where the resulting embedding
closely tracks (correlation 0.9973) the
[[file:mds_iris_analysis.org][companion MDS report]]'s own Euclidean
embedding -- unsurprising, since a =k=-nearest-neighbor graph that
dense makes geodesic distance nearly indistinguishable from
straight-line distance. The Swiss-roll experiment shows the opposite
and more interesting regime: with =n_neighbors= set to =5=, Isomap
achieves near-perfect, cleanly axis-separated recovery of the withheld
ground truth (\(|r|=0.9928\) against \(t\),
\(|r|=0.9598\) against =height=) and visibly unrolls the roll into a flat
rectangle, while PCA and MDS on the identical data both max out at
\(|r|=0.2426\) -- a genuine demonstration of nonlinear manifold learning
succeeding exactly where linear methods are expected to fail. But a
full =n_neighbors= sweep shows this success is narrow and fragile: both
goodness of fit and ground-truth correlation collapse sharply for
=n_neighbors= past about 6, a real short-circuit failure mode with
concrete numbers and a visibly tangled embedding to match, not a
hypothetical caveat. As with its companions, this entire report is
reproducible end to end via =just org-example isomap_iris_analysis=,
which re-executes §3.3.13's assembly block -- and therefore every
number and figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
