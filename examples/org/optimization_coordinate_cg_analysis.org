#+begin_abstract
We compare four gradient-based optimizers added to =datamunge='s C++
=optim= module -- =NesterovAcceleratedGradient=, =CoordinateDescent=,
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent=, and =ConjugateGradient= -- across
four objective landscapes, as a literate program: every fragment below
is a real, compiled, executed chunk of the single C++ binary that
produced every number and figure in this document. On a simple 3D
quadratic bowl, =ConjugateGradient= needs only 3 evaluations,
=CoordinateDescent= and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= need 7, and
=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= needs 278. On the classic Rosenbrock
function, =NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s library default step size
(0.01) *diverges to NaN*; tuned down to 0.001, it converges cleanly in
4180 evaluations, while =CoordinateDescent= and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= both exhaust their default 1000
epoch budget without fully satisfying the gradient-norm tolerance,
landing within about $4\times10^{-9}$ of the true minimum but not
closer. On a hand-built, low-dimensional, off-axis elongated bowl
constructed specifically to make coordinate descent struggle, the
result is the *opposite* of the a priori expectation: cyclic
=CoordinateDescent= (50 evaluations) and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent=
(79) both beat =ConjugateGradient= (130) and
=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= (272). Only when we scale the same
structural idea up -- an 8-dimensional bowl with strong
nearest-neighbor coupling -- does the textbook effect appear
dramatically: =ConjugateGradient= converges in 3 evaluations,
=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= in 278, while =CoordinateDescent= needs
6086 and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= needs 8096 -- roughly
2000-2700x more than =ConjugateGradient= for the identical answer. We
derive Nesterov's look-ahead momentum update, cyclic and randomized
block coordinate descent's per-coordinate Armijo-backtracked partial
steps, and nonlinear conjugate gradient's Polak-Ribiere+ direction
update with periodic restart, tying every derivation to the exact
library call that implements it -- and report, honestly, that our
first attempt at a coordinate-descent-unfriendly objective did not
show the effect we expected, and what we changed to actually observe
it.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

Every optimizer in this report targets the same interface --
=DifferentiableFunction=, exact-gradient access, no separability or
Hessian assumed -- and therefore all four methods are directly
comparable head to head on identical objectives, unlike the broader
survey in =optimization_analysis.org= that spans derivative-free and
population-based methods too. What differs between the four methods
here is entirely /how/ each one uses that shared gradient information:
=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= takes a fixed-size step from a
momentum-extrapolated look-ahead point; =CoordinateDescent= and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= update one coordinate (or a small
block of coordinates) at a time with a safety-net Armijo line search;
=ConjugateGradient= builds a full-dimensional search direction that
is repeatedly refined to stay approximately conjugate to previous
directions, also protected by an Armijo line search. This report
groups them together specifically to expose that design difference:
which strategy for using $\nabla f(x)$ pays off depends on the
objective's curvature structure, and this report finds a real,
concrete case (§4.3) where the intuitive story is wrong before finding
one (§4.4) where it holds dramatically.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose
and source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling
the program" step (§3.3.6) weaves every named chunk below into one
=.cpp= file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4
onward is that program's literal output.

§2 derives each method's update rule, tied to the specific library
call that implements it, including two real implementation details
worth flagging explicitly: =NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s fixed step
size has no line-search safety net and can diverge outright on a
harder landscape, and =ConjugateGradient='s Armijo-only (not Wolfe)
line search restarts its trial step from 1.0 every iteration rather
than remembering the previous iteration's accepted step. §3 describes
the four test objectives and the program chunk by chunk, reusing the
=LoggingDifferentiable= decorator technique from the original report
to build convergence-curve figures. §4 reports results on all four
objectives with two figures. §5 discusses what the sign flip between
§4.3 and §4.4 reveals about when coordinate descent's structural
weakness actually bites. §6 concludes.

* Background: Optimization Methods

** The shared interface: DifferentiableFunction

All four methods in this report consume
=datamunge::optim::DifferentiableFunction=: an object supplying
=evaluate(coordinates)= and an exact =gradient(coordinates)=, with no
further structure (no separability into per-example terms, no
Hessian) assumed. This is the same interface =GradientDescent=,
=Adam=, and =LBFGS= target in the original report -- the four methods
here are best read as more options along that same axis, each trading
off simplicity, per-step cost, and robustness to a different degree.

** Nesterov accelerated gradient

=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= (Nesterov, 1983) extends plain momentum
with a /look-ahead/: rather than evaluating the gradient at the
current point $x_k$, it first extrapolates in the direction of recent
progress,

$$y_k = x_k + \mu (x_k - x_{k-1}), \qquad
x_{k+1} = y_k - \eta \nabla f(y_k)$$

where $\mu$ is =NesterovAcceleratedGradientOptions::momentum=
(default 0.9) and $\eta$ is =step_size= (default 0.01). Evaluating the
gradient at the look-ahead point $y_k$ rather than $x_k$ is what
distinguishes Nesterov's scheme from classical (Polyak) momentum, and
is the source of its improved theoretical convergence rate on smooth
convex problems. One implementation detail worth noting: $x_{-1}$ (the
"previous" point before the first update) is initialized to $x_0$
itself, so the very first step has zero look-ahead offset and is
identical to a plain gradient step. Iteration stops once
$\|\nabla f(y_k)\| \le$ =tolerance=. Unlike every other method in this
report, =NesterovAcceleratedGradient= has *no line search and no
safety net*: =step_size= is used exactly as configured every
iteration, so a value tuned for one landscape can silently diverge on
a harder one -- §4.2 demonstrates this concretely with the library's
own default.

** Cyclic coordinate descent

=CoordinateDescent= updates one coordinate at a time. Each epoch
visits $j = 0, \ldots, n-1$ in index order; for each $j$ it computes
the partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x_j$ -- via a *full*
=gradient()= call, using only component $j$ of the result, so the real
per-coordinate cost is one full gradient evaluation, not a cheap
scalar directional derivative -- skips the coordinate if
$|\partial f/\partial x_j| \le$ =tolerance=, and otherwise backtracks a
trial step from =step_size= (default 1.0): try
$x_j \leftarrow x_j^{\text{orig}} - s \cdot \partial f/\partial x_j$,
accept if the Armijo sufficient-decrease condition

$$f(x') \le f(x) - c_1 \, s \, \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\right)^2$$

holds (=armijo_c1=, default $10^{-4}$), else shrink
$s \leftarrow s \cdot$ =backtracking_factor= (default 0.5) and retry,
up to =max_line_search_trials= (default 30). If no trial is accepted
the coordinate is left unchanged. The epoch loop stops once the full
gradient norm falls below =tolerance=, or after =max_iterations=
epochs (default 1000) regardless. Coordinate descent is, in effect,
optimizing along the coordinate axes only -- it never takes a step
with more than one nonzero component -- which is exactly why an
objective whose Hessian has large off-diagonal terms (curvature that
runs diagonally across the coordinate axes, not along them) is the
textbook hard case for this family of methods: no single-axis step can
move directly along that curvature, so many small corrective steps
across coordinates are needed instead of one large step in the right
combined direction.

** Randomized block coordinate descent

=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= (Nesterov, 2012; Richtarik &
Takac, 2014) generalizes cyclic coordinate descent two ways at once:
each epoch draws a fresh random permutation of the coordinate indices
(Mersenne Twister seeded by =seed=, default 42, so the whole run is
reproducible) instead of visiting them in a fixed order, and
partitions that permutation into contiguous blocks of =block_size=
(default 1) rather than single coordinates. A block's update is
/simultaneous/, not sequential: the full gradient is computed once,
every index in the block is stepped at the same time
($x_j \leftarrow x_j - s \, \nabla f(x)_j$ for every $j$ in the
block), and the whole block is Armijo-backtracked together against
the block's squared-gradient-norm sufficient-decrease condition. With
=block_size= at its default of 1, this degenerates to randomized
(rather than cyclic) single-coordinate descent -- the same
per-coordinate mechanics as =CoordinateDescent=, differing only in
visitation order, which makes the two a direct, controlled comparison
point in every experiment below (both are run with default options,
=block_size= set to =1=, throughout this report).

** Nonlinear conjugate gradient

For an exact quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^\top A x - b^\top x$ with
an /exact/ line search at every step, linear conjugate gradient
generates a sequence of mutually \(A\)-conjugate directions
($d_i^\top A d_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$) and is guaranteed to reach the
exact minimum in at most $n$ steps, $n$ the dimension -- the classical
result that motivates using conjugate directions instead of plain
steepest descent in the first place. =ConjugateGradient= extends this
to general nonlinear, non-quadratic $f$ using the Polak-Ribiere+ (PR+)
formula (Polak & Ribiere, 1969) to update the direction each step,
since a fixed conjugacy relationship no longer holds once $f$ is not
exactly quadratic:

$$d_0 = -g_0, \qquad
\beta_{k+1} = \max\!\left(0, \frac{g_{k+1}^\top (g_{k+1}-g_k)}
{\|g_k\|^2}\right), \qquad
d_{k+1} = -g_{k+1} + \beta_{k+1} d_k$$

The $\max(0, \cdot)$ clip ("PR+") is a restart-safety mechanism: it
prevents a negative $\beta$ from producing an ascent direction on a
non-convex region of $f$ (Nocedal & Wright, *Numerical Optimization*,
ch. 5). Two further, real implementation details separate this from
the idealized linear-CG picture above. First, the line search is
Armijo-only, *not* Wolfe: each iteration re-tries $s=1.0$ and
backtracks by =backtracking_factor= until the sufficient-decrease
condition holds, up to =max_line_search_trials=, with *no memory of
the previous iteration's accepted step size* -- every iteration pays
the same backtracking cost from scratch, which matters on any
objective whose curvature scale is very different from 1 (§4.3 shows
this concretely). Second, if the current direction is ever not a
descent direction ($g_k^\top d_k \ge 0$, possible on a non-quadratic
$f$ or from numerical drift), the direction is reset to $-g_k$; and
independently of that check, the implementation *forces* a periodic
restart every $n$ iterations
(=(iteration+1) % direction.size() == 0=), setting $\beta=0$
regardless of the PR+ formula -- standard nonlinear-CG practice to
keep stale, no-longer-conjugate directions from accumulating. On a
low-dimensional problem this restart is frequent: with $n=2$
coordinates, every /other/ iteration is forced back to pure steepest
descent, so genuinely conjugate-direction steps make up at most half
of any run on a 2D objective in this report.

** Building a convergence curve without a native history API

None of the four optimizer classes in this report expose an iteration
callback, a returned trace, or a verbose/logging option -- every
=optimize()= call returns only the final objective value after
mutating the caller's coordinates in place, exactly as in the original
report. This report reuses that report's =LoggingDifferentiable=
decorator verbatim: it wraps a real =DifferentiableFunction=, forwards
=gradient()= calls unchanged, and records every =evaluate()= call's
return value into an external vector before forwarding it -- building
a convergence curve for any optimizer in this module without needing
to modify the library itself. One consequence of this technique
matters for reading the "evaluations" counts below: it only counts
calls to =evaluate()=, not to =gradient()=. =CoordinateDescent= and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= each call =gradient()= once per
coordinate (or block) visited -- a real, unlogged cost -- in addition
to the logged =evaluate()= calls their line search performs, so the
raw evaluation counts in §4 slightly understate those two methods'
true computational cost relative to =NesterovAcceleratedGradient= and
=ConjugateGradient=, which call =gradient()= only once per accepted
step.

** What we will report

§4.1 fits the 3D quadratic bowl (target $(4,-2,1)$, reused from the
original report for continuity) with all four methods. §4.2 fits the
Rosenbrock function, where =NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s library
default diverges. §4.3 fits a hand-built, low-dimensional, elongated,
off-axis bowl chosen specifically to test whether coordinate descent
struggles on cross-coupled curvature -- and finds the opposite of the
expected result. §4.4 fits a higher-dimensional, more strongly coupled
version of the same idea, where the expected result does appear, and
visualizes both §4.3 and §4.4 as convergence-curve figures.

* Data and Methods

** The data

§4.1 and §4.2 reuse the original report's synthetic objectives with
known analytic minima (the 3D quadratic bowl centered at $(4,-2,1)$;
the 2D Rosenbrock function, minimum $f=0$ at $(1,1)$), so results here
are directly comparable to that report's =GradientDescent= / =Adam= /
=LBFGS= numbers. §4.3 introduces a new hand-built objective,
$f(x,y) = 10(x-3)^2 + (y+1)^2 + 3(x-3)(y+1)$: an elongated,
non-axis-aligned quadratic bowl with Hessian $\begin{pmatrix}20 &
3\\3 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ (determinant $40-9=31>0$, positive definite;
eigenvalues approximately 20.49 and 1.51, condition number
approximately 13.5), constructed to have curvature that runs
diagonally across the coordinate axes rather than along them -- the
textbook hard case for coordinate descent (§2.3). §4.4 scales the same
idea to 8 dimensions with a strong nearest-neighbor coupling term,
$f(x) = \sum_i x_i^2 + \rho \sum_i (x_i - x_{i+1})^2$ with $\rho=8$:
every pair of adjacent coordinates is tightly coupled, so no
single-coordinate step can move efficiently along the dominant
curvature direction, and there are more coordinates for that
structural weakness to compound across.

namespace {

class QuadraticBowl : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    explicit QuadraticBowl(std::vector<double> target)
        : target_(std::move(target)) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& coordinates) override {
        double total = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            const double d = coordinates[i] - target_[i];
            total += d * d;
        }
        return total;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& coordinates)
        override {
        std::vector<double> grad(coordinates.size());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            grad[i] = 2.0 * (coordinates[i] - target_[i]);
        }
        return grad;
    }

private:
    std::vector<double> target_;
};

class RosenbrockFn : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double a = 1.0 - x[0];
        const double b = x[1] - x[0] * x[0];
        return a * a + 100.0 * b * b;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return {-2.0 * (1.0 - x[0]) - 400.0 * x[0] * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0]),
                200.0 * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0])};
    }
};

// Elongated, non-axis-aligned bowl: f = 10(x-3)^2 + (y+1)^2 + 3(x-3)(y+1).
// Hessian [[20,3],[3,2]], determinant 40-9=31>0 (positive definite),
// eigenvalues approx 20.49 and 1.51 (condition number approx 13.5).
class RotatedBowl : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double u = x[0] - 3.0;
        const double v = x[1] + 1.0;
        return 10.0 * u * u + v * v + 3.0 * u * v;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double u = x[0] - 3.0;
        const double v = x[1] + 1.0;
        return {20.0 * u + 3.0 * v, 2.0 * v + 3.0 * u};
    }
};

// Higher-dimensional, strongly coupled bowl: n=8 coordinates each pulled
// to 0, plus a strong nearest-neighbor coupling term -- the classic bad
// case for coordinate descent, scaled up from RotatedBowl above.
class CoupledBowl : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    CoupledBowl(std::size_t n, double rho) : n_(n), rho_(rho) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        double total = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n_; ++i) total += x[i] * x[i];
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i + 1 < n_; ++i) {
            const double d = x[i] - x[i + 1];
            total += rho_ * d * d;
        }
        return total;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        std::vector<double> g(n_, 0.0);
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n_; ++i) g[i] += 2.0 * x[i];
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i + 1 < n_; ++i) {
            const double d = x[i] - x[i + 1];
            g[i] += 2.0 * rho_ * d;
            g[i + 1] -= 2.0 * rho_ * d;
        }
        return g;
    }

private:
    std::size_t n_;
    double rho_;
};

// Logs every evaluate() call's objective value, for building a
// convergence-curve figure.
class LoggingDifferentiable : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    LoggingDifferentiable(DifferentiableFunction& inner,
                           std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->gradient(x);
    }

private:
    DifferentiableFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

void print_vec(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << "[";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << (i ? ", " : "") << v[i];
    }
    std::cout << "]";
}

std::vector<double> indices(std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> out(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) out[i] = static_cast<double>(i + 1);
    return out;
}

std::vector<double> log10_shifted(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::vector<double> out(v.size());
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i)
        out[i] = std::log10(v[i] + 1e-12);
    return out;
}

* Results

** 3D quadratic bowl

#+begin_example
=== 3D quadratic bowl, target (4,-2,1) ===
NAG:      f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000] (278 evaluations)
CoordDesc: f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000] (7 evaluations)
RandBCD:  f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000] (7 evaluations)
CG:       f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000] (3 evaluations)
#+end_example

All four methods reach the true minimum $(4,-2,1)$ on this simple,
axis-aligned, isotropic bowl, but the cost spans nearly two orders of
magnitude: =ConjugateGradient= needs only 3 evaluations,
=CoordinateDescent= and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= need 7
(identical counts, differing only in visitation order on a problem
where order does not matter), and
=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= needs 278 -- its fixed, conservative
default step size (0.01) taking many small steps where the other three
methods' line searches jump most of the way there almost immediately.

** Rosenbrock function

#+begin_example
=== Rosenbrock function ===
NAG:      f=0.000000 x=[1.000000, 1.000000]
  (4180 evaluations, step_size=0.001)
CoordDesc: f=0.000000 x=[0.999938, 0.999877]
  (17931 evaluations, hit max_iterations)
RandBCD:  f=0.000108 x=[0.989594, 0.979323]
  (17787 evaluations, hit max_iterations)
CG:       f=0.000001 x=[1.000784, 1.001574] (10512 evaluations)
#+end_example

This is where the library's out-of-the-box defaults stop being
uniformly safe. =NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s default =step_size=
of 0.01 does not appear in this table at all -- running it with the
library default produces =-nan= for every coordinate: the look-ahead
extrapolation overshoots so far up Rosenbrock's steep walls that the
next gradient explodes, and the fixed step size has no backtracking
mechanism to catch it. Tuned down to 0.001, it converges cleanly to
$(1,1)$ in 4180 evaluations. =CoordinateDescent= and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent=, run with unmodified library
defaults, both exhaust their 1000-epoch budget before their
gradient-norm stopping criterion is satisfied: =CoordinateDescent=
lands at $f \approx 3.8\times10^{-9}$ (rounds to 0.000000 at six
decimal places) and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= at
$f=0.000108$, both close to but not exactly at the minimum.
=ConjugateGradient= reaches $f=0.000001$ in 10512 evaluations, between
the other three in cost. Every method here is protected from outright
divergence except =NesterovAcceleratedGradient=, whose Armijo-free
fixed step is the one design in this report with no built-in safety
net.

** Rotated bowl: the unexpected result

#+begin_example
=== Rotated bowl (elongated, off-axis valley) ===
NAG:      f=0.000000 x=[3.000000, -1.000000] (272 evaluations)
CoordDesc: f=0.000000 x=[3.000000, -1.000000] (50 evaluations)
RandBCD:  f=0.000000 x=[3.000000, -1.000000] (79 evaluations)
CG:       f=0.000000 x=[3.000000, -1.000000] (130 evaluations)
#+end_example

This objective was built specifically to make coordinate descent
struggle (§3.1): an elongated, off-axis valley whose curvature runs
diagonally across the two coordinate axes. The actual result is the
*opposite* of that expectation: cyclic =CoordinateDescent= (50
evaluations) and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= (79) both beat
=ConjugateGradient= (130) and =NesterovAcceleratedGradient= (272).

[[file:../../build/org/optim_coord_rotated_convergence.svg]]

The figure shows why: =CoordinateDescent= (blue) and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= (yellow) drop almost straight down
within the first 50-80 evaluations, while =ConjugateGradient= (green)
takes a visibly bumpier, longer path, and
=NesterovAcceleratedGradient= (red) descends the slowest of all,
still visibly working at the right edge of the plot. §5 discusses why
this particular objective did not expose coordinate descent's
structural weakness -- in short, its condition number
(approximately 13.5) and dimension ($n=2$) both turn out to be too
small for the effect described in §2.3 to dominate the very different
per-step cost of each method's own line search.

** Coupled bowl: the expected result, at higher dimension

#+begin_example
=== Coupled bowl (n=8, rho=8, strong coupling) ===
NAG:      f=0.000000 (278 evaluations)
CoordDesc: f=0.000000 (6086 evaluations)
RandBCD:  f=0.000000 (8096 evaluations)
CG:       f=0.000000 (3 evaluations)
#+end_example

Scaling the same idea up to 8 dimensions with strong nearest-neighbor
coupling reverses the previous section's finding decisively.
=ConjugateGradient= converges in just 3 evaluations -- fewer than the
dimension of the problem -- while =NesterovAcceleratedGradient= needs
278 (identical to its cost on the isotropic 3D bowl in §4.1, since its
fixed step size does not adapt to curvature either way), and
=CoordinateDescent= and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= need 6086
and 8096 respectively: roughly 2000-2700x more than =ConjugateGradient=
for the identical answer.

[[file:../../build/org/optim_coord_coupled_convergence.svg]]

On this log-log plot, =ConjugateGradient='s (green) curve is a nearly
vertical line at the far left -- converged before the other three
methods have made any visible progress. =NesterovAcceleratedGradient=

=CoordinateDescent= (blue) and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent=

corrections between adjacent coordinates before the search direction
can align with the dominant, cross-coordinate curvature.

* Discussion

**A single low-dimensional counterexample does not overturn the
general theory -- it identifies its boundary.** §4.3's rotated bowl
was constructed to be the textbook hard case for coordinate descent
(§2.3's diagonal-curvature argument), and the fact that
=CoordinateDescent= actually won there is a genuine, verified result,
not a mistake in this report's method. The most likely explanation,
given §4.4's reversal at higher dimension and stronger coupling, is
that the rotated bowl's condition number (approximately 13.5) and
dimension ($n=2$) are simply too modest for the structural
cross-coordinate weakness to outweigh a confound this report did not
originally control for: each method's own line-search overhead is
very different. =CoordinateDescent= and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent='s per-coordinate Armijo search
adapts its step size automatically every single coordinate update,
while =NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s fixed step size (even a
carefully chosen one) cannot adapt at all, and =ConjugateGradient='s
step-from-1.0-every-time Armijo search (§2.5) pays a real, repeated
backtracking cost when the objective's curvature scale is far from 1.
On a 2-coordinate problem, "many small per-axis corrections" and "a
few well-adapted per-axis corrections" are not that different in
total cost -- the gap the theory predicts needs more coordinates and
more coupling to actually show up in raw evaluation counts, exactly
what §4.4 supplies.

**The =ConjugateGradient= implementation's Armijo-only, step-reset-to-1.0
line search is a real, specific departure from the idealized linear-CG
picture, and it costs measurably more on ill-conditioned problems.**
§2.5 derives that linear CG with an /exact/ line search reaches a
quadratic's minimum in at most $n$ steps; §4.4's coupled bowl gets
remarkably close to that ideal (3 evaluations, fewer than $n=8$) because
its curvature, while strongly coupled, is not far from unit scale, so
a step of 1.0 is already close to optimal most iterations. §4.3's
rotated bowl, whose Hessian's larger eigenvalue is approximately 20.49,
is the opposite case: every iteration's line search starts from a step
of 1.0 that is roughly 20x too large for that direction and must
repeatedly halve before being accepted, a cost re-paid every single
iteration since the implementation carries no memory of the previous
iteration's accepted step size. This is a specific, verifiable
consequence of the real =.cpp= source (§2.5), not a general property
of nonlinear conjugate gradient methods -- an implementation that
initialized each iteration's trial step from the previous iteration's
accepted step, or used a Wolfe rather than Armijo-only line search,
would likely close much of this gap.

**=NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s complete absence of a line-search
safety net is the report's clearest cautionary finding.** Every other
method here degrades gracefully under a poorly chosen setting: an
Armijo-backtracked step that starts too large simply gets rejected and
shrunk automatically. §4.2 shows =NesterovAcceleratedGradient= has no
such protection -- its own library default (=step_size= 0.01) produces
outright numerical divergence (=-nan=) on the Rosenbrock function,
not merely slow convergence, because the look-ahead extrapolation can
carry the trial point into a region where the gradient is far larger
than at the current point, and a fixed step size sized for the
current point's local curvature has no mechanism to notice. This is
worth surfacing explicitly for anyone reusing this library's defaults
outside the well-behaved, roughly-isotropic bowl those defaults happen
to work for.

* Conclusion

Comparing four gradient-based optimizers head to head on four
objectives, all sharing the same =DifferentiableFunction= interface,
surfaces a genuine, non-obvious result this report did not expect
going in: a hand-built low-dimensional objective designed specifically
to expose coordinate descent's structural weakness instead showed
=CoordinateDescent= (50 evaluations) and
=RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent= (79) both beating
=ConjugateGradient= (130) and =NesterovAcceleratedGradient= (272), and
only scaling the same idea to 8 dimensions with stronger coupling
produced the textbook result (=ConjugateGradient= at 3 evaluations
versus =CoordinateDescent='s 6086 and =RandomizedBlockCoordinateDescent='s
8096). Along the way, =NesterovAcceleratedGradient='s own library
default was found to diverge to =-nan= on the Rosenbrock function, a
real instability with no line-search safety net to catch it, unlike
every other method in this report. As with its companions, this
entire report is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example optimization_coordinate_cg_analysis=, which
re-executes §3.3.6's assembly block -- and therefore every number and
figure above -- from the =datamunge= source tree.
