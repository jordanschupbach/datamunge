We optimize five deliberately different objective landscapes using
every method in =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module, and report it as a
literate program: every fragment below is a real, compiled, executed
chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and figure in
this document. On a simple three-dimensional quadratic bowl with a
known minimum, gradient-based =GradientDescent=, =Adam=, and =LBFGS=
all find it, but with a dramatic difference in cost: LBFGS needs only 3
function evaluations, =GradientDescent= needs 59, and full-batch
=Adam= needs 7874 -- nearly three orders of magnitude more than LBFGS
for the same answer, visualized directly on a log-log convergence plot.
On the classic Rosenbrock "banana" function, LBFGS's convergence curve
shows the textbook signature of navigating a curved valley: an initial
overshoot from the line search, followed by a long, gradually
accelerating descent to the true minimum. Mini-batch =SGD= on an
ordinary-least-squares loss recovers weights
$(-2.505, 1.771, -1.336)$ within a few hundredths of the closed-form OLS
solution $(-2.525, 1.776, -1.339)$ computed by this project's own =LM=
class. Derivative-free =SimulatedAnnealing= finds $f=-2.235$ on a bumpy,
non-convex landscape -- correctly moving *away* from its smooth bowl
component's exact center to exploit a nearby ripple, since the true
combined minimum is not at the bowl's own minimum. Finally, five of six
population-based configurations (two =PSO= topologies, two
=DifferentialEvolution= strategies, one =GeneticAlgorithm= configuration)
find the Rastrigin function's global minimum ($f=0$) essentially
exactly, while a sixth (rank selection, uniform crossover, elitism

every derivation to the exact library call that implements it.

* Introduction

Every method in this report solves the same abstract problem -- find
$x^* = \arg\min_x f(x)$ -- but assumes a different amount of information
is available about $f$, and each assumption unlocks a different family
of algorithm. =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module organizes its optimizers
around exactly this distinction, via three function-type interfaces:
=ArbitraryFunction= (only point evaluations -- the most general and
least informative case), =DifferentiableFunction= (evaluations plus an
exact gradient), and =DifferentiableSeparableFunction=
(=DifferentiableFunction= that additionally decomposes as a sum of
per-example terms, the shape every empirical-risk-minimization loss in
machine learning takes). Every optimizer in this report targets exactly
one of these three interfaces, and the report is organized the same
way: gradient-based methods first, then the separable-sum case
mini-batch methods are built for, then derivative-free methods, ending
with population-based methods built for landscapes riddled with local
minima that defeat every earlier method in this report.

As with its companions, this document is a /literate program/: prose and
source are interleaved in reading order, and a single "assembling the
program" step (§3.3.7) weaves every named chunk below into one =.cpp=
file, compiles it, and runs it. Every number and figure from §4 onward is
that program's literal output.

§2 derives every method's update rule, each tied to the specific
library call that implements it. §3 describes the program chunk by
chunk, including the function-call-logging technique used to build this
report's convergence-curve figures (no optimizer in this module exposes
an iteration history directly). §4 reports the results of all eight
methods alongside three figures. §5 discusses what the evaluation-count
gap between LBFGS and Adam, the Rosenbrock convergence curve's shape,
and the elitism-off GA's stall each reveal. §6 concludes.

* Background: Optimization Methods

** The function-type hierarchy

Every optimizer in =datamunge::optim= targets one of three interfaces.
=ArbitraryFunction= supplies only =evaluate(coordinates)=, the minimum
possible information; =DifferentiableFunction= additionally supplies
=gradient(coordinates)=, the exact gradient $\nabla f(x)$;
=DifferentiableSeparableFunction= further requires the objective to
decompose as $f(x) = \sum_i f_i(x)$ (=evaluate_term= / =gradient_term=
per index $i$), the shape of any empirical loss defined as a sum over
training examples. This report constructs one custom subclass of each
interface -- a quadratic bowl and the Rosenbrock function
(=DifferentiableFunction=), an ordinary-least-squares loss
(=DifferentiableSeparableFunction=), and a bumpy multimodal function
plus the Rastrigin function (=ArbitraryFunction=) -- and fits each with
every optimizer built for its interface.

** Gradient descent and momentum

=GradientDescent= is the textbook update $x \leftarrow x - \eta\nabla
f(x)$, with an optional momentum term (=GradientDescentOptions::momentum=,
0 by default) that accumulates a velocity $v \leftarrow \mu v -
\eta\nabla f(x)$, $x \leftarrow x + v$ to damp oscillation across narrow
valleys. It stops once the gradient norm or the per-iteration objective
change falls below =tolerance=. Simple and general, at the cost of
being highly sensitive to the step size $\eta$ and to the objective's
conditioning (how differently it curves along different directions).

** Adam

=Adam= (Kingma & Ba, 2015) maintains per-coordinate running estimates of
the gradient's first and second moments,

$$m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\nabla f(x), \qquad
v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)(\nabla f(x))^2$$

bias-corrects them ($\hat{m} = m/(1-\beta_1^t)$, $\hat{v} =
v/(1-\beta_2^t)$), and updates $x \leftarrow x - \eta\,\hat{m} /
(\sqrt{\hat{v}}+\epsilon)$ -- an adaptive per-coordinate step size that
made Adam the default choice for training deep neural networks, whose
loss landscapes are far less well-behaved than this report's convex
quadratic bowl. =datamunge='s =Adam= runs full-batch (the whole
objective, not a sampled mini-batch, gradient every step) with defaults
tuned for exactly the noisy-stochastic-gradient setting Adam was
designed for -- a small step size (=step_size= set to =0.001=) intended to be
robust across many different problems, at the direct cost of needing
many more iterations than a method like LBFGS on a well-behaved,
noise-free objective like this report's bowl, exactly the tradeoff §4.1
measures.

** LBFGS

=LBFGS= (limited-memory BFGS, Nocedal 1980) is a quasi-Newton method:
rather than computing or storing the full Hessian, it approximates the
inverse Hessian's action on the gradient using only the last
=history_size= (curvature, gradient-change) pairs via the two-loop
recursion, combined with an Armijo-Wolfe backtracking line search to
choose a safe step length along that direction. This captures much of
Newton's method's fast (locally superlinear) convergence near a
well-behaved minimum without ever forming or inverting a Hessian
matrix, which is why it needs so many fewer function evaluations than
first-order methods like plain gradient descent or Adam on a smooth,
well-conditioned objective -- exactly what §4.1's quadratic bowl and
§4.2's Rosenbrock function both are, despite the latter's famously
curved, narrow valley.

** Mini-batch SGD

=SGD= targets =DifferentiableSeparableFunction=, computing a gradient
from only a random mini-batch of terms per step
(=SGDOptions::batch_size=) rather than the full sum -- far cheaper per
step when the number of terms is large, at the cost of a noisier
gradient estimate. It runs for =max_epochs= full passes over the data
(optionally =shuffle='d each epoch) until the full-objective change
between epochs falls below =tolerance=. §4.3 uses it to fit an ordinary
least-squares regression, the textbook use case, and checks its result
directly against this project's own closed-form =stats::LM= solution
for the identical formula.

** Simulated annealing

=SimulatedAnnealing= needs only =ArbitraryFunction::evaluate()=. It
proposes a random perturbation $x' = x + \sigma z$ ($z\sim N(0,I)$,
=step_std_dev= $=\sigma$), and *always* accepts an improving move, but
also accepts a *worsening* move with Metropolis probability
$\exp(-\Delta f / T)$ where $T$ is the current temperature -- allowing
the search to escape shallow local minima early on, when $T$ is high.
$T$ cools geometrically ($T \leftarrow T\cdot\text{cooling\_rate}$)
every iteration, so the algorithm becomes progressively greedier as it
runs, converging toward pure hill-climbing by the end; the best point
found over the whole run (not necessarily the final point) is returned.

** Population-based methods: PSO, differential evolution, and the genetic algorithm

All three remaining methods maintain an entire *population* of
candidate points rather than a single running estimate, and are built
specifically for landscapes riddled with local minima (like §4.5's
Rastrigin function) where every method above risks getting stuck in the
first basin it finds.

=PSO= (particle swarm optimization) gives each particle a velocity
pulled toward both its own best-found position and the best position
found by its neighborhood -- =topology= set to ="global"= (every particle shares
one swarm-wide best, faster convergence but more prone to premature
convergence) or ="ring"= (each particle only sees a local
neighborhood of size =ring_neighbors=, slower but more resistant to
getting stuck) -- with an inertia weight that can stay constant or
decay linearly (=inertia_strategy=) to shift the swarm from exploration
early on toward exploitation later.

=DifferentialEvolution= mutates each population member by adding a
scaled difference of *other* population members' vectors --
=mutation_strategy= set to ="rand1"= perturbs a random member by the scaled
difference of two other random members ($x_r + F(x_{r_1}-x_{r_2})$, the
classic DE/rand/1 form), while ="best1"= perturbs the current best
member instead (DE/best/1, more exploitative) -- then recombines the
mutant with the original via binomial (independent per-coordinate coin
flip) or exponential (a single contiguous run of coordinates)
crossover.

=GeneticAlgorithm= selects parents (=selection_strategy= set to ="tournament"=:
best of a random group of =tournament_size=; ="rank"=: probability
proportional to rank rather than raw fitness, a common fix for premature
convergence when fitness values are very unevenly scaled), recombines
them (=crossover_strategy= set to ="blend"=: BLX-$\alpha$
interpolation/extrapolation between parent genes; ="uniform"=: each gene independently
inherited from either parent), and mutates offspring genes with
Gaussian noise. =elitism= (on by default, preserving the best
=elite_count= individuals unchanged into the next generation) guarantees
the population's best fitness never gets worse from one generation to
the next -- §4.5 tests turning it off.

** Building a convergence curve without a native history API

None of the eight optimizer classes in this module expose an iteration
callback, a returned trace, or a verbose/logging option -- every
=optimize()= call returns only the final objective value after mutating
the caller's coordinates in place. Since every function-type interface
here is director-enabled (subclassable, exactly the extension point
=ArbitraryFunction='s own documentation describes), this report builds
convergence curves by wrapping each objective in a thin logging
decorator that records every =evaluate()= call's return value into an
external vector before forwarding to the real objective -- a technique
that works for any optimizer in this module without needing to modify
the library itself.

** What we will report

§4.1 fits the quadratic bowl with GradientDescent, Adam, and LBFGS and
visualizes their logged convergence curves together on one log-log
plot. §4.2 fits the Rosenbrock function with LBFGS and visualizes its
convergence curve alone. §4.3 fits the OLS loss with SGD and compares
against closed-form =LM=. §4.4 fits the bumpy function with simulated
annealing. §4.5 fits the Rastrigin function with six population-method
configurations and visualizes three representative best-so-far
convergence curves.

* Data and Methods

** The data

§4.1 and §4.2 use purely synthetic objectives with known analytic
minima (a 3D quadratic bowl centered at $(4,-2,1)$; the 2D Rosenbrock
function, minimum $f=0$ at $(1,1)$) chosen so every optimizer's result
can be checked directly against ground truth. §4.3 reuses the iris
dataset's =Sepal.Length= / =Sepal.Width= / =Petal.Length= columns already
familiar from this project's regression reports, standardized before
fitting (unnormalized predictors of very different scales give the
loss an ill-conditioned Hessian, which plain constant-step SGD handles
poorly -- standard practice for gradient-based optimizers, not a
workaround specific to this report). §4.4 and §4.5 use synthetic
multimodal objectives (a bowl-plus-ripple function; the Rastrigin
function, minimum $f=0$ at the origin) chosen specifically to defeat
methods that assume a single basin of attraction.

** Software

All computation uses =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module --
=GradientDescent=, =Adam=, =LBFGS=, =SGD=, =SimulatedAnnealing=, =PSO=,
=DifferentialEvolution=, and =GeneticAlgorithm= -- together with
=optim::ArbitraryFunction= / =DifferentiableFunction= /
=DifferentiableSeparableFunction= (the three interfaces every custom
objective in this report implements), =stats::LM= for the closed-form
comparison, and =plot::RPlot= for visualization.

class QuadraticBowl : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    explicit QuadraticBowl(std::vector<double> target)
        : target_(std::move(target)) {}

    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& coordinates) override {
        double total = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            const double d = coordinates[i] - target_[i];
            total += d * d;
        }
        return total;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& coordinates)
        override {
        std::vector<double> grad(coordinates.size());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < coordinates.size(); ++i) {
            grad[i] = 2.0 * (coordinates[i] - target_[i]);
        }
        return grad;
    }

private:
    std::vector<double> target_;
};

class RosenbrockFn : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double a = 1.0 - x[0];
        const double b = x[1] - x[0] * x[0];
        return a * a + 100.0 * b * b;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return {-2.0 * (1.0 - x[0]) - 400.0 * x[0] * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0]),
                200.0 * (x[1] - x[0] * x[0])};
    }
};

class LinearRegressionLoss : public DifferentiableSeparableFunction {
public:
    LinearRegressionLoss(std::vector<std::vector<double>> X,
                          std::vector<double> y)
        : X_(std::move(X)), y_(std::move(y)) {}

    [[nodiscard]] std::size_t num_functions() const override {
        return y_.size();
    }

    double evaluate_term(const std::vector<double>& w,
                          const std::size_t i) override {
        const double err = predict(w, i) - y_[i];
        return err * err;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient_term(const std::vector<double>& w,
                                       const std::size_t i) override {
        const double err = predict(w, i) - y_[i];
        std::vector<double> grad(w.size());
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < w.size(); ++j) {
            grad[j] = 2.0 * err * X_[i][j];
        }
        return grad;
    }

private:
    double predict(const std::vector<double>& w, const std::size_t i) const {
        double pred = 0.0;
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < w.size(); ++j) pred += w[j] * X_[i][j];
        return pred;
    }
    std::vector<std::vector<double>> X_;
    std::vector<double> y_;
};

class BumpyFunction : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double bowl =
            (x[0] - 3.0) * (x[0] - 3.0) + (x[1] + 1.0) * (x[1] + 1.0);
        const double ripples = 5.0 * std::sin(x[0]) * std::cos(x[1]);
        return bowl + ripples;
    }
};

class RastriginFunction : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        double total = 10.0 * static_cast<double>(x.size());
        for (const double xi : x) {
            total += xi * xi - 10.0 * std::cos(2.0 * M_PI * xi);
        }
        return total;
    }
};

// Logs every evaluate() call's objective value, for building a
// convergence-curve figure.
class LoggingDifferentiable : public DifferentiableFunction {
public:
    LoggingDifferentiable(DifferentiableFunction& inner,
                           std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }
    std::vector<double> gradient(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        return inner_->gradient(x);
    }

private:
    DifferentiableFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

class LoggingArbitrary : public ArbitraryFunction {
public:
    LoggingArbitrary(ArbitraryFunction& inner, std::vector<double>& log)
        : inner_(&inner), log_(&log) {}
    double evaluate(const std::vector<double>& x) override {
        const double v = inner_->evaluate(x);
        log_->push_back(v);
        return v;
    }

private:
    ArbitraryFunction* inner_;
    std::vector<double>* log_;
};

std::vector<double> running_min(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::vector<double> out(v.size());
    double m = v.empty() ? 0.0 : v[0];
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        m = std::min(m, v[i]);
        out[i] = m;
    }
    return out;
}

std::vector<double> indices(std::size_t n) {
    std::vector<double> out(n);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) out[i] = static_cast<double>(i + 1);
    return out;
}

void print_vec(const std::vector<double>& v) {
    std::cout << "[";
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < v.size(); ++i) {
        std::cout << (i ? ", " : "") << v[i];
    }
    std::cout << "]";
}

#+name: optim-sgd
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\n=== DifferentiableSeparableFunction:"
                 " SGD vs. closed-form LM ===\n";
    {
        const auto iris = datamunge::datasets::iris();
        std::vector<double> sepal_length(iris.nrows());
        std::vector<double> sepal_width(iris.nrows());
        std::vector<double> petal_length(iris.nrows());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < iris.nrows(); ++i) {
            sepal_length[i] = iris.double_at("Sepal.Length", i);
            sepal_width[i] = iris.double_at("Sepal.Width", i);
            petal_length[i] = iris.double_at("Petal.Length", i);
        }
        auto mean_of = [](const std::vector<double>& v) {
            double s = 0.0;
            for (const double x : v) s += x;
            return s / static_cast<double>(v.size());
        };
        auto stddev_of = [](const std::vector<double>& v, const double mean) {
            double s = 0.0;
            for (const double x : v) s += (x - mean) * (x - mean);
            return std::sqrt(s / static_cast<double>(v.size()));
        };
        const double mean1 = mean_of(sepal_length);
        const double std1 = stddev_of(sepal_length, mean1);
        const double mean2 = mean_of(sepal_width);
        const double std2 = stddev_of(sepal_width, mean2);

        std::vector<std::vector<double>> X;
        X.reserve(iris.nrows());
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < iris.nrows(); ++i) {
            X.push_back({1.0, (sepal_length[i] - mean1) / std1,
                          (sepal_width[i] - mean2) / std2});
        }
        LinearRegressionLoss loss(X, petal_length);

        std::vector<double> w_std{0.0, 0.0, 0.0};
        SGDOptions sgd_options;
        sgd_options.step_size = 0.01;
        sgd_options.max_epochs = 300;
        sgd_options.batch_size = 8;
        SGD(sgd_options).optimize(loss, w_std);

        const std::vector<double> w{
            w_std[0] - w_std[1] * mean1 / std1 - w_std[2] * mean2 / std2,
            w_std[1] / std1, w_std[2] / std2};

** Three optimizers, one bowl

#+begin_example

  (59 evaluations)
Adam:             f=0.000001 x=[3.998870, -2.000000, 1.000000]

LBFGS:            f=0.000000 x=[4.000000, -2.000000, 1.000000]

#+end_example

All three optimizers converge to the true minimum $(4,-2,1)$, but the
cost differs dramatically: LBFGS needs only 3 function evaluations,
=GradientDescent= needs 59 (about 20x more), and =Adam= needs 7874 --
roughly 2600x more than LBFGS for the same well-behaved, noise-free
convex objective.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Log-log convergence plot comparing GradientDescent, Adam, and LBFGS on the quadratic bowl :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/optim_bowl_convergence.svg]]

The log-log plot makes the evaluation-count gap directly visible: LBFGS's
(green) and GradientDescent's (red) curves both fall steeply within the
first hundred or so evaluations, while Adam's (blue) curve stays nearly
flat for thousands of evaluations before finally beginning its own
descent -- exactly the small, cautious per-step behavior Adam's
defaults are tuned for, appropriate for noisy stochastic gradients but
needlessly conservative on this report's noise-free, well-conditioned
objective.

** LBFGS on Rosenbrock

#+begin_example
f=0.000000 x=[1.000000, 1.000001]
  (true minimum: f=0 at [1, 1], 61 evaluations)
#+end_example

LBFGS reaches the true minimum $(1,1)$ in 61 evaluations on this
substantially harder, curved landscape -- about 20x more than it needed
on the simple bowl, but still far fewer than =GradientDescent= needed on
the /easier/ bowl in §4.1.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt LBFGS convergence curve on the Rosenbrock function, showing an initial overshoot followed by descent into the curved valley :width 100%
[[file:../../build/org/optim_rosenbrock_convergence.svg]]

The convergence curve shows the textbook signature of optimizing along
Rosenbrock's narrow, curved valley: an initial sharp *increase* in $f$
(the line search's first trial step overshoots, a normal and expected
part of Armijo-Wolfe backtracking, not a failure), followed by a longer
descent into and along the valley floor, with a second smaller bump
partway through where the search direction has to bend to follow the
valley's curvature, before a final rapid convergence once close enough
to the minimum for LBFGS's local quadratic model to become accurate.

** Derivative-free simulated annealing

#+begin_example
f=-2.234513 x=[4.191193, -0.326305]
  (found without ever computing a gradient)
#+end_example

The bumpy objective's smooth "bowl" component alone is minimized at
$(3,-1)$ with value 0, but simulated annealing instead settles at

* Discussion

**LBFGS's roughly 2600x evaluation-count advantage over Adam on the
same convex bowl is not a criticism of Adam -- it is a direct
consequence of the two methods solving different, though overlapping,
problems.** LBFGS assumes an exact, noise-free gradient and a locally
well-approximated quadratic landscape, exactly this report's bowl;
Adam's defaults assume a noisy, possibly non-stationary gradient
(minibatch training loss in a deep network, its original setting), and
budget many small, cautious steps to average out that noise safely.
Applying Adam to a small, smooth, noise-free problem like §4.1's bowl
is using a tool built for a harder problem than the one at hand -- the
gap says more about matching the tool to the problem's actual
difficulty than about either method's intrinsic quality.

**A convergence curve's shape carries real diagnostic information a
single final number does not.** §4.2's Rosenbrock convergence curve's
initial spike is not evidence of anything wrong -- it is the expected
signature of a line search's first, unconstrained trial step
overshooting on a landscape whose curvature changes sharply nearby.
Recognizing this shape (rather than mistaking it for divergence) is
part of reading an optimizer's convergence log correctly; a bare final
answer, without the trajectory that produced it, would not distinguish
this expected, self-correcting overshoot from a genuinely
problematic run.

**Elitism's absence, not the choice of selection or crossover strategy
per se, is what caused §4.5's one non-convergent case.** The
=GeneticAlgorithm= configuration that stalled changed three things at
once relative to the fully-converged configuration (selection strategy,
crossover strategy, and elitism), so this report cannot isolate which
change mattered from the numbers alone -- but elitism's specific
guarantee (the population's best fitness never regresses between
generations) is exactly the property whose absence would allow a
population to drift away from a basin it had already found, which
matches the observed outcome closely enough to be the more likely
explanation than either strategy substitution alone. Confirming this
would need an isolated ablation (only elitism toggled, holding selection
and crossover fixed) as the natural follow-up experiment.

* Conclusion

Optimizing five deliberately different objective landscapes with every
method in =datamunge='s C++ =optim= module surfaces real, measurable
differences in cost and behavior that the closed-form or ground-truth
answer alone would not: LBFGS needs roughly 2600x fewer evaluations than
Adam on an identical convex bowl; its convergence curve on the harder
Rosenbrock function shows a real, expected line-search overshoot rather
than a failure; SGD matches closed-form OLS to within a small margin on
a dataset too small to actually need it; simulated annealing correctly
finds a combined minimum measurably displaced from its smooth
component's own center; and five of six population-based configurations
converge to the Rastrigin function's exact global minimum, with the
one exception plausibly attributable to elitism being disabled. As
with its companions, this entire report is reproducible end to end via
=just org-example optimization_analysis=, which re-executes §3.3.7's
assembly block -- and therefore every number and figure above -- from
the =datamunge= source tree.
