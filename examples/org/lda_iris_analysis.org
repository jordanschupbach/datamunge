#+begin_abstract
We fit a classical linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to Fisher's (1936) iris
data -- 150 flowers, three species, four floral measurements -- using
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::LDA= class, and report the resulting classifier as a
literate program: every code fragment below is a real, compiled, executed
chunk of the single C++ binary that produced every number and figure in this
document. The fitted model separates the three species with 98.00% training
accuracy, misclassifying only 3 of 150 flowers, all of them between
/versicolor/ and /virginica/; a single derived axis (LD1) already carries
99.12% of the between/within-class variance ratio, meaning the three-species,
four-measurement classification problem is, in a precise sense, almost
one-dimensional. We derive LDA from both its Bayesian and Fisher-projection
formulations, connect each formula to the corresponding library call, and
close with a discussion of the model's assumptions and limits.
#+end_abstract

* Introduction

The iris flower dataset, collected by Edgar Anderson and popularized by
Ronald Fisher's 1936 paper introducing linear discriminant analysis, is the
oldest continuously-used benchmark in statistical classification. Fifty
specimens each of /Iris setosa/, /Iris versicolor/, and /Iris virginica/ were
measured on four characters -- sepal length, sepal width, petal length, and
petal width, all in centimeters -- and the applied question is simple to
state: given only these four numbers, which species is the flower?

This report answers that question with LDA, and does so as a /literate
program/ in Knuth's sense: the source code and the prose explaining it are
interleaved in the order a reader should encounter them, not the order a
compiler needs them. A final "assembling the program" step (§3.3.7) stitches
every fragment below into one =.cpp= file, in the order shown, compiles it,
and runs it; the numbers and figures quoted throughout §4 are its literal
output, regenerated fresh every time this document is exported.

The rest of the report proceeds as follows: §2 derives LDA from first
principles, in both its Bayesian and geometric forms, and connects the
formulas to the specific library calls that implement them; §3 describes the
data and walks through the program chunk by chunk; §4 reports and interprets
the results; §5 discusses the model's assumptions and limitations; §6
concludes.

* Background: Linear Discriminant Analysis

** Setup and notation

We observe $n=150$ labeled flowers $(x_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, where
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^4$ collects the four measurements and
$y_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, $K = 3$, labels the species. Class $k$ has $n_k =
50$ members, an empirical prior $\hat\pi_k = n_k / n$, and a sample mean
$\hat\mu_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i : y_i = k} x_i$. Classical LDA makes one
strong simplifying assumption that gives the method its name: every class is
multivariate normal around its own mean but shares a single, common
(''pooled'') covariance matrix $\Sigma$, estimated by pooling the
within-class residuals over all $K$ classes. This is precisely what
=datamunge='s =stats::LDA= assumes -- see the class-level documentation in
[[file:../../include/datamunge/stats/lda.hpp][=include/datamunge/stats/lda.hpp=]]
-- and what distinguishes LDA from quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA),
which fits a separate $\Sigma_k$ per class and is not covered here.

** The Bayes decision rule

Bayes' rule states that the classifier with the lowest possible error rate
assigns $x$ to $\arg\max_k P(Y = k \mid X = x)$. Under the shared-covariance
Gaussian assumption above, Bayes' theorem gives
$P(Y = k \mid X = x) \propto \pi_k f_k(x)$ with
$f_k(x) = (2\pi)^{-p/2}|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 (x-\mu_k)^\top
\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_k)\big)$. Taking logs, every term that does not depend on
$k$ -- including the quadratic form $x^\top \Sigma^{-1} x$, since $\Sigma$ is
shared -- cancels out of the comparison across classes, leaving a
/discriminant function that is linear in/ $x$:

\[
\delta_k(x) \;=\; x^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu_k \;-\; \tfrac12\,\mu_k^\top
\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k \;+\; \log \pi_k .
\]

The predicted class is $\arg\max_k \delta_k(x)$, and the class posteriors are
recovered by exponentiating and normalizing:
$P(Y=k\mid x) = \exp(\delta_k(x)) / \sum_j \exp(\delta_j(x))$. This is
exactly what =LDA::predict_detail()= returns as
=LDAPrediction::posterior=: internally, the class stores the two pieces of
$\delta_k$ that don't depend on $x$ -- $\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k$ and
$\mu_k^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k$ -- once at fit time (as =precision_means_= and
=mahalanobis_offset_=), so classifying a new row is a single dot product per
class, not a matrix solve.

** Fisher's projection view

Fisher's original 1936 derivation reaches the same classifier from a
completely different, non-probabilistic starting point: find the linear
projection $w$ that maximizes the ratio of between-class to within-class
variance of the projected scores $w^\top x$,

\[
J(w) \;=\; \frac{w^\top \Sigma_B w}{w^\top \Sigma_W w}, \qquad
\Sigma_B = \sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k(\hat\mu_k - \bar x)(\hat\mu_k - \bar x)^\top,
\qquad
\Sigma_W = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i \,:\, y_i = k} (x_i - \hat\mu_k)(x_i -
\hat\mu_k)^\top .
\]

The stationary points of $J$ are the eigenvectors of $\Sigma_W^{-1}\Sigma_B$,
equivalently the solutions of the generalized eigenproblem
$\Sigma_B w = \lambda \Sigma_W w$; ranking them by eigenvalue $\lambda$ gives
an ordered set of /discriminant axes/ LD1, LD2, .... Because $\Sigma_B$ has
rank at most $K - 1$, there are at most $\min(p, K-1)$ axes with nonzero
eigenvalue -- here $\min(4, 2) = 2$, so the whole four-dimensional problem
reduces to two discriminant axes, LD1 and LD2. This is exactly
=LDA::scaling()= (a $p \times \text{num\_discriminants}$ matrix whose columns
are the $w$'s) and =LDA::proportion_of_trace()= (the eigenvalues, normalized
to sum to 1). Remarkably, the Bayes decision boundary of §2.2 and Fisher's
projection axes describe the /same/ classifier: this is why a single =LDA=
object in the library is simultaneously a probabilistic classifier
(=predict=, =predict_detail=) and a supervised two-dimensional embedding
(=plot_discriminants=) -- §4.4 uses both views on the same fitted model.

** What we will report

Given the fit, four diagnostics tell the whole story: the /group means/ and
/discriminant coefficients/ themselves, which are directly interpretable
(the larger $|w_j|$ is, the more classification weight predictor $j$
carries); the /proportion of trace/, which says whether one axis alone
already does most of the separating; a /training confusion matrix/ and
/training accuracy/; and a /discriminant-space scatter/ (LD1 vs LD2, colored
by true class) that turns the same linear algebra into a picture. Training
(resubstitution) accuracy is an optimistic estimate of how the model would
generalize -- we return to this in §5.

* Data and Methods

** The data

=datamunge::datasets::iris()= returns the same 150 rows in the same
sepal/petal centimeter units as R's built-in =datasets::iris=: 50 flowers
each of /setosa/, /versicolor/, and /virginica/, with columns
=Sepal.Length=, =Sepal.Width=, =Petal.Length=, =Petal.Width=, and =Species=.
There are no missing values.

** Software

The entire analysis -- loading the data, exploratory figures, model fit,
diagnostics, and every plot -- is one C++ program built against
=datamunge=: =dstruct::DataFrame= holds the tabular data,
=stats::LDA= (formula-driven, like R's =MASS::lda=) fits the model, and
=plot::RPlot=/=plot::RLayout=, an R-base-graphics-style SVG renderer built
into the library, draws every figure. No external plotting or statistics
package is used anywhere in this document.

*** Fitting the model

=stats::LDA= takes a =DataFrame= and an R-style formula whose left-hand side
is the categorical outcome; the right-hand side supports the same term
syntax as =stats::LM= (interactions, transforms, categorical predictors),
though here it is just the four raw measurements. =print_summary= writes an
R =MASS::lda=-style report: priors, group means, discriminant coefficients,
and proportion of trace, all covered in §2.

#+name: lda-fit
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    LDA model(iris, kFormula);
    std::cout << "\n";
    model.print_summary(std::cout);
#+end_src

*** Training diagnostics

=confusion_matrix()= cross-tabulates actual versus predicted class on the
training set (rows = actual, columns = predicted, both ordered by
=classes()=); =training_accuracy()= is its diagonal sum over $n$.

#+name: lda-confusion
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    std::cout << "\nConfusion matrix (rows = actual, cols = predicted):\n";
    const auto cm = model.confusion_matrix();
    std::cout << std::setw(14) << "";
    for (const auto& c : model.classes()) std::cout << std::setw(12) << c;
    std::cout << "\n";

Because §4.2 will show that LD1 alone captures the overwhelming majority of
the discriminant variance, it is worth isolating: we recompute each
training row's LD1 score via =predict_detail= and group it by true species

#+name: lda-ld1-boxplot
#+begin_src c++ :eval no
    {
        std::vector<std::vector<double>> ld1_by_class(kSpecies.size());
        const auto detail = model.predict_detail(iris);
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < detail.class_label.size(); ++i) {
            const std::string actual = iris.string_column("Species").at(i);
            for (std::size_t k = 0; k < kSpecies.size(); ++k) {
                if (actual == kSpecies[k]) {
                    ld1_by_class[k].push_back(detail.discriminants[i][0]);
                    break;
                }
            }
        }
        auto ld1_box = RPlot::boxplot(ld1_by_class, kSpecies, {96, 165, 250});
        ld1_box.title("First Discriminant (LD1) by Species")
            .y_label("LD1 score")
            .background({250, 250, 252});
        ld1_box.save_svg("build/org/lda_iris_ld1_boxplot.svg");
        std::cout << "wrote lda_iris_ld1_boxplot.svg\n";
    }

* Discussion

The pooled-covariance assumption that gives LDA its name and its linear
decision boundaries is only ever an approximation; it does not hold exactly
here, since /versicolor/ and /virginica/ do not share precisely the same
covariance structure in the raw measurements. That mismatch is almost
certainly the direct cause of the residual overlap in §4.4 -- and of the fact
that all three training errors, and the one genuinely ambiguous new flower
in §4.5, involve exactly that pair of species and no other. A quadratic
discriminant analysis (QDA), which fits a separate covariance per class,
would relax this assumption at the cost of the closed-form, two-axis
picture that makes LDA's output so directly interpretable -- and on a
dataset this small and this separable, is unlikely to change the practical
answer.

A second caveat concerns the 98.00% figure itself: it is a /resubstitution/
(training-set) accuracy, which is optimistic by construction because the
same 150 rows were used to estimate $\hat\mu_k$ and $\hat\Sigma$ and to score
the model. LDA has no tunable complexity/regularization knob to overfit
with, and iris is famously close to linearly separable, so the gap between
this number and a properly held-out or cross-validated estimate is expected
to be small -- but this report does not compute that estimate, and a
generalization claim would need to.

What the fit does deliver unambiguously is /interpretability/: two numbers
(the LD1/LD2 coordinates of a flower) reduce a four-measurement, three-class
problem to a picture a person can read directly, the discriminant
coefficients say in one table which measurements matter and by how much, and
every prediction comes with a calibrated-under-the-model posterior rather
than a bare label. That combination -- a closed-form fit, an exact geometric
picture, and legible per-prediction confidence -- is precisely why LDA
remains a standard first classifier to reach for eighty-nine years after
Fisher introduced it.

Finally, this entire report is reproducible end to end: running

* Conclusion

A linear discriminant analysis of Fisher's iris data, fit with
=datamunge='s C++ =stats::LDA=, separates the three species with 98.00%
training accuracy from four floral measurements, misclassifying only 3 of
150 flowers, all between the two most similar species. A single derived
axis, LD1, carries 99.12% of the model's discriminative power and is driven
overwhelmingly by the two petal measurements, matching what the raw
exploratory boxplots already suggested before any model was fit. Beyond the
numbers, this document is itself the artifact the introduction promised: a
