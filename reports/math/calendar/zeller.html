    Zeller's Congruence: Calendar Arithmetic Behind Monthly Data

    Abstract

    A weekday can be computed from a calendar date without counting through its preceding days. Zeller's congruence achieves this by moving January and February to the previous year and encoding month lengths modulo seven. We derive both the full-year and century forms, account for negative years with floor division, and prove the 400-year Gregorian repeat. The dataset application augments dm::datasets::air_passengers() with month lengths, weekdays and weekend counts, then distinguishes monthly passenger totals from daily averages. An exact cycle count also revisits the old report's Friday-the-thirteenth question without treating calendar dates as random.

    The data: monthly totals need a calendar denominator

    The built-in airline series contains 144 monthly totals from 1949 to 1960, in thousands of international passengers. Its columns are year, month and passengers. A monthly total combines demand with the amount of time in the month: February has fewer days than January even in a leap year. Calendar normalisation is therefore useful before comparing levels. It does not by itself remove growth or seasonal demand.

    The method: put the leap day at the end of the year

    Let the input be a valid Gregorian date \((y,m,q)\). If \(m<3\), replace \(m\) by \(m+12\) and set \(Y=y-1\); otherwise set \(Y=y\). The adjusted year runs from March through February. All variable leap-day behaviour is now at its end.

    Define the month term

    \[M(m)=\left\lfloor\frac{13(m+1)}5\right\rfloor,\qquad 3\le m\le14.\]

    For March through February its values are 10, 13, 15, 18, 20, 23, 26, 28, 31, 33, 36 and 39. Consecutive differences are 3 or 2, matching the lengths of the preceding 31- or 30-day months modulo seven. February's variable length appears only when the adjusted year advances.

    An ordinary year contributes \(365\equiv1\pmod7\). The Gregorian rule adds leap days in years divisible by four, omits century years and restores those divisible by 400. Including the month and day terms gives

    \[h=\left(q+M(m)+Y+\left\lfloor\frac Y4\right\rfloor -\left\lfloor\frac Y{100}\right\rfloor +\left\lfloor\frac Y{400}\right\rfloor\right)\bmod7.\]

    The convention is \(h=0\) Saturday, 1 Sunday, 2 Monday, through 6 Friday. The constant implicit in \(M\) agrees with the proleptic Gregorian anchor 1 January year 1, a Monday: the adjusted year is zero and the sum is \(1+36=37\equiv2\pmod7\). Month differences, year differences and this anchor establish the formula for every valid date under that calendar. The implementation validates dates instead of assigning weekdays to 31 April or 29 February 1900.

    Recovering the century form

    Write \(Y=100J+K\), where \(J=\lfloor Y/100\rfloor\) and \(0\le K<100\). Then the full-year part simplifies modulo seven:

    \[Y+\left\lfloor\frac Y4\right\rfloor -\left\lfloor\frac Y{100}\right\rfloor +\left\lfloor\frac Y{400}\right\rfloor =124J+K+\left\lfloor\frac K4\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac J4\right\rfloor \equiv5J+K+\left\lfloor\frac K4\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac J4\right\rfloor\pmod7.\]

    This is the original report's expression. Mathematical floor is essential for negative years: C++ integer division instead truncates towards zero. The library now uses explicit floor division and widens the year before subtracting one in January or February, so even the minimum int year is handled without overflow. Year zero denotes 1 BCE, using astronomical year numbering. The calculation is proleptic Gregorian: it does not apply a historical cutover to the Julian calendar.

    Applying it: month lengths and weekend counts

    gregorian_month_features reads the dataset's year and month columns and returns an owned augmented table. The original rows and measurements stay in order. For month length \(d=7a+r\), every full week contributes two weekend days; only the final \(r<7\) dates need their weekday checked. The helper therefore needs at most six remainder checks per record. A weekend here means Saturday or Sunday; this is not a holiday or airline working-calendar model.

    The table shows a reversal: January's mean monthly total exceeds February's, but February's mean per-day rate exceeds January's after allowing for its shorter months. The July and August peak remains large under either view. Month length is an exposure correction, not an explanation of that seasonality.

    Why the calendar repeats, and which weekday gets the thirteenth

    In 400 Gregorian years there are 100 multiples of four, four century exceptions and one restored leap year, hence 97 leap years. The total is

    \[400\cdot365+97=146097=7\cdot20871.\]

    The leap pattern repeats and the weekday shift is zero, so every date recurs with the same weekday after 400 years. Any consecutive 400-year block contains a complete set of year residues. Counts of a given month-day are therefore independent of the chosen cycle start.

    The calculation is an enumeration, not a statistical significance test. A uniformly selected month in a full cycle has a thirteenth on Friday in 688 of 4800 cases. The small imbalance follows from month lengths and leap years; no sampling argument or superstition is needed. The helper also supports days 29 to 31, skipping months where that day does not exist.

    Conclusion

    March-based years isolate the leap correction, and the floor terms account for Gregorian century exceptions. Date validation and wide signed arithmetic make the implementation match that mathematical contract. Applied to the airline series, the same calendar logic supplies real month lengths and weekday counts. Daily-average normalisation corrects exposure but leaves the demand trend and seasonal pattern. The complete cycle gives an exact Friday-the-thirteenth count and a strong repeatability check on the weekday calculation.
