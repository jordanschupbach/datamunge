    Berlekamp's Algorithm

    Abstract

    Berlekamp's algorithm factors a polynomial over a finite field with linear algebra. The polynomials \(h\) with \(h^p\equiv h \pmod f\) form a vector space whose dimension equals the number of distinct irreducible factors, and gcds with \(h-c\) split \(f\) apart. We derive the method, fix a bug in datamunge's implementation (it built the matrix untransposed and returned composite "irreducible" factors), and read the 1,797 handwritten digits of dm::datasets::digits() as polynomials over GF(17). After removing the power of \(x\) from the blank border, their factor statistics match random polynomials and the Gauss–Möbius counting formula: on average 4.6 distinct factors, 0.9 non-zero roots and a 1.6% chance of being irreducible. Timing up to degree 256 shows cost growing like \(n^{2.7}\), approaching the cubic Gaussian elimination.

    Factoring over a finite field

    Over the field \(\mathrm{GF}(p)\) of integers modulo a prime \(p\), every polynomial factors uniquely into a constant times monic irreducible polynomials. Unlike over the integers, there are only finitely many polynomials of each degree, so factoring is a finite problem. But trying every possible divisor takes time exponential in the degree. Berlekamp (1967) found a method that needs only linear algebra over \(\mathrm{GF}(p)\). It is used in coding theory, cryptography and computer algebra, where factoring over the integers starts by factoring modulo a prime.

    Removing repeated factors

    Berlekamp's method needs a square-free polynomial, one with no repeated factor. If \(f=g^2h\), then \(g\) divides both \(f\) and its derivative \(f'=2gg'h+g^2h'\), so \(\gcd(f,f')\) exposes the repeated part. Yun's algorithm repeats this to split \(f=\prod_m g_m^m\) into square-free, pairwise coprime \(g_m\). Characteristic \(p\) adds one twist: \(f'\) can vanish even when \(f\) is not constant, because \((x^p)'=px^{p-1}=0\). Then \(f(x)=h(x^p)=h(x)^p\), since \(a^p=a\) for every \(a\in\mathrm{GF}(p)\). The algorithm takes the \(p\)-th root \(h\) and multiplies its multiplicities by \(p\).

    The Berlekamp subalgebra

    Let \(f=f_1f_2\cdots f_k\) be monic and square-free of degree \(n\), with distinct irreducible \(f_i\) of degrees \(d_i\). By the Chinese remainder theorem,

    \[\mathrm{GF}(p)[x]/(f)\;\cong\;\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{GF}(p)[x]/(f_i)\;\cong\;\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{GF}(p^{d_i}).\]

    In a field \(\mathrm{GF}(p^d)\), the elements with \(a^p=a\) are exactly the \(p\) roots of \(x^p-x\), that is, \(\mathrm{GF}(p)\) itself. So the polynomials \(v\) of degree below \(n\) with

    \[v(x)^p\equiv v(x)\pmod f\]

    correspond to tuples \((s_1,\dots,s_k)\in\mathrm{GF}(p)^k\), with \(v\equiv s_i\pmod{f_i}\). They form a vector space of dimension exactly \(k\), the Berlekamp subalgebra. It is a linear condition: \(v^p=\sum_iv_ix^{ip}\) because the Frobenius map \(a\mapsto a^p\) is additive and fixes the coefficients. So with the Berlekamp matrix \(Q\), whose row \(i\) holds the coefficients of \(x^{ip}\bmod f\), the condition reads

    \[v^{\mathsf T}(Q-I)=0,\qquad k=n-\operatorname{rank}(Q-I).\]

    Counting the factors costs one Gaussian elimination: \(f\) is irreducible exactly when \(k=1\). (algebra::berlekamp_factor_count.)

    Splitting

    Take any \(v\) in the subalgebra that is not a constant. Over \(\mathrm{GF}(p)\), \(y^p-y=\prod_{s\in\mathrm{GF}(p)}(y-s)\), so

    \[f\;\big|\;v^p-v=\prod_{s\in\mathrm{GF}(p)}(v-s)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f=\prod_{s\in\mathrm{GF}(p)}\gcd(f,\,v-s).\]

    The factors \(v-s\) are pairwise coprime, so the gcds are too, and their product is \(f\). Each \(f_i\) goes into the gcd for its own \(s_i\). Since \(v\) is not a constant, the \(s_i\) are not all equal, and at least two gcds are non-trivial. Running through a basis of the subalgebra separates every pair of factors. algebra::factor_gf_p chains the square-free step, Berlekamp splitting and the multiplicities.

    While building this report, the existing Berlekamp code in the library turned out to row-reduce \(Q-I\) instead of its transpose. That finds a space of the right dimension, so the factor count was right, but the vectors were wrong. A random test of reconstruction and irreducibility over GF(2), GF(3), GF(5) and GF(17) failed 28 times. With the transpose it passes, and exhaustive enumeration of all monic polynomials over GF(3) up to degree 5 agrees with the counting formulas below.

    What a random polynomial looks like

    Gauss counted the monic irreducible polynomials of degree \(d\). Every element of \(\mathrm{GF}(p^d)\) is a root of \(x^{p^d}-x\), which is the product of all monic irreducibles whose degree divides \(d\). Comparing degrees gives \(p^d=\sum_{e\mid d}eI_e\), and Möbius inversion gives

    \[I_d=\frac1d\sum_{e\mid d}\mu\!\left(\frac de\right)p^{e}=\frac{p^d}{d}+O\!\left(\frac{p^{d/2}}{d}\right).\]

    So a random monic polynomial of degree \(n\) is irreducible with probability about \(1/n\). A fixed monic irreducible \(g\) of degree \(d\le n\) divides it with probability exactly \(p^{-d}\), because the quotient can be any monic polynomial of degree \(n-d\). By linearity of expectation, the expected number of distinct irreducible factors is

    \[\mathbb E[\#\text{factors}]=\sum_{d=1}^{n}I_d\,p^{-d}\;\approx\;\sum_{d=1}^n\frac1d=H_n\approx\ln n+0.577,\]

    the same as the expected number of cycles of a random permutation of \(n\) items. The linear factors are the roots: each \(c\in\mathrm{GF}(p)\) is a root with probability \(1/p\), so a random polynomial has exactly one distinct root on average.

    Handwritten digits as polynomials over GF(17)

    dm::datasets::digits() holds 1,797 handwritten digits as \(8\times8\) images with intensities 0 to 16, exactly the residues modulo 17. We read image \(j\) as the polynomial

    \[f_j(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{63}\text{pixel}_k\,x^k\in\mathrm{GF}(17)[x],\]

    with pixel 0 at the top left and pixel 63 at the bottom right, and factor all of them. Images have blank borders, so the low coefficients are zero, and \(x^m\) divides \(f_j\) where \(m\) is the number of leading blank pixels. The top coefficients can also be zero, which lowers the degree. The interesting part is \(g_j=f_j/x^m\). To see whether it behaves like a random polynomial, each image is paired with a random polynomial of the same degree and the same \(m\), whose other coefficients are uniform on \(\mathrm{GF}(17)\) (the constant term and the leading coefficient non-zero).

The digits behave like random polynomials. The mean number of distinct factors of \(g\) is 4.615 for images, 4.584 for random polynomials of the same shape and 4.572 from \(\sum_d I_d p^{-d}\) averaged over the same degrees. The difference of 0.03 is within the sampling error of about 0.04 for 1,797 draws. The share of non-zero roots, 0.922 against a theoretical \((p-1)/p=0.941\), and the share of irreducible \(g\), 0.016 against 0.018, also agree within sampling error (the latter's standard error is 0.003). The two histograms in the figure overlap bar for bar.

    The per-digit means range from 4.42 for nines to 4.88 for zeros. With about 180 images per digit that spread is a few standard errors, so there may be faint structure: zeros, with a hole in the middle, have a different coefficient pattern. Factor counts are a poor feature for recognising digits, though, which is the point. Reducing pixel intensities modulo 17 destroys the geometry, and arithmetic over GF(17) sees the images as essentially random.

    The cost of Berlekamp's algorithm

    Building \(Q\) takes \(n\) rows, each obtained from the previous by \(p\) multiplications by \(x\) modulo \(f\). That is \(O(pn^2)\) here, or \(O(n^2\log p)\) with repeated squaring. The Gaussian elimination is \(O(n^3)\), and splitting needs up to \(p\) gcds per basis vector. For small \(p\) the elimination dominates as \(n\) grows.     On this machine a degree-16 polynomial factors in 0.04 ms and a degree-256 one in 23 ms. The log–log slope rises from about 1.8 at small degrees to 2.68 over the last two doublings: the quadratic construction of \(Q\) dominates at first, and the cubic elimination takes over as \(n\) grows. The mean factor counts follow \(H_n\) as the permutation analogy predicts. The match is only rough, because each degree uses few samples (8 at degree 256) and the count has a variance close to \(\ln n\).

    Summary

    • Berlekamp's algorithm turns factoring into linear algebra. The kernel of \(Q^\top-I\) has dimension equal to the number of distinct irreducible factors, and gcds with \(h-c\) for kernel vectors \(h\) split them apart. After a square-free step it factors any polynomial.
    • The library built \(Q-I\) instead of its transpose and returned composite factors. That is fixed, and new tests check that every factor is irreducible and that the product rebuilds the input. The new API is algebra::factor_gf_p, berlekamp_factor_count, count_monic_irreducibles and expected_distinct_irreducible_factors.
    • Digit images read as polynomials over GF(17) are statistically indistinguishable from random polynomials once the power of \(x\) is removed. Their factor counts, roots and irreducibility rate all match the Gauss–Möbius predictions.
    • Cost grows like \(n^{2.7}\) between degrees 64 and 256, heading to the \(O(n^3)\) of Gaussian elimination. Cantor–Zassenhaus or Kaltofen–Shoup is preferable for large degrees.
