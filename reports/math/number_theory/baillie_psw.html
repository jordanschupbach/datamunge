    Baillie–PSW: Two Primality Tests With No Common Liar

    Abstract

    A probable-prime test checks an identity that every prime satisfies. Composites that satisfy it anyway are its pseudoprimes, or liars. The Baillie–PSW test combines a strong Fermat test to base 2, which checks \(2^{n-1}\equiv1\) through its chain of square roots, and a strong Lucas test, which checks the analogous identity \(U_{n+1}\equiv0\) for a Lucas sequence chosen by Selfridge's rule. We derive both identities: the first from the fact that \(\pm1\) are the only square roots of 1 modulo a prime, the second from the Frobenius map in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\). A census of all odd composites below \(10^7\) finds 162 strong pseudoprimes to base 2 and 178 strong Lucas pseudoprimes, and no number in both lists. The two kinds of liar have different shapes: a base-2 liar needs \(n-1\) to be divisible by the order of 2 modulo each prime factor \(p\), a Lucas liar needs \(n+1\) to be divisible by the matching rank in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\), and the factors of the two kinds of liar share visibly different amounts of \(p-1\) with \(n-1\). No composite below \(2^{64}\) passes both, so Baillie–PSW is a deterministic test for 64-bit integers; on 64-bit primes it costs about 3.8 times a single strong Fermat test, a third of a deterministic Miller–Rabin test, and on random odd numbers less than either.

    Two identities that every prime satisfies

    The strong Fermat test

    Fermat's little theorem says \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\) for a prime \(p\nmid a\). Write \(n-1=d\cdot2^s\) with \(d\) odd. If \(n\) is prime, the sequence

    \[a^{d},\ a^{2d},\ a^{4d},\ \dots,\ a^{2^sd}=a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n\]

    ends at 1, and each term is the square of the one before. Modulo a prime the only square roots of 1 are \(\pm1\), because \(x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)\equiv0\) forces one factor to vanish in a field. So the first 1 in the sequence is either the very first term or is preceded by \(-1\):

    \[a^{d}\equiv1\quad\text{or}\quad a^{2^rd}\equiv-1\ \text{for some }0\le r<s.\]

    An odd composite \(n\) that satisfies this for \(a=2\) is a strong pseudoprime to base 2. For a composite modulus 1 has other square roots (by the Chinese remainder theorem), which is why the strong test catches many composites that pass \(a^{n-1}\equiv1\) alone.

    The strong Lucas test

    Let \(P,Q\) be integers with discriminant \(D=P^2-4Q\), and let \(\alpha,\beta\) be the roots of \(x^2-Px+Q\). The Lucas sequences are

    \[U_k=\frac{\alpha^k-\beta^k}{\alpha-\beta},\qquad V_k=\alpha^k+\beta^k,\]

    integers satisfying \(X_{k+1}=PX_k-QX_{k-1}\) with \(U_0=0,U_1=1,V_0=2,V_1=P\). Now take a prime \(p\nmid 2QD\) with Jacobi symbol \((D/p)=-1\). Then \(D\) is not a square mod \(p\), the polynomial is irreducible, and \(\alpha,\beta\) live in the field \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\) as conjugates. The Frobenius map \(x\mapsto x^p\) is the field's only non-trivial automorphism, so it swaps the roots: \(\alpha^p=\beta\). Hence \(\alpha^{p+1}=\alpha\beta=Q=\beta^{p+1}\), and

    \[U_{p+1}=\frac{\alpha^{p+1}-\beta^{p+1}}{\alpha-\beta}\equiv0\pmod p .\]

    This is the Lucas analogue of Fermat's theorem, with \(p+1\) in place of \(p-1\). The strong form follows the same square-root argument in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\): writing \(n+1=d\cdot2^s\) with \(d\) odd, a prime satisfies \(U_d\equiv0\) or \(V_{d2^r}\equiv0\) for some \(0\le r<s\), using \(U_{2k}=U_kV_k\) and \(V_{2k}=V_k^2-2Q^k\). Selfridge's rule fixes the parameters: \(D\) is the first of \(5,-7,9,-11,\dots\) with \((D/n)=-1\), \(P=1\), \(Q=(1-D)/4\). (For a perfect square no such \(D\) exists, so squares are rejected first.) lucas_sequence_mod() computes \(U_k\) and \(V_k\) modulo \(n\) in \(O(\log k)\) doubling and increment steps.

    Strengthening each test removes about four fifths of its liars: below \(10^7\) there are 750 Fermat pseudoprimes to base 2 but 162 strong ones, and 757 Lucas pseudoprimes but 178 strong ones. The first lists agree with the published sequences (OEIS A001262 and A217255). The two strong families are about equally common and grow at about the same rate, yet not one number below \(10^7\) is in both. Exhaustive searches have extended this to every \(n<2^{64}\), so for 64-bit integers Baillie–PSW makes no mistakes. Pomerance gave a heuristic argument that counterexamples exist in infinite number, but none has ever been found.

    Why the liars differ

    Let \(n=\prod p_i\) be squarefree. Fermat's congruence \(2^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n\) holds exactly when, for each \(p_i\), the multiplicative order of 2 modulo \(p_i\) divides \(n-1\). The Lucas congruence \(U_{n+1}\equiv0\) holds when, for each \(p_i\), the rank of apparition (the least \(k\) with \(p_i\mid U_k\)) divides \(n+1\). For a prime with \((D/p)=-1\) that rank divides \(p+1\). So a base-2 liar needs its prime factors' \(p-1\) to share structure with \(n-1\), and a Lucas liar needs their \(p+1\) to share structure with \(n+1\). A number fooling both would need both at once, and since \(n-1\) and \(n+1\) share only the factor 2, this is rare enough that no example is known. The block checks this for the liars found above, measuring each liar's factors \(p\) by \(\gcd(p-1,n-1)/(p-1)\) and \(\gcd(p+1,n+1)/(p+1)\), the share of \(p\mp1\) that divides \(n\mp1\).

    The horizontal axis separates the two families almost completely. For strong base-2 liars the median share is \(2^{-2.8}\): each prime factor's \(p-1\) mostly divides \(n-1\), and for many liars all of it does. For strong Lucas liars the median is \(2^{-9.8}\), essentially only the common factor 2. The vertical axis separates them less cleanly (medians \(2^{-10.4}\) and \(2^{-8.4}\)). The Lucas condition needs only the rank of apparition, which can be a small divisor of \(p+1\), to divide \(n+1\), just as the base-2 condition needs only the order of 2 to divide \(n-1\). Still, the picture shows why a common liar is hard to build: its factors would have to sit at the right of the red cloud and the top of the blue one at the same time.

    Cost

    Each Fermat test takes about \(\log_2 n\) modular squarings. The Lucas test's doubling step updates \(U\), \(V\) and \(Q^k\), and an increment step adds more multiplications, so it costs a small multiple of one Fermat test. The block times both, Baillie–PSW, and is_probable_prime() (deterministic Miller–Rabin with a fixed set of bases for 64-bit numbers) on a million random odd 64-bit numbers and on 100,000 64-bit primes.

    About 4.5% of random odd 64-bit numbers are prime (the prime number theorem predicts \(2/\ln2^{63.5}\approx4.5\%\)), and all four tests agreed on every one of the million numbers. The strong Lucas test costs about 2.7 times a strong Fermat test, so on a prime Baillie–PSW costs both, about 1.2 µs on this machine, against 3.8 µs for the deterministic Miller–Rabin test, which runs several bases. On random odd numbers Baillie–PSW is the cheapest test of all, at about 150 ns: trial division by the primes up to 37 removes about 70% of odd numbers at once, and almost every remaining composite fails the base-2 test, so the Lucas test runs only on numbers that are very likely prime.

    Summary

    • Primes satisfy the strong Fermat condition because \(\pm1\) are the only square roots of 1 in a field, and the strong Lucas condition because Frobenius swaps the roots in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\), giving \(U_{p+1}\equiv0\) when \((D/p)=-1\).
    • Below \(10^7\) the strong base-2 test has 162 liars and the strong Lucas test 178; none is common to both. No common liar exists below \(2^{64}\), so Baillie–PSW is deterministic there.
    • The liars' prime factors share most of \(p-1\) with \(n-1\) for the base-2 test and almost none for the Lucas test, a structural reason the families do not meet.
    • On primes the test costs about 3.8 strong Fermat tests, a third of deterministic Miller–Rabin; on random inputs, with trial division first, it is cheaper still.
