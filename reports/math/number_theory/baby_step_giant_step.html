    Baby-Step Giant-Step: Discrete Logarithms by Meeting in the Middle

    Abstract

    Given a prime \(p\), a base \(g\) and a target \(h\), the discrete logarithm problem asks for \(x\) with \(g^x\equiv h\pmod p\). Trying every exponent takes up to \(p-1\) multiplications. Shanks's baby-step giant-step algorithm splits \(x=im+j\), tabulates the \(m\) "baby steps" \(g^j\), and walks "giant steps" \(h\,g^{-mi}\) until one lands in the table. We derive its cost, \(m+\lceil n/m\rceil\) in the worst case and \(m+n/(2m)\) on average for a group of order \(n\), so the best table holds \(\sqrt n\) entries for the worst case but only \(\sqrt{n/2}\) for the average. A sweep over table sizes at \(p=1{,}000{,}003\) matches both formulas. Across primes from \(10^3\) to \(10^{12}\) the measured work grows as \(p^{0.500}\), and a 40-bit Diffie–Hellman secret falls in about a tenth of a second on this machine. Finally, when \(g\) generates only a subgroup of prime order \(q\), searching that subgroup costs \(\sqrt q\) instead of \(\sqrt p\), which is the idea behind the Pohlig–Hellman algorithm.

    Meeting in the middle

    Let \(g\) have order \(n\) modulo \(p\), so \(g^n\equiv1\) and the powers \(g^0,\dots,g^{n-1}\) are distinct. When \(g\) is a primitive root, \(n=p-1\). Every \(x\in[0,n)\) can be written by the division algorithm as

    \[x=i\,m+j,\qquad 0\le j<m,\qquad 0\le i<\lceil n/m\rceil .\]

    Multiplying \(g^{im+j}\equiv h\) by \(g^{-im}\) separates the two unknowns:

    \[g^{\,j}\;\equiv\;h\cdot\bigl(g^{-m}\bigr)^{i}\pmod p .\]

    The left side depends only on \(j\), the right only on \(i\). The algorithm stores the \(m\) baby steps \(g^j\) in a hash table, then computes the giant steps \(h,\ hg^{-m},\ hg^{-2m},\dots\), one multiplication each, and looks each up. A hit at giant step \(i\) and table entry \(j\) gives \(x=im+j\). Because the table keeps the smallest \(j\) for each value and \(i\) increases, the first hit is the smallest solution. If \(h\) is not a power of \(g\), no giant step can hit, and after \(\lceil n/m\rceil\) steps the algorithm reports that no logarithm exists.

    Since \(2\) has order 100 modulo 101, it is a primitive root and \(n=p-1=100\), so \(m=10\). The fourth giant step, \(17\cdot2^{-30}\equiv1\), matches the baby step \(2^0=1\), giving \(x=3\cdot10+0=30\), and indeed \(2^{30}\equiv17\pmod{101}\). The search used 10 baby steps and 4 giant steps, 14 multiplications against the 31 needed to try \(2^0,2^1,\dots,2^{30}\) in order. The baby steps scatter across the residues with no visible order. That is the point: nothing about \(g^j\bmod p\) reveals \(j\) except the table, which is why the logarithm is hard.

    Choosing the table size

    Worst case and average case

    With \(m\) baby steps, the table costs \(m\) multiplications and \(m\) entries of memory. The solution \(x\) is found at giant step \(i=\lfloor x/m\rfloor\), after \(\lfloor x/m\rfloor+1\) lookups, and no more than \(\lceil n/m\rceil\) are ever needed. The worst-case work is therefore

    \[W(m)=m+\Bigl\lceil\frac nm\Bigr\rceil\approx m+\frac nm,\qquad W'(m)=1-\frac n{m^2}=0\ \Rightarrow\ m^*=\sqrt n,\quad W(m^*)=2\sqrt n .\]

    If the secret \(x\) is uniform on \([0,n)\), the expected number of giant steps is \(\mathbb E\lfloor x/m\rfloor+1\approx n/(2m)+\tfrac12\). The expected work is

    \[A(m)\approx m+\frac n{2m}+\frac12,\qquad m^\dagger=\sqrt{n/2},\qquad A(m^\dagger)\approx\sqrt{2n}\approx1.41\sqrt n .\]

    At \(m=\sqrt n\) the average is \(1.5\sqrt n\), only 6% above the optimum, so the textbook choice protects the worst case at little cost to the average. The table is the expensive part in practice: memory, not time, limits baby-step giant-step, and that is what Pollard's rho and kangaroo methods remove.

To the right of the minimum the table dominates and both curves approach \(W=m\); to the left the giant steps dominate, and the average is half the worst case because the secret is, on average, halfway through the range.

    Scaling and Diffie–Hellman

    Diffie–Hellman key exchange publishes \(g\) and \(A=g^a\bmod p\) and keeps \(a\) secret. Anyone who can solve \(g^x\equiv A\) recovers \(a\), so baby-step giant-step sets a floor on the size of \(p\): its \(O(\sqrt p)\) work must be out of reach. The block takes the first prime above \(10^k\) for \(k=3,\dots,12\), uses its smallest primitive root, and recovers 20 random secrets per prime, timing each.     The number of steps is \(1.43\sqrt p\) to \(1.57\sqrt p\) at every size, close to the \(1.5\sqrt p\) expected at \(m=\sqrt n\), and its log-log slope is 0.500. Time grows a little faster (slope 0.56 from \(10^6\)) because the hash table outgrows the processor caches: at \(10^{12}\) it holds a million entries, and the time per step rises from about 20 ns to 65 ns. Trying exponents in order has slope 0.99, as expected for \(O(p)\), and is already 600 times slower at \(10^8\).

    A 40-bit prime (about \(10^{12}\)) gives no security: its secret falls in about 0.1 s. Each extra factor of 100 in \(p\) costs 10 times more time and memory, so a 2048-bit prime would need about \(2^{1024}\) steps. Real attacks on such primes use the index calculus instead, which is why recommended Diffie–Hellman primes are so much larger than \(\sqrt p\) alone would suggest.

    Searching a subgroup

    The order of any \(g\) divides \(p-1\) (Lagrange's theorem). If \(g\) has prime order \(q\), then \(x\) only matters modulo \(q\), and the search can use \(n=q\): \(\sqrt q\) baby steps and at most \(\sqrt q\) giant steps, however large \(p\) is. Pohlig and Hellman turned this into an attack on any \(p\) for which \(p-1\) has only small prime factors: solve the logarithm in each prime-order subgroup and combine the answers with the Chinese remainder theorem. That is why Diffie–Hellman uses a safe prime \(p=2q+1\) with \(q\) prime, or a large prime-order subgroup.

Searching as if \(g_q\) generated the whole group took about a million: the table of \(\sqrt{p-1}\approx10^6\) entries is built in full even though \(g_q\) has only \(q\) distinct powers, so the work is the table, and one giant step then always hits. Knowing the order of \(g\) saves a factor of 670 here. The prime \(p=1{,}000{,}047{,}000{,}133\) is weak in exactly this way: \(p-1=1{,}000{,}044\cdot q\), and Pohlig–Hellman would solve logarithms to the base \(g\) one subgroup at a time.

    Summary

    • Writing \(x=im+j\) turns \(g^x\equiv h\) into the collision \(g^j\equiv h\,g^{-mi}\) between \(m\) stored baby steps and at most \(\lceil n/m\rceil\) giant steps.
    • The worst-case work \(m+n/m\) is minimised at \(m=\sqrt n\) (\(2\sqrt n\)), the mean \(m+n/(2m)\) at \(m=\sqrt{n/2}\) (\(1.41\sqrt n\)); a sweep at \(p=1{,}000{,}003\) matches both.
    • From \(10^3\) to \(10^{12}\) the work grows exactly as \(\sqrt p\); a 40-bit Diffie–Hellman secret falls in about 0.1 s, against about half an hour for trying exponents in order.
    • Memory, \(\sqrt n\) table entries, is the real limit, and searching a prime-order subgroup costs \(\sqrt q\) rather than \(\sqrt p\), the basis of Pohlig–Hellman.
