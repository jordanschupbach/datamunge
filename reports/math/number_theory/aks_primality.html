    The AKS Primality Test: Deterministic, Polynomial and Slow

    Abstract

    In 2002 Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena showed that primality can be decided deterministically in time polynomial in \(\log n\). Their test starts from a polynomial form of Fermat's little theorem, \((x+a)^n\equiv x^n+a \pmod n\), which holds exactly when \(n\) is prime. To keep the degree small, the identity is checked modulo \(x^r-1\) for a small \(r\) with \(\operatorname{ord}_r(n)>\log_2^2 n\). We prove the identity in both directions, sketch why the reduced checks still prove primality, and bound \(r\) between \(\log_2^2 n\) and \(\log_2^5 n\). A step-by-step trace, algorithms::aks_trace, shows which step decides each number. Up to 1000, every composite is caught by a perfect-power or small-factor check, and every prime from 37 on needs the full set of congruences. The congruences reject a composite only when all its prime factors exceed \(r\), and then at once. The chosen \(r\) is within a few per cent of \(\log_2^2 n\) up to 63 bits, far below the proven \(\log_2^5 n\). The cost grows as about \(\log^{7} n\): a 20-bit prime takes about a second, where Miller-Rabin takes under a microsecond.

    A polynomial Fermat test

    *Lemma.* Let \(n\ge2\) and \(\gcd(a,n)=1\). Then \(n\) is prime if and only if, in \((\mathbb Z/n)[x]\),

    \[(x+a)^n \equiv x^n + a \pmod n .\]

    Proof. The coefficient of \(x^k\) in \((x+a)^n\) is \(\binom nk a^{n-k}\).

    If \(n=p\) is prime, then \(p\mid\binom pk\) for \(0<k<p\), since \(p\) divides \(p!\) but not \(k!\,(p-k)!\). By Fermat, \(a^p\equiv a\). So \((x+a)^p\equiv x^p+a\).

    If \(n\) is composite, let \(q\) be a prime factor with \(q^s\,\|\,n\). Among the \(q\) consecutive numbers \(n, n-1, \dots, n-q+1\), only \(n\) is divisible by \(q\). So \(\binom nq=n(n-1)\cdots(n-q+1)/q!\) is divisible by exactly \(q^{s-1}\), and therefore not by \(q^s\). Since \(q\nmid a\), the coefficient \(\binom nq a^{n-q}\) is non-zero modulo \(q^s\), and hence modulo \(n\). \(\square\)

    The test is exact but useless as it stands: \((x+a)^n\) has \(n+1\) coefficients. AKS reduces modulo \(x^r-1\) as well, which folds exponents modulo \(r\) and leaves \(r\) coefficients:

    \[(x+a)^n \equiv x^{n \bmod r} + a \pmod{x^r-1,\; n},\qquad a = 1,\dots,\ell,\quad \ell=\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{\varphi(r)}\,\log_2 n\bigr\rfloor .\]

    A composite can satisfy one reduced congruence, so a single \(a\) is no longer enough. The theorem is that \(\ell\) values are enough.

    Why the reduced checks suffice

    *Theorem (AKS).* Suppose that:

    • \(n\) is not a perfect power;
    • \(n\) has no prime factor \(\le r\);
    • \(\operatorname{ord}_r(n)>\log_2^2 n\);
    • the reduced congruence holds for \(a=1,\dots,\ell\).

    Then \(n\) is prime.

    Sketch. Let \(p\) be a prime factor of \(n\). Call \(m\) introspective for a polynomial \(f\) if \(f(x)^m\equiv f(x^m)\pmod{x^r-1,\,p}\).

    • Both \(p\) (by Frobenius) and \(n\) (by hypothesis) are introspective for every \(f=\prod_a (x+a)^{e_a}\).
    • Introspective numbers are closed under multiplication, so every \(n^ip^j\) is introspective.

    Two groups give a contradiction:

    • *The exponent group.* Let \(G\) be the subgroup of \((\mathbb Z/r)^\times\) generated by \(n\) and \(p\). Its size satisfies \(t=|G|\ge\operatorname{ord}_r(n)>\log_2^2 n\).
    • *The polynomial group.* Let \(\mathcal G\) be the group generated by the \(x+a\) in the field \(\mathbb F_p[x]/(h)\), where \(h\) is an irreducible factor of the \(r\)-th cyclotomic polynomial.
    • *Lower bound.* Distinct products of the \(x+a\) of degree below \(t\) stay distinct in \(\mathcal G\). So \(|\mathcal G|\ge\binom{t+\ell}{t-1}>n^{\sqrt t}\), using \(t>\log_2^2 n\).
    • *Upper bound.* If \(n\) is not a power of \(p\), the numbers \(n^ip^j\) with \(0\le i,j\le\lfloor\sqrt t\rfloor\) give two exponents \(m\ne m'\) that are congruent modulo \(r\). Every element of \(\mathcal G\) is then a root of \(y^m-y^{m'}\). This forces \(|\mathcal G|\le n^{\sqrt t}\).

    The bounds contradict each other, so \(n=p^k\). Since \(n\) is not a perfect power, \(n=p\). \(\square\)

    *How large is \(r\)?*

    • Below: \(\operatorname{ord}_r(n)\le\varphi(r)<r\), so \(r>\log_2^2 n\).
    • Above: every \(r\) whose order is at most \(B=\log_2^2 n\) divides \(\prod_{i\le B}(n^i-1)<2^{\log_2^5 n}\). The lcm of the first \(m\) integers is at least \(2^m\), so some \(r\le\lceil\log_2^5 n\rceil\) escapes.

    Both \(r\) and \(\ell\le\sqrt r\log_2 n\) are therefore polynomial in \(\log n\), and so is the test. algorithms::aks_trace runs it in five steps:

    1. reject perfect powers;
    2. find \(r\) (aks_r);
    3. reject any \(n\) with \(1<\gcd(a,n)<n\) for some \(a\le r\);
    4. accept if \(n\le r\);
    5. check the \(\ell\) congruences (aks_congruence_holds).

    The traces show each step in action:

    • *Small primes.* 7 is settled at step 4, because \(r=n\).
    • *Perfect powers and small factors.* \(3^{13}=1\,594\,323\) is rejected at step 1. \(1\,000\,001=101\cdot9901\) and the Carmichael number \(561=3\cdot11\cdot17\) are rejected at step 3, by a gcd.
    • *Large prime factors.* The only composite in the table to reach the polynomial step is \(1009\cdot1013\). Both its factors exceed \(r=409\). It fails at once, at \(a=1\): in the reduced ring all 409 coefficients of \((x+1)^n\) are nonzero.
    • *Primes.* A prime pays for all \(\ell\) congruences: 398 for the 20-bit prime \(1\,000\,003\).

    Up to 1000, the tally puts:

    • all 40 perfect powers at step 1;
    • the other 791 composites at step 3;
    • the 11 primes up to 31 at step 4, since \(n\le r\);
    • the remaining 157 primes at step 5.

    No composite reaches the polynomial step until its smallest prime factor exceeds \(r\approx\log_2^2n\). For a product of two primes that means \(n>r^2\).

    In \(\operatorname{ord}_r(n)\) on the first row, 0 means \(r\) and \(n\) share a factor, so the order is undefined.

    Cost

    One multiplication in \((\mathbb Z/n)[x]/(x^r-1)\) costs \(r^2\) coefficient products with schoolbook arithmetic. Raising \(x+a\) to the \(n\)-th power takes \(\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor\) squarings and \(\nu(n)-1\) other multiplications, where \(\nu(n)\) is the number of 1 bits. A prime needs all \(\ell\) congruences, so the coefficient products number about

    \[W(n) \approx \ell\,\bigl(\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor+\nu(n)\bigr)\,r^2 \;\approx\; \sqrt r\,\log_2 n\cdot 2\log_2 n\cdot r^2 \;=\; 2\,r^{5/2}\log_2^2 n .\]

    With \(r\approx\log_2^2 n\), as observed, \(W\) grows as \(\log^{7} n\). The worst-case analysis with \(r=O(\log^5 n)\) and fast polynomial arithmetic gives the published \(\tilde O(\log^{21/2} n)\). Lenstra and Pomerance later reduced this to \(\tilde O(\log^6 n)\).

    Over 8 to 20 bits, log time rises against log bits with slope 7.3, close to the 6.7 of the work model \(W\) and to the predicted 7. The time per coefficient product drifts only from 0.22 to 0.40 ns, as the polynomials outgrow the cache. The model even predicts the bump at 9 bits, where the large \(r\) makes 509 slower to prove than 1021.

    At 20 bits AKS takes about a second, against 0.8 microseconds for Miller-Rabin, a factor of over a million. Extrapolating \(\log^7 n\), a 64-bit prime would take about an hour.

    Summary

    • \((x+a)^n\equiv x^n+a\pmod n\) characterises primes exactly: the middle binomial coefficients vanish for primes, and for composites the coefficient of \(x^q\) survives, where \(q\) is a prime factor of \(n\). Reducing modulo \(x^r-1\) keeps the degree below \(r\). The AKS theorem shows that \(\lfloor\sqrt{\varphi(r)}\log_2 n\rfloor\) reduced checks then decide primality, provided \(\operatorname{ord}_r(n)>\log_2^2n\).
    • Most composites never reach the polynomial step. Up to 1000, perfect powers and small factors catch every composite. A composite reaches the congruences only when all its prime factors exceed \(r\), and then fails at \(a=1\).
    • The chosen \(r\) stays within 5% of \(\log_2^2 n\) for every bit length from 4 to 63 except one, far below the \(\log_2^5 n\) of the worst-case bound.
    • The cost grows as about \(\log^7 n\), as the work model predicts: about a second at 20 bits, against under a microsecond for Miller-Rabin. AKS matters because it is unconditional and polynomial, not because it is practical.
