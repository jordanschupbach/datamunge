    The Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe Formula

    Abstract

    The Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe series \(\pi=\sum_k16^{-k}\bigl(\frac4{8k+1}-\frac2{8k+4}-\frac1{8k+5}-\frac1{8k+6}\bigr)\) lets one compute hexadecimal digits of \(\pi\) at position \(n\) without any of the earlier digits, using modular exponentiation and constant memory. We prove the series from an integral of a rational function and derive the digit-extraction scheme. We then check it against the exact digits, which datamunge now computes from Machin's formula in fixed-point arithmetic and which agree with GNU MPFR for the first 100,000 digits. In double precision BBP was right at all 20,000 positions tested, both one digit and eight digits at a time. Reading ten digits per evaluation failed in 55 of 2,000 evaluations. The error of \(\{16^n\pi\}\) grows roughly like \(\sqrt n\,2^{-53}\), as for a random walk of rounding errors. At positions \(10^6\) and \(10^7\) one evaluation still gives 10 and 8 correct digits of the published values. The cost grows linearly in \(n\) (slope 1.06 on a log–log plot), reaching about 2 seconds at \(n=10^7\) on this machine. The first 50,000 hex digits pass \(\chi^2\) tests of uniform single digits (\(p=0.86\)) and digit pairs (\(p=0.66\)). This agrees with, but cannot prove, the conjecture that \(\pi\) is normal in base 16.

    A series for π in base 16

    In 1995 Bailey, Borwein and Plouffe found, by computer search, the series

    \[\pi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac1{16^k}\Bigl(\frac4{8k+1}-\frac2{8k+4}-\frac1{8k+5}-\frac1{8k+6}\Bigr) =\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{16^k}\,\frac{120k^2+151k+47}{512k^4+1024k^3+712k^2+194k+15}.\]

    Each term adds a little more than one hexadecimal digit, which is slow by modern standards. What makes the formula remarkable is that the powers of 16 let a single hexadecimal digit far out in the expansion be computed without computing any digit before it.

    Proof

    For \(1\le j\le8\), expand \(1/(1-x^8)\) as a geometric series and integrate term by term:

    \[\int_0^{1/\sqrt2}\frac{x^{j-1}}{1-x^8}\,dx=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\int_0^{1/\sqrt2}x^{j-1+8k}\,dx =\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(1/\sqrt2)^{8k+j}}{8k+j}=\frac1{\sqrt2^{\,j}}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{16^k(8k+j)}.\]

    So each of the four sums in the BBP series is an integral. Multiplying by \(\sqrt2^{\,j}\) and combining with the weights \(4,-2,-1,-1\) for \(j=1,4,5,6\) gives

    \[\sum_k\frac1{16^k}\Bigl(\frac4{8k+1}-\frac2{8k+4}-\frac1{8k+5}-\frac1{8k+6}\Bigr) =\int_0^{1/\sqrt2}\frac{4\sqrt2-8x^3-4\sqrt2\,x^4-8x^5}{1-x^8}\,dx =\int_0^1\frac{16y-16}{y^4-2y^3+4y-4}\,dy,\]

    after the substitution \(y=\sqrt2\,x\). The denominator factors as \((y^2-2)(y^2-2y+2)\), and partial fractions give

    \[\frac{16y-16}{(y^2-2)(y^2-2y+2)}=\frac{4y}{y^2-2}-\frac{4y-8}{y^2-2y+2},\]

    whose antiderivative is \(2\log|y^2-2|-2\log(y^2-2y+2)+4\arctan(y-1)\). Between 0 and 1 this is \((0-0+0)-(2\log2-2\log2-\pi)=\pi\). \(\blacksquare\)

    Extracting one digit

    The hexadecimal digits of \(\pi\) from position \(n\) on are the expansion of the fractional part \(\{16^n\pi\}\), where position 0 is the first digit after the point. Write \(S_j=\sum_k16^{-k}/(8k+j)\), so \(\{16^n\pi\}=\{4\{16^nS_1\}-2\{16^nS_4\}-\{16^nS_5\}-\{16^nS_6\}\}\). Split each sum at \(k=n\):

    \[\{16^nS_j\}=\Bigl\{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{16^{n-k}\bmod(8k+j)}{8k+j}+\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty\frac{16^{n-k}}{8k+j}\Bigr\}.\]

    For \(k\le n\) the term \(16^{n-k}/(8k+j)\) is an integer plus \((16^{n-k}\bmod(8k+j))/(8k+j)\), and the integer cannot affect a fractional part, so it is dropped. The remainder comes from binary modular exponentiation in \(O(\log n)\) multiplications of numbers below \(8n+j\). The tail with \(k>n\) shrinks by a factor of 16 per term. So one digit costs \(O(n\log n)\) word operations and constant memory, with no arithmetic on numbers longer than a machine word. Every intermediate sum is reduced modulo 1, so it stays in \([0,1)\).

    The catch is rounding. Each of the roughly \(4n\) additions rounds to the 53-bit double format, with an error of up to \(2^{-53}\). If these errors behave like independent random signs, the accumulated error grows like \(\sqrt n\,2^{-53}\) instead of \(n\,2^{-53}\). A hex digit at offset \(i\) is right when the error is smaller than the distance from \(16^i\{16^n\pi\}\) to the nearest integer. About \(\log_{16}(1/\text{error})\) leading digits of each evaluation can be trusted.

    algorithms::bbp_pi_fraction(n) returns \(\{16^n\pi\}\), and algorithms::bbp_pi_hex_digits(n, c) reads \(c\le12\) digits from it. To check them we need the true digits. algorithms::pi_hex_digits(N) computes the first \(N\) exactly from Machin's formula \(\pi=16\arctan\tfrac15-4\arctan\tfrac1{239}\), in fixed-point arithmetic on 32-bit limbs. Its first 100,000 digits agree exactly with the GNU MPFR library's value of \(\pi\).

They therefore read only the first few digits of each evaluation, or use higher precision.

    The time grows almost exactly in proportion to \(n\). The slope over the last two decades is 1.06, consistent with \(O(n\log n)\): the \(\log n\) factor, the number of squarings per modular exponentiation, grows only from 20 to 26 over this range. On this machine the digits at position \(10^7\) took about 2 seconds, and the memory used is a handful of numbers whatever \(n\) is. The "digits that agree" column is a check that needs no reference: evaluations at \(n\) and \(n+1\) are computed independently but must give overlapping strings. All eight agree at every position in the table, consistent with the eight-digit reliability seen above.

    Are the digits random?

    A number is normal in base 16 if every block of \(m\) hex digits occurs with limiting frequency \(16^{-m}\). Almost every real number is normal, but no one has proved that \(\pi\) is. Bailey and Crandall (2001) used the BBP formula to reduce base-16 normality of \(\pi\) to an unproved hypothesis about a simple chaotic sequence. Statistics cannot prove normality, but they can look for evidence against it. We test the first 50,000 exact digits for uniform single-digit frequencies (\(\chi^2\) with 15 degrees of freedom) and for uniform frequencies of non-overlapping digit pairs (255 degrees of freedom). We also follow the single-digit statistic as the sample grows.

    No departure from uniformity appears. Every digit count lies within two binomial standard deviations of 3,125: the extremes are 3,038 for D and 3,189 for 1. The single-digit statistic is 9.33 on 15 degrees of freedom (\(p=0.86\)), and the pair statistic is 245.2 on 255 (\(p=0.66\)). The running statistic never reaches the 5% critical value at any of the 100 checkpoints. It does stay below its expectation of 15 throughout. That is unremarkable, because successive values share most of their digits and are strongly correlated, so a single run tends to stay on one side of its mean. These tests can only fail to find structure: the digits of \(\pi\) behave like random digits at this scale, as far larger studies have also found.

    Summary

    • The BBP series follows from \(\int_0^1\frac{16y-16}{y^4-2y^3+4y-4}\,dy=\pi\), each of its four sums being an integral of \(x^{j-1}/(1-x^8)\).
    • Splitting the sum at \(k=n\) and reducing \(16^{n-k}\) modulo \(8k+j\) gives the digits at position \(n\) in \(O(n\log n)\) time and constant memory. The measured time grew with slope 1.06 on a log–log scale, to about 2 seconds at \(n=10^7\).
    • In double precision the error of \(\{16^n\pi\}\) grows like \(\sqrt n\,2^{-53}\). Eight digits per evaluation were all correct up to \(n=20{,}000\), and ten were not. At \(n\approx10^6\) and \(10^7\), 10 and 8 digits matched the published values.
    • algorithms::pi_hex_digits (Machin's formula on 32-bit limbs) gives exact reference digits and agrees with MPFR for the first 100,000. The first 50,000 pass \(\chi^2\) tests for uniform digits and digit pairs, consistent with the unproved conjecture that \(\pi\) is normal in base 16.
