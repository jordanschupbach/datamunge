    The Sieve of Atkin: Primes From Quadratic Forms

    Abstract

    The Sieve of Eratosthenes finds primes by crossing out multiples. The Sieve of Atkin (Atkin and Bernstein, 2004) instead flips a flag for every solution of a quadratic form such as \(4x^2+y^2=n\), keeps the numbers flipped an odd number of times, and removes those with a square factor. We derive why this works for the first form from Jacobi's two-square theorem, \(r_2(n)=4\,(d_1(n)-d_3(n))\), and check the parity rule against every squarefree number up to 20,000. We count the work of both sieves and compare it with formulas derived from lattice-point areas and Mertens' theorem. Atkin visits about \(1.04N\) lattice points while Eratosthenes makes about \(N(\ln\ln N-\ln2+0.26)\) crossings, so in operation counts Atkin pulls ahead, by a factor of 2.15 at \(N=10^8\). In wall-clock time, however, the simple versions here are within 10% of each other, because Atkin's work per point (a square, a remainder and a branch) costs more than a crossing. The primes up to \(10^8\) then serve as data: Riemann's \(R(x)\) predicts \(\pi(x)=5{,}761{,}455\) at \(10^8\) to within 97, far better than \(x/\ln x\) or \(\mathrm{li}(x)\), and primes \(\equiv3\pmod4\) lead those \(\equiv1\pmod4\) at all but 0.03% of \(x\le10^8\) (Chebyshev's bias), with the first lead change at 26,861.

    Primes from quadratic forms

    The sieve rests on a classical fact about sums of two squares. For any \(n\ge1\), let \(r_2(n)\) count the integer pairs \((a,b)\), signs and order included, with \(a^2+b^2=n\), and let \(d_1(n)\) and \(d_3(n)\) count the divisors of \(n\) that are \(\equiv1\) and \(\equiv3\pmod4\). Jacobi's two-square theorem says

    \[r_2(n)=4\,\bigl(d_1(n)-d_3(n)\bigr).\]

    The first form, derived

    Take \(n\equiv1\pmod4\) that is not a perfect square. In any solution of \(a^2+b^2=n\) one of \(a,b\) is even and the other odd, since \(n\) is odd, and neither is zero. The 4 sign choices leave \(r_2(n)/4\) solutions in positive integers, and swapping \(a\) and \(b\) pairs each solution with even \(b\) with one with even \(a\). So exactly half, \(r_2(n)/8\), have \(b\) even. Writing \(b=2x\) and \(a=y\), these are the positive solutions of \(4x^2+y^2=n\):

    \[\#\{x,y\ge1 : 4x^2+y^2=n\}=\frac{d_1(n)-d_3(n)}{2}.\]

    Now let \(n\) be squarefree, \(n=p_1\cdots p_k\). The function \(\chi(d)=+1,-1,0\) for \(d\equiv1,3\) or even is multiplicative, and \(d_1-d_3=\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d)=\prod_i(1+\chi(p_i))\). If some \(p_i\equiv3\pmod4\) the product is 0, so the count is 0. Otherwise every \(p_i\equiv1\pmod4\) and the product is \(2^k\), so the count is \(2^{k-1}\). That is odd exactly when \(k=1\):

    \[n\ \text{squarefree},\ n\equiv1\!\!\pmod4:\qquad \#\{4x^2+y^2=n\}\ \text{is odd}\iff n\ \text{is prime}.\]

    Atkin's sieve applies this to \(n\equiv1,5\pmod{12}\) (the classes \(\equiv1\pmod4\) not divisible by 3). The same kind of argument, using the Eisenstein integers and the form \(x^2+3y^2\), gives the other two rules: for squarefree \(n\equiv7\pmod{12}\), \(n\) is prime iff \(3x^2+y^2=n\) has an odd number of positive solutions, and for squarefree \(n\equiv11\pmod{12}\), iff \(3x^2-y^2=n\) with \(x>y\) does. The remaining classes mod 12 are even or divisible by 3, so 2 and 3 are added by hand. A number with a square factor can pass the parity test: for example \(25=4\cdot2^2+3^2\) is its only representation. So the sieve finishes by clearing every multiple of \(p^2\) for the primes \(p\) it has found.

    The block checks the rule directly with dm::algorithms::atkin_form_representations() for the numbers up to 100, and then for every squarefree number up to 20,000.

    Every squarefree number checked obeys the parity rule: 6,076 of 6,076 up to 20,000. The table shows the three ways it can go. Primes such as 13 and 97 have exactly one representation. \(65=5\cdot13\) and \(85=5\cdot17\), products of two primes \(\equiv1\pmod4\), have two (\(65=4\cdot1^2+7^2=4\cdot4^2+1^2\)), matching \(2^{k-1}=2\). \(35=5\cdot7\) has none. The exception is \(25\), which is not squarefree, has one representation and is cleared only by the final pass; 375 numbers up to 20,000 are rescued this way.

    The figure shows why only about a third of the points flip a flag (0.31 of \(N\) in the next table). For the first form, an even \(y\) makes \(n\) even, and the remaining residues mod 12 are partly multiples of 3, so the grey points cannot hold primes. Bernstein's version skips such points in advance by working with residues mod 60.

    How much work?

    Both sieves use \(O(N)\) memory. Their operation counts can be predicted.

    *Atkin.* The number of lattice points with \(x,y\ge1\) under a curve is close to the area under it. The quarter ellipses \(4x^2+y^2\le N\) and \(3x^2+y^2\le N\) have areas \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt N}{2}\cdot\sqrt N=\tfrac{\pi N}{8}\) and \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N/3}\sqrt N=\tfrac{\pi N}{4\sqrt3}\). The region \(3x^2-y^2\le N\), \(0<y<x\) has area \(\tfrac N4-\tfrac{N}{\sqrt3}\int_1^{\sqrt{3/2}}\!\sqrt{u^2-1}\,du\approx0.190N\). In total

    \[\text{lattice points}\approx\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{8}+\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt3}+0.190\Bigr)N\approx1.036\,N .\]

    The final pass clears \(N/p^2\) multiples for each prime \(p\ge5\), about \(N\sum_{p\ge5}p^{-2}=N\,(P(2)-\tfrac14-\tfrac19)\approx0.091N\), where \(P(2)=\sum_p p^{-2}=0.4522\) is the prime zeta function.

    *Eratosthenes.* Crossing out the multiples of \(p\) from \(p^2\) costs \((N-p^2)/p\) steps, so the total is \(N\sum_{p\le\sqrt N}\tfrac1p-\sum_{p\le\sqrt N}p\). Mertens' second theorem, \(\sum_{p\le x}1/p=\ln\ln x+M+o(1)\) with \(M=0.2615\), gives

    \[\text{crossings}\approx N\bigl(\ln\ln\sqrt N+M\bigr)=N\bigl(\ln\ln N-\ln2+M\bigr),\]

    which grows, slowly, faster than \(N\). The ratio of the two counts therefore grows like \(\ln\ln N\): the asymptotic advantage of Atkin. Bernstein's optimized version also enumerates only the residues mod 60 that can flip a flag, cutting its work to \(O(N/\ln\ln N)\).

    The counts match the derivation. Atkin's lattice points per number converge to 1.036 (the area formula gives 1.0362; the shortfall at small \(N\) is the boundary of the region, of order \(\sqrt N\)). The square removals approach \(P(2)-\tfrac14-\tfrac19=0.0911\). Eratosthenes' crossings per number grow from 1.41 to 2.43 and stay 0.056 to 0.09 below \(\ln\ln N-\ln2+M\): the sum \(\sum_{p\le\sqrt N}p\) that the formula drops is about \(N/\ln N\), which is 0.054 of \(N\) at \(10^8\).

    So at \(N=10^8\) Atkin does 1.13 operations per number against Eratosthenes' 2.43, and the gap grows like \(\ln\ln N\). Yet the timings (about 130 ms against 140 ms on this machine) are nearly equal. Atkin's inner loop computes a quadratic form and a remainder mod 12 and branches, while a crossing is a single strided store. Both write to a bit array far larger than the cache, so memory traffic dominates; practical sieves therefore segment the range, and the mod 60 wheel of Bernstein's primegen is what makes Atkin competitive in practice.

    The primes up to 10⁸ as data

    How many primes?

    The prime number theorem says \(\pi(x)\sim x/\ln x\). Gauss's better guess is the logarithmic integral \(\mathrm{li}(x)=\int_0^x dt/\ln t\), and Riemann's is \(R(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\tfrac{\mu(n)}{n}\mathrm{li}(x^{1/n})\), which subtracts the overcount of prime powers that \(\mathrm{li}\) implicitly includes. logarithmic_integral() and riemann_prime_count() evaluate both.

    The table confirms \(\pi(10^8)=5{,}761{,}455\). The estimate \(x/\ln x\) is low by 5.8% at \(10^8\). \(\mathrm{li}(x)\) is high by 754, and \(R(x)\) by 97. In the scaled plot, \(\pi-R\) fluctuates around zero with amplitude about \(\tfrac12\sqrt x/\ln x\), the size the Riemann hypothesis predicts for the oscillating terms from the zeros of \(\zeta\). \(\pi-\mathrm{li}\) sits below zero, tending towards \(-1\). This follows from the second term of \(R\): \(\mathrm{li}(x)-R(x)\approx\tfrac12\mathrm{li}(\sqrt x)\approx\sqrt x/\ln x\), which counts the prime squares \(\mathrm{li}\) implicitly includes. \(\pi(x)<\mathrm{li}(x)\) holds for every \(x\) anyone has computed, yet Littlewood proved it fails infinitely often; the first failure lies beyond \(10^{19}\).

    Chebyshev's bias

    Every odd prime is \(\equiv1\) or \(\equiv3\pmod4\), and by Dirichlet's theorem the two classes are equally common in the limit. In 1853 Chebyshev noticed that the class \(3\bmod4\) is nearly always ahead. The sieve's first rule makes the classes concrete: the primes \(\equiv1\pmod4\) are exactly those that are sums of two squares.

    Primes \(\equiv1\pmod4\) first pull ahead at \(x=26{,}861\), and over all \(x\le10^8\) they lead at only 30,624 integers, 0.03% of the range; the dip near \(1.2\times10^7\) in the plot is one of those spells. The largest lead of \(3\bmod4\) is 1,012 and the lead at \(10^8\) is 446.

    The scaled lead hovers around 1, as the explicit formula predicts. The squares of odd primes are all \(\equiv1\pmod4\), so the prime powers counted by the natural weighted sums are lopsided towards class 1 by about \(\pi(\sqrt x)\approx 2\sqrt x/\ln x\), and the primes alone must lean the other way by half that, \(\sqrt x/\ln x\). Rubinstein and Sarnak (1994) showed, assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis, that class 3 leads on a set of logarithmic density 0.9959, so class 1 does lead infinitely often, but rarely.

    Summary

    • For squarefree \(n\equiv1\pmod4\), the number of positive solutions of \(4x^2+y^2=n\) is \((d_1-d_3)/2\), which is odd exactly when \(n\) is prime. All 6,076 squarefree numbers up to 20,000 obey the parity rules; non-squarefree numbers such as 25 need the final pass.
    • Atkin visits \(1.036N\) lattice points plus \(0.091N\) square removals, as the area and prime-zeta formulas predict, against Eratosthenes' \(N(\ln\ln N-\ln2+M)\) crossings: 2.15 times as many at \(10^8\). The simple implementations still run in about the same time.
    • \(R(x)\) is within 97 of \(\pi(10^8)=5{,}761{,}455\); \(\mathrm{li}(x)\) overshoots by about \(\sqrt x/\ln x\), the prime-square correction.
    • Primes \(\equiv3\pmod4\) lead at all but 0.03% of \(x\le10^8\), by about \(\sqrt x/\ln x\) on average.
