    Chan's Algorithm

    Abstract

    Given \(n\) points in the plane, the convex-hull problem asks for the corners of the smallest convex polygon that contains them all, in order round the boundary. Hulls underlie collision tests, shape summaries, outlier peeling and many other geometric algorithms, so their cost matters, and it should depend on the size of the answer: a hull often has far fewer than \(n\) corners. Graham scan finds the hull in \(O(n\log n)\) time. Gift wrapping takes \(O(nh)\), where \(h\) is the number of hull vertices. Chan (1996) combined the two into an \(O(n\log h)\) algorithm, which is optimal. It guesses \(m\), hulls groups of \(m\) points, and gift-wraps the groups for at most \(m\) steps, finding each group's best candidate by binary search. If the wrap does not close, it squares \(m\) and tries again. We derive the three facts that make this work and trace both rounds on twenty points. We then count orientation tests on random clouds whose hulls have \(\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})\), \(\Theta(\log n)\), \(\Theta(n^{1/3})\) and \(n\) vertices. Chan's count stays below \(9\,n\log_2 h\) in every case, while doubling \(m\) instead of squaring it grows like \(n\log^2 h\). Its constant is large, though. Graham scan makes at most \(1.3\,n\log_2 n\) tests, and Chan's algorithm made fewer in only 2 of 24 runs, both on hulls of a dozen vertices. In wall-clock time it was 2 to 9 times slower than Graham scan in every case. Finally, we peel the Old Faithful eruptions into 21 convex layers. The innermost layer lies inside the larger cluster, but points in the empty gap between the clusters are rated as central as the core of the smaller cluster, because convex depth ignores density.

    The problem

    The convex hull of a finite set \(S\) of \(n\) points in the plane is the smallest convex polygon containing it. Its corners, the hull vertices, are points of \(S\); let \(h\) be their number. Every function in geometry/hull_algorithms.hpp returns them counterclockwise, without points that lie on an edge.

    Everything below is built from one primitive, the orientation test

    \[\operatorname{orient}(a,b,c)=(b_x-a_x)(c_y-a_y)-(b_y-a_y)(c_x-a_x),\]

    which is positive when \(c\) lies left of the directed line \(a\to b\), negative when it lies to the right, and zero when the three points are collinear. We measure the cost of a hull algorithm by how many orientation tests it makes, since that count does not depend on the machine.

    Two lower bounds frame the problem. Sorting reduces to hulls: the points \((x_i,x_i^2)\) are all hull vertices, and reading the hull in order sorts the \(x_i\). So \(\Omega(n\log n)\) tests are needed in the worst case, which Graham scan attains. But when \(h\) is small this is not the right yardstick. Kirkpatrick and Seidel (1986) proved the sharper bound \(\Omega(n\log h)\). Gift wrapping (Jarvis march) costs \(n-2\) tests per hull vertex, \(O(nh)\) in all, which is good only when \(h<\log n\). Chan's algorithm meets the \(n\log h\) bound for every \(h\).

    The method

    One wrap step

    Gift wrapping walks round the hull. From a hull vertex \(p\), the next vertex \(q\) is the point with no point of \(S\) strictly right of \(p\to q\) (if several points qualify, all collinear with \(p\), take the farthest). Write \(a\prec_p b\) when \(a\) lies right of \(p\to b\), so \(a\) is more clockwise than \(b\) as seen from \(p\). Because \(p\) is a hull vertex, all of \(S\) lies in a half-plane through \(p\), so \(\prec_p\) orders \(S\setminus\{p\}\) by angle, and \(q\) is its minimum.

    Lemma 1 (groups). Split \(S\) into groups \(G_1,\dots,G_r\). Then the minimum of \(\prec_p\) over \(S\) is the minimum over \(i\) of the group minima, and each group minimum is a vertex of \(\operatorname{CH}(G_i)\).

    Proof. The minimum of a union is the minimum of the minima. For one group, let \(q_i\) be its minimum. Every point of \(G_i\) is left of or on the line \(p\to q_i\), so that line supports \(\operatorname{CH}(G_i)\) at \(q_i\). A supporting point is on the boundary, and taking the farthest of collinear points makes it a corner. \(\blacksquare\)

    So each wrap step only needs one candidate per group: the vertex of the group's hull where the line from \(p\) touches it. This is the tangent from \(p\).

    The tangent by binary search

    Let the group hull have vertices \(v_0,\dots,v_{k-1}\) counterclockwise, and let \(p\) be outside it. The orientation test is unchanged by cyclic shifts, so \(\operatorname{orient}(p,v_i,v_{i+1})=\operatorname{orient}(v_i,v_{i+1},p)\). The left side says whether the direction from \(p\) turns counterclockwise from \(v_i\) to \(v_{i+1}\). The right side says whether \(p\) is left of edge \(i\), that is, whether edge \(i\) faces away from \(p\).

    Lemma 2 (bitonic directions). As \(i\) runs round the polygon, the direction from \(p\) to \(v_i\) turns clockwise along the edges that face \(p\) and counterclockwise along the others. The edges that face \(p\) form one contiguous run. So the directions form a cyclic bitonic sequence: they rise from the minimum (the tangent we want) to a maximum (the other tangent), then fall back.

    Proof. The first part is the identity above. For the second, let \(\nu_i\) be the outward unit normal of edge \(i\). The edge faces \(p\) exactly when \(\nu_i\cdot p>\max_{x\in P}\nu_i\cdot x\), that is, when \(\nu_i\) lies in every open half-circle \(\{\nu:\nu\cdot(p-x)>0\}\) for \(x\) in the polygon \(P\). An intersection of open half-circles is a single arc. The outward normals turn monotonically as \(i\) goes round, so the edges whose normals fall in that arc are consecutive. \(\blacksquare\)

    The minimum of a cyclic bitonic sequence can be found by halving. Keep a chain \(v_a,\dots,v_b\) that contains the minimum, starting from the whole cycle. Probe the middle vertex \(c\). One test says whether edge \(c\) rises, a second whether edge \(c-1\) falls (if both, \(v_c\) is the minimum), and comparing the slopes and heights at \(a\) and \(c\) decides which half keeps the minimum. Each halving costs at most five tests, so a group of size \(m\) is queried in \(O(\log m)\). geometry::convex_polygon_tangent implements this and returns its probes.

    Rounds with a guess

    Suppose we knew a number \(m\ge h\). Split the points into \(r=\lceil n/m\rceil\) groups of at most \(m\) and hull each one with Graham scan. Then wrap: each of the \(h\le m\) steps asks every group for its tangent. The cost is

    \[\underbrace{r\cdot O(m\log m)}_{\text{group hulls}}+\underbrace{h\cdot r\cdot O(\log m)}_{\text{wrap}} =O(n\log m)\quad\text{when } h\le m.\]

    Without knowing \(h\), run the wrap for at most \(m\) steps. If it returns to the start, the hull is done. If not, \(h>m\): discard the round and square the guess. Starting from \(m_0=4\), round \(t\) uses \(m_t=2^{2^{t+1}}\), so \(\log_2 m_t=2^{t+1}\), and each round costs at most \(c\,n\log_2 m_t\) for some constant \(c\). If round \(T\) is the first to close, then \(m_{T-1}<h\) and \(\log_2 m_T=2\log_2 m_{T-1}<2\log_2 h\). The round costs form a geometric series:

    \[\sum_{t=0}^{T}c\,n\,2^{t+1}<c\,n\,2^{T+2}=2c\,n\log_2 m_T<4c\,n\log_2 h .\]

    This is \(O(n\log h)\), matching the lower bound. Squaring matters. Doubling the guess, \(m_t=4\cdot2^t\), takes about \(\log_2 h\) rounds whose costs \(c\,n(t+2)\) add up to roughly \(\tfrac c2 n\log_2^2 h\). Squaring wastes at most a factor of 2 in the last round, since \(m_T\) can overshoot up to \(h^2\), but it makes the failed rounds cost less than the last one.

    Two details keep the total honest. Duplicates are removed by hashing, because sorting would already cost \(n\log n\). And the groups are consecutive runs of the input, in any order: Lemma 1 holds for every partition.

    Simple examples

    Twenty points, two rounds

    Take twenty points with an 8-vertex hull. Round 0 guesses \(m=4\): five groups of four, and a wrap that is cut off after four steps, halfway round. Round 1 guesses \(m=16\): two groups, and the wrap closes after eight steps. chans_algorithm_trace records both rounds when record_geometry is set.

    The groups are runs of the input, so in round 0 their hulls overlap and cross; Lemma 1 does not care. The wrap starts at the lowest point and takes four steps, then stops at vertex 3 because \(h=8>4\). That round is wasted, but cheap: 134 tests. In round 1 the first group happens to hold all eight hull vertices, so its hull is the answer, and each of the eight steps compares two candidates, one from each group. Most of round 1's tests (119 of 143) go into the two Graham scans, which is the \(n\log m\) term of the cost.

    One tangent query

    The wrap spends most of its tests in tangent queries. Here is one: a 51-vertex polygon (the hull of 3,000 random points in the unit disc) and an outside point \(p\). The binary search probes vertex 0, then halves the cycle.

    51 vertices; probes [0, 25, 38, 31, 28]; tangent vertex 28; 17 orientation tests (a linear scan needs 50; the bound is \(5\lceil\log_2 k\rceil + O(1)\), here \(5\cdot6\)).

    The first probe, vertex 0, is the leftmost vertex. Probe 2 lands on vertex 25 on the right and probe 3 on vertex 38 near the top. Each answer halves the chain that must hold the most clockwise direction, and probes 4 and 5 close in on vertex 28, where the line from \(p\) leaves the whole polygon on its left. The search needed 17 tests; a scan needs 50. Every group is queried at every step of a round, so this \(\log m\) is what keeps a round at \(O(n\log m)\).

    Checking the theory

    Clouds with different hull sizes

    geometry::random_point_cloud draws points from shapes whose expected hull sizes are known (Rényi and Sulanke 1963): \(\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})\) for a Gaussian, \(\Theta(\log n)\) in a square, \(\Theta(n^{1/3})\) in a disc, and \(h=n\) on the parabola \(y=x^2\), where every point is a vertex. Coordinates are rounded to multiples of \(2^{-20}\), so every orientation test is exact. For each cloud and \(n=2^{10},2^{12},\dots,2^{20}\) we count the orientation tests of Chan's algorithm (squaring and doubling the guess) and of Graham scan.

    Over all 24 runs the largest Chan ratio is 8.90. Chan's algorithm made fewer tests than Graham scan in 2 runs: Gaussian at \(n=2^{16}\) (h = 12), Gaussian at \(n=2^{18}\) (h = 14).

    The bound holds: every run is under \(9\,n\log_2 h\). Doubling the guess bends upwards as predicted: at \(h=2^{20}\) it costs 50 times \(n\log_2 h\), against 8.7 for squaring.

    The blue points are not on a straight line, though. The cost depends on how far the last guess overshoots: the Gaussian cloud at \(n=2^{20}\) has \(h=19\), just above the guess 16, so it pays for a round with \(m=256\) and its ratio jumps from 5.5 to 8.2. On the parabola the last guess is capped at \(n\) (for \(n=2^{12}\), \(m=4096\) instead of \(2^{16}\)), which makes the last round cheaper and flattens the middle of the plot.

    The constant is what matters in practice. Graham scan's sort costs about \(1.3\log_2 n\) tests per point (1.1 on the parabola). Chan's algorithm costs 5 to 9 times \(\log_2 h\), so it makes fewer tests only when \(\log_2 h\) is below a quarter to a sixth of \(\log_2 n\): for a million points, a hull of 10 to 25 vertices, depending on how far the last guess overshoots. That happened twice, on the Gaussian clouds with 12 and 14 vertices.

    Why the tangents must be binary searches

    Replacing the binary search by a scan of each group hull keeps the hull correct but changes the cost of a step from \(r\log m\) to the total size of the group hulls. On the parabola every point is a vertex of its group's hull, so a step costs \(n\) tests and a failed round \(nm\).

It is not quadratic here only because the last round has \(m=n\): a single group, whose hull is the answer, needs no tangent queries at all. The failed round with \(m=256\) does the damage, at \(256\) steps times \(n\) tests. For \(n=2^8\) the scan is slightly cheaper, since the groups are so small that binary search has nothing to save.

    Wall-clock time

    Counting tests ignores memory traffic and the bookkeeping of groups. We time the three \(O(n\log n)\) or better algorithms at \(n=2^{20}\), taking the median of three runs.

    Chan's algorithm is the slowest everywhere: about twice Graham scan's time on the Gaussian and square clouds, three times on the disc and eight to nine times on the parabola, and four to eleven times the monotone chain's. Even on the Gaussian cloud, where its test count was competitive at smaller sizes, the extra rounds, the group bookkeeping and the scattered memory access of the tangent queries cost more than the sort they avoid. Timings vary between runs; the ranking did not.

    A dataset example: convex layers of Old Faithful

    Peeling a point cloud like an onion gives its convex layers: layer 0 is the hull, layer 1 the hull of what remains, and so on. The layer number is a simple measure of how central a point is (Barnett 1976), and the innermost layer gives a robust "median" of a two-dimensional cloud. Peeling is a natural fit for an output-sensitive hull: each layer is a hull with few vertices, computed from the points that remain. geometry::convex_layers peels with Chan's algorithm, so the total cost is \(O(\sum_j n_j\log h_j)\).

    dm::datasets::faithful() holds 272 eruptions of the Old Faithful geyser: the eruption length and the waiting time before it, both in minutes.

    Deepest layer reached: short eruptions (< 2.5 min, 92 points) 15, the gap (2.5 to 3.5 min, 14 points) 15, long eruptions (> 3.5 min, 166 points) 20. Coordinatewise median: (4, 76).

    The innermost layer, four eruptions near 4.1 minutes and 78 minutes, sits in the larger cluster, close to the coordinatewise median of (4, 76). That is the peeling median doing its job. The layers also show the method's blind spot. Every ring stretches along the diagonal, because a convex layer cannot bend round an empty region. The fourteen eruptions in the gap between 2.5 and 3.5 minutes, which are rare, reach layer 15, as deep as the core of the short-eruption cluster. Convex depth measures position relative to the shape of the whole cloud, not density, so on clustered data it should be computed per cluster.

    Each layer here has 4 to 19 points, so Chan's algorithm makes each peel cost about \(n_j\log h_j\) rather than \(n_j\log n_j\). With 272 points that saving is invisible. Chazelle (1985) showed that all layers together can be found in \(O(n\log n)\).

    Conclusion

    Chan's algorithm reaches the \(\Omega(n\log h)\) lower bound by combining three ideas, each checked above: a wrap step needs only one candidate per group (Lemma 1); that candidate is a tangent, found in \(O(\log m)\) because directions seen from outside a convex polygon are bitonic (Lemma 2); and squaring the guess makes the failed rounds a geometric series dominated by the last. The counts confirm it: under \(9\,n\log_2 h\) on every cloud tested, while doubling the guess grows like \(n\log^2 h\) and scanning the tangents costs three to four times as much.

    The limits are practical. The constant is four to seven times Graham scan's, so on a million points Chan's algorithm only wins on test counts when the hull has about ten vertices, and in time it lost every comparison. Its value is theoretical, as the algorithm that settled the complexity of planar hulls, and as a design pattern: guess an output size, run a budgeted algorithm, and square the budget on failure. For practical work, geometry::convex_hull (the monotone chain) is the fastest choice here.
