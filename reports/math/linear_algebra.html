    Linear Algebra: Transformations, Systems and Projections

    Abstract

    Given a linear rule and some observations, we want to compute its output, recover its input, or find the closest output it can produce. These are the matrix problems behind coordinate changes, simultaneous equations and fitting a straight line. This tutorial starts with two dimensions: a matrix sends a square to a parallelogram, a linear system finds the intersection of two lines, and eigenvectors reveal directions that the matrix only stretches. A three-point least-squares example then shows an orthogonal projection. Finally, a controlled perturbation checks exactly how a condition number predicts sensitivity. All inputs are small constructed examples; no external dataset is needed.

    The method: a matrix is a linear map

    A vector \(x\in\mathbb R^n\) is a column of coordinates. A matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) maps it to \(Ax\in\mathbb R^m\). Its columns \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) are the images of the standard basis vectors, so

    \[Ax=\sum_{j=1}^n x_j a_j,\qquad A(\alpha x+\beta z)=\alpha Ax+\beta Az.\]

    This gives a geometric reading of multiplication: scale each column by its input coordinate and add. Matrix multiplication composes maps: \(BAx=B(Ax)\), so the rightmost map acts first. The transpose \(A^\top\) exchanges rows and columns, and the dot product \(x^\top z\) measures their alignment. The Euclidean length is \(\lVert x\rVert_2=\sqrt{x^\top x}\); perpendicular vectors have zero dot product.

    The diagram uses \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\). Its columns are independent, so every output has a unique input. Its determinant is \(2\cdot2-1\cdot1=3\): areas grow by a factor of three and orientation is preserved.

    The column space contains all possible outputs. Its dimension is the rank. The null space contains inputs sent to zero. Rank plus null-space dimension equals \(n\): independent columns give independent input directions, and every remaining direction can be combined with them to make a null vector. Thus a square full-rank matrix has a unique solution to \(Ax=b\). A rank-deficient matrix can have no solution or infinitely many, depending on whether \(b\) lies in its column space.

    For a square system, dm::linalg::lu(A) factors \(PA=LU\), where \(P\) permutes rows, \(L\) is lower triangular and \(U\) is upper triangular. Solving \(Lz=Pb\) and then \(Ux=z\) recovers the input. Partial pivoting chooses the largest available absolute entry in each pivot column. Factorization takes \(O(n^3)\) operations; each further right-hand side takes \(O(n^2)\). Factor once and reuse .solve() for several observations.

    All C++ blocks below are independent complete programs using <dm/dm.hpp>. DenseMatrix initialisers list entries row by row, and element indices start at zero. Program loops only prepare plot coordinates or display results; the library performs the factorizations.

    Simple examples

    Recovering the input: two intersecting equations

    Take \(b=(4,5)^\top\). The rows of \(Ax=b\) give two lines. Elimination exposes the intermediate equation and the answer:

    \[\begin{aligned} 2x_1+x_2&=4,\\ x_1+2x_2&=5,\\ \text{row 2}-\tfrac12\text{row 1}:\quad \tfrac32x_2&=3, \qquad x_2=2,\quad x_1=1. \end{aligned}\]

    Equivalently, the output is the column combination \(b=1(2,1)^\top+2(1,2)^\top\). The program solves the system and checks the result by multiplying back. Forming the inverse would add work when only a solution is needed.

    Eigenvectors: directions that do not turn

    An eigenvector \(v\ne0\) satisfies \(Av=\lambda v\). For this symmetric matrix the sum and difference of the two coordinate directions give

    \[v_+=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad Av_+=3v_+, \qquad v_-=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\quad Av_-=v_-.\]

    Since these vectors are orthonormal, every vector has a unique decomposition \(x=c_+v_++c_-v_-\), where \(c_\pm=v_\pm^\top x\). Applying the map gives \(Ax=3c_+v_++c_-v_-\): resolve along the two axes, stretch, and recombine. In matrix notation this is \(A=V\Lambda V^\top\). A unit circle therefore becomes an ellipse with semiaxes 3 and 1. Their product is the area multiplier, matching the determinant.

    Eigenvector signs are arbitrary: \(-v\) has the same eigenvalue as \(v\). The residual and reconstruction checks avoid depending on a particular sign convention. The real orthogonal decomposition used here applies to symmetric matrices; general real matrices need not have a real eigenbasis. Singular values describe stretching even in that general case.

    Least squares: the closest attainable output

    Fit a line \(y\approx\beta_0+\beta_1t\) through the three points \((0,1),(1,2),(2,4)\). There are more equations than unknowns, and no line passes through all three. The columns of the design matrix span a plane in three-dimensional observation space. Least squares projects \(y\) onto that plane:

    \[X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{pmatrix},\qquad \widehat\beta=\arg\min_\beta\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2.\]

    Differentiate the squared length: its gradient is \(-2X^\top(y-X\beta)\). At a minimum \(X^\top r=0\), where \(r=y-X\widehat\beta\). The residual is perpendicular to every column of \(X\). Here the normal equations and their solution are

    \[\begin{pmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}\widehat\beta =\begin{pmatrix}7\\10\end{pmatrix},\qquad \widehat\beta=\begin{pmatrix}5/6\\3/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad r=\begin{pmatrix}1/6\\-1/3\\1/6\end{pmatrix},\quad \lVert r\rVert_2^2=1/6.\]

    Orthogonality also proves optimality: for any coefficient change \(h\), \(\lVert r-Xh\rVert_2^2=\lVert r\rVert_2^2+\lVert Xh\rVert_2^2\). It proves uniqueness when the columns of \(X\) are independent. In particular, the two checks here are \(\sum r_i=0\) and \(\sum t_i r_i=0\).

    The program uses the default Householder QR solver. With \(X=QR\), orthogonality of \(Q\) preserves distances; a triangular solve recovers the coefficients without forming \(X^\top X\). This matters because forming the normal equations squares the 2-norm condition number. If columns are dependent, QR rejects the design; select dm::linalg::LeastSquaresMethod::SVD to obtain a minimum-norm solution instead.

    Checking the theory: sensitivity is a property of the problem

    A small residual does not by itself guarantee an accurate input. For nonsingular \(A\), perturbing only the right-hand side gives \(A\delta x=\delta b\), hence

    \[\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert_2}{\lVert x\rVert_2} \le\underbrace{\lVert A\rVert_2\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_2}_{\kappa_2(A)} \frac{\lVert\delta b\rVert_2}{\lVert b\rVert_2},\qquad \kappa_2(A)=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}.\]

    The bound follows from \(\lVert\delta x\rVert\le\lVert A^{-1}\rVert\lVert\delta b\rVert\) and \(\lVert b\rVert\le\lVert A\rVert\lVert x\rVert\). To attain it, use \(A_\tau=\operatorname{diag}(1,\tau)\), \(b=(1,0)^\top\) and \(\delta b=(0,10^{-8})^\top\), for \(0<\tau\le1\). Then \(x=(1,0)^\top\), \(\delta x=(0,10^{-8}/\tau)^\top\), and \(\kappa_2(A_\tau)=1/\tau\). We change the input deliberately; this experiment measures data sensitivity, not floating-point error introduced by the solver.

    The final system is nonsingular, but its weak second direction magnifies the input change by \(10^8\). At \(\tau=0\) it loses rank: the second input coordinate becomes invisible, and the perturbed observation cannot be matched at all. This is why numerical rank decisions and measurement precision matter as well as the solver's residual.

    Conclusion

    Columns explain a transformation; rows explain its simultaneous equations. Eigenvectors expose invariant directions, and least squares finds the closest output when observations cannot be matched exactly. LU, the symmetric eigensolver and Householder QR reproduce the small examples above, with residual and reconstruction checks. The diagonal sweep attains the condition-number bound and shows how an accurate solve can still be sensitive to data. These examples concern small real dense matrices. For factorization derivations, rank-deficient fits and dataset applications, continue with the dense linear algebra report at ../numerics/linear_algebra/dense_linear_algebra.mepml.
