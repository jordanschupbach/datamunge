    Best Bin First

    Abstract

    Best Bin First searches a k-d tree in order of a lower bound on each cell's distance to the query, and stops after a fixed budget of distance computations. We derive the two lower bounds in use, the distance to the splitting hyperplane (Beis and Lowe) and the incrementally maintained distance to the whole cell (Arya and Mount). We then test both on the 64-dimensional handwritten digits of dm::datasets::digits() with the new geometry::BestBinFirstKd. Exact search examines 86% of the 1,500 indexed digits and is slower than a plain scan. With a budget of 128 points, Best Bin First finds the exact nearest neighbour for 71% of queries, returns one only 3% farther on average, and classifies digits as accurately as exact 1-NN (95.6% against 94.6%), at under a third of the cost of the scan. Projecting onto principal components shows the curse of dimensionality directly. Exact search touches 0.8% of points in 2 dimensions and 44% in 64, as relative contrast falls from 60 to 2.8. Rotating to principal axes alone halves the work in the full 64 dimensions.

    Nearest neighbours in a k-d tree

    Given \(n\) points in \(\mathbb R^d\) and a query \(q\), the nearest-neighbour problem asks for the point closest to \(q\). A scan costs \(nd\) operations per query. A k-d tree (Bentley 1975) organises the points so that most of them can be ruled out without measuring them. Each internal node splits its points at the median of one coordinate \(a\), so the left child holds those with \(x_a\le v\) and the right child the rest. Splitting stops at buckets of at most \(b\) points, giving a tree of depth \(\lceil\log_2(n/b)\rceil\). Each node owns a box-shaped cell. geometry::BestBinFirstKd splits on the coordinate of largest spread, which adapts the tree to data that vary in only some directions.

    A lower bound for every cell

    The search needs, for every cell \(C\), a lower bound on the distance from \(q\) to any point inside. The exact distance to a box is a sum over axes:

    \[d(q,C)^2=\sum_{a=1}^{d}o_a^2,\qquad o_a=\begin{cases}\ell_a-q_a&q_a<\ell_a\\0&\ell_a\le q_a\le u_a\\q_a-u_a&q_a>u_a\end{cases}\]

    for the cell \([\ell_1,u_1]\times\dots\times[\ell_d,u_d]\). Splitting a cell at \(x_a=v\) changes only the interval on axis \(a\). If the query lies on the near side, the near child keeps the parent's bound, and the far child's offset on axis \(a\) becomes \(|q_a-v|\):

    \[d(q,C_{\text{far}})^2=d(q,C)^2-o_a^2+(q_a-v)^2 .\]

    This is Arya and Mount's (1993) incremental distance, an \(O(1)\) update per node once the offsets are stored. Beis and Lowe (1997) used the cheaper bound \((q_a-v)^2\), the distance to the splitting hyperplane alone. Since the hyperplane bound is never larger than the cell bound, both are valid lower bounds, but the cell bound is tighter. BinPriority::Cell and BinPriority::Hyperplane select them.

    The search

    Best Bin First keeps a priority queue of unexplored branches keyed by their lower bound. It pops the smallest, descends to the leaf on the query's side, and queues each far branch it passes. Then it measures the leaf's points and repeats. Two rules end the search:

    • *Pruning.* If the smallest queued bound exceeds the distance to the \(k\)-th best point found, no queued branch can improve the answer, and the result is exact.
    • *Budget.* After a fixed number \(E\) of distance computations, stop and return the best so far.

    Without the budget this is the classic exact search, but visiting cells in order of their bound rather than depth-first. With the budget it is approximate. Because the most promising cells come first, the true neighbour is usually found early. The budget was introduced for matching 128-dimensional SIFT descriptors, where exact search would examine most of the data (Lowe 2004).

    Why high dimensions hurt

    Exact search must open every cell that meets the ball of radius \(r=d(q,\text{NN})\) around \(q\). In high dimensions two things go wrong. A tree on \(n\) points splits only \(\log_2(n/b)\) times along any root-to-leaf path, so with \(d>\log_2(n/b)\) most coordinates are never split, and cells are long slabs. Meanwhile distances concentrate: the nearest neighbour is barely closer than a typical point (Beyer et al. 1999). The ball then reaches across many cells, and the lower bounds of nearly all of them fall below \(r\). We measure both effects by projecting the digits onto their first \(d\) principal components, recording the relative contrast

    \[\mathrm{RC}=\frac{\operatorname{median}_i\,\|q-x_i\|}{\min_i\,\|q-x_i\|},\]

    and counting the points examined by exact search.

    Searching the handwritten digits

    The digits are a hard case for a k-d tree. With a tree only \(\log_2(1500/8)\approx 8\) levels deep and 64 coordinates, exact search computes 1,292 distances per query with the cell key, 86% of the index, and 1,434 with the hyperplane key. Managing the priority queue then costs more than it saves: exact search takes 0.044 ms per query against 0.02 ms for brute force on this machine. Best Bin First pays off once the budget bites. Recall, the share of queries whose exact nearest neighbour is found, grows roughly linearly in \(\log E\): 0.15 at 8 points, 0.53 at 64, 0.84 at 256 and 1.0 at 1,024. The cell key is ahead of the hyperplane key at every budget, by up to 0.04 at \(E=128\), because its tighter bounds order the queue better.

    For classification the misses hardly matter. When the search misses the nearest neighbour it returns a close runner-up, 7.5% farther on average at \(E=64\) and 3.3% at \(E=128\), which is usually the same digit. Accuracy reaches 93.6% at 64 points and 95.6% at 128, against 94.6% for exact 1-NN. The difference of three queries out of 297 in favour of the budgeted search is noise. At \(E=128\) a query costs 0.006 ms, about a third of the scan and a seventh of exact tree search.

    The curse of dimensionality

    To separate the effect of dimension from that of the data, we project the digits onto their first \(d\) principal components (stats::PCA, centred, not scaled) for \(d=2,4,\dots,64\). We rebuild the tree in each dimension and measure exact search and a fixed budget of 64 points.

    In two principal components the tree works as designed. The nearest neighbour is 60 times closer than the median point, exact search opens two leaves, and it measures 12 points, 0.8% of the index. Each doubling of the dimension weakens both. The relative contrast falls to 5.5 at \(d=8\) and 2.8 at \(d=64\). The share of points examined grows from 2.5% at \(d=4\) to 10% at \(d=8\), then jumps to 26% at \(d=16\), just as \(d\) passes the tree depth of about 8 levels and some coordinates stop being split at all. It reaches 44% at \(d=64\). Recall at a fixed budget of 64 points falls in step, from 1.0 to 0.84.

    The most striking number is the last. With all 64 components, distances are exactly those of the raw pixels, since PCA is a rotation. Yet exact search examines 44% of the points in principal axes against 86% in pixel axes. Axis-aligned cells fit data whose variance lies along the axes. In pixel space the variance is spread over correlated directions, every split cuts across them, and the cells are poor. Rotating first, as randomised and PCA k-d trees do (Silpa-Anan and Hartley 2008), is the cheapest improvement available.

    Summary

    • A k-d cell's distance to the query can be maintained in \(O(1)\) per node: \(d(q,C_{\text{far}})^2=d(q,C)^2-o_a^2+(q_a-v)^2\). Both it and the hyperplane distance are lower bounds, so a best-first search that prunes on them is exact. BestBinFirstKd confirms this against brute force for both keys.
    • On the 64-dimensional digits, exact tree search examines 86% of the points and is slower than a scan. A budget of 128 points finds the exact neighbour 71% of the time, and 1-NN accuracy is as good as exact (95.6% against 94.6%) at a third of the scan's cost.
    • The cell key beats Beis and Lowe's hyperplane key at every budget, by up to 4 points of recall, and needs 10% fewer distance computations for exact search.
    • Through PCA the curse of dimensionality is measurable: as the dimension rises from 2 to 64, relative contrast falls from 60 to 2.8 and exact search work rises from 0.8% to 44%. The jump comes when the dimension passes the tree depth.
    • Rotating the data to principal axes halves exact-search work at the same 64 dimensions, a free gain for axis-aligned trees.
