    Xiaolin Wu's Line Algorithm: How Two Pixels Share a Line

    Abstract

    A line between pixel centres usually passes between rows or columns. Wu's two-pixel antialiasing splits ink between the two adjacent centres. We derive the split from conservation of weight and first moment, distinguish it from exact area coverage, and account for fractional endpoint columns. The existing full-column API is retained alongside an explicit clipped endpoint policy. The built-in air_passengers dataset supplies a real polyline: rendering it at two small resolutions shows both the benefit of fractional weights and the information a small raster cannot preserve.

    The data: a monthly airline series

    dm::datasets::air_passengers() contains 144 monthly observations from 1949 to 1960. passengers is the number of international airline passengers in thousands. The frame stores year and month separately; a calendar index puts successive records one unit apart. The increasing level and seasonal peaks are features of the observations, not consequences of antialiasing.

    Deriving the two-pixel split

    After exchanging axes for a steep line and ordering endpoints, the major coordinate increases and the absolute minor-axis slope is at most one. For a major-axis centre \(i\), the ideal minor coordinate is \(y(i)=y_0+m(i-x_0)\). Write \(j=\lfloor y(i)\rfloor\) and \(f=y(i)-j\). If the column's total ink weight is \(w_i\), require both conservation of that weight and the correct weighted centre:

    \[\alpha+\beta=w_i,\qquad j\alpha+(j+1)\beta=w_i y(i) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha=w_i(1-f),\qquad\beta=w_i f.\]

    For an interior column, \(w_i=1\). Nonnegative weights follow from \(0\le f<1\). The first-moment identity explains the split more precisely than saying one pixel is always the nearer one: the upper pixel is nearer when \(f>1/2\). The weights form a linear, triangular filter in the minor direction. They are not the exact intersection areas of a finite-width geometric stroke with square pixels.

    Endpoint conventions change the conservation statement

    The four-argument function retains the previous convention: every rounded major-axis column has weight one, including both endpoints. An integer segment of major-axis length \(D\) therefore has total weight \(D+1\). This useful simple convention differs from endpoint-clipped Wu filtering. The explicit ClippedColumns overload instead uses

    \[w_i=\max\left(0,\min(x_1,i+1/2)-\max(x_0,i-1/2)\right),\qquad \sum_i w_i=x_1-x_0=D.\]

    For separated integer endpoints, the first and last columns have weight one half. The same interval-intersection formula also handles a short segment whose endpoints round to the same column without counting the endpoint twice. Nonzero segments have total weight \(D=\max(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|)\); this is a projected length, not Euclidean arclength. For slope magnitude at most one, the latter is \(D\sqrt{1+m^2}\). Constant ink per major-axis step does not imply constant total ink per Euclidean length across all orientations.

    A coincident-endpoint call is explicitly a unit-weight point marker in both policies, rather than a zero-length stroke. Coordinates must be finite and safely inside the integer range before rounding. Endpoint reversal and exchanging axes preserve the corresponding raster, subject to floating-point rounding. The implementation evaluates the line directly at each centre to avoid accumulated slope-update drift.

    For \(P\) major-axis columns, there are at most \(2P\) contributions: \(O(P)\) time, \(O(1)\) auxiliary state and \(O(P)\) returned storage. The library's grayscale plotting helper additionally merges repeated coordinates in \(O(P\log P)\) time. This floating-point implementation does not reproduce every integer optimisation of Wu's original paper.

    Applying the rasteriser to monthly observations

    wu_polyline() keeps the frame's row order and scales each axis into the viewport interior, leaving a one-pixel margin. A constant coordinate maps to its axis centre. It neither averages observations nor sorts them. Each adjacent pair of monthly records becomes a segment with clipped endpoints. Contributions at shared coordinates add; the rendering helper saturates their sum at one before mapping it to grey. This is additive ink visualisation, not alpha-over compositing or a model of exact stroke joins.

    At the lower resolution several months share nearby horizontal pixel columns. Fractional shading softens the staircase but cannot encode each original measurement distinctly. The numerical input is preserved in the frame; the rendered image is a lossy representation. Larger viewports also change the relative horizontal and vertical scaling slightly because of the fixed margin, so total ink is not required to double exactly.

    Conclusion

    Two adjacent weights preserve a column's total ink and its ideal minor-axis first moment. Explicit endpoint policies distinguish the existing full-column variant from interval-clipped endpoint filtering. Correct conservation is per projected major-axis length, and neither brightness nor grey display values should be mistaken for exact geometric coverage. The airline series demonstrates practical polyline rendering and the limits of a small pixel grid: antialiasing improves the representation of a line without adding missing resolution to the observations.
