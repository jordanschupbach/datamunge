    Native vsim port: grasp skill and scheduler

    Native articulated robot physics: what limits the fruit sorter

    This report runs the fruit-sorting workcell on datamunge's in-house rigid-body engine (vsim, 960 Hz) with bounded effort servos and actual wrist RGB classification. MuJoCo is absent from the standard build. An earlier version of this page transferred the MuJoCo-trained dueling DQN scheduler unchanged and showed a 20-second clip with zero sorts. Here we ask why, test two remedies (a learned scheduler trained natively for longer, and a corrected grasp skill), and keep whichever wins on held-out streams. Finally, we retrain the wrist-camera classifier on native views, which removes wrong-bin sorts in closed-loop tests.

    ## Where the time goes

    The scheduler only chooses which fruit to intercept and when (wait, early or late on the leading fruit, early or late on the next one). The reach, close, lift and drop motions are fixed inverse-kinematics skills. Instrumenting 10 simulated minutes of native runs at 0.4 fruit/s showed:

    StageRate
    Arrivals that the arm attemptsabout 50%
    Attempts that end in a stable graspabout 50%
    Grasps that are sorted into the correct binabout 85%

    A successful pick occupies the arm for about 7.6 s and a failed one for about 4.6 s, so the arm tops out near 4 sorts per minute: 0.4 fruit/s is roughly three times its capacity, and most fruit must be missed whatever the scheduler does.

    Can a better scheduler pick the fruit it will grasp? We logged 726 attempts with the 25 observation features the scheduler sees and fitted logistic regression, gradient boosting and a small neural network to predict grasp success. The cross-validated AUC was only about 0.59 for all three. A neural reward or success model therefore has little signal to exploit: grasp outcome is decided mostly by contact details during closing, not by anything visible at decision time.

    ## Grasp skill: two opt-in corrections

    Tracing failed grasps in the intercept loop exposed two mechanical causes:

    • The tool follows the moving fruit with a steady 13-15 mm lag, just above the fixed 12 mm alignment threshold, so the jaws often never start closing.
    • The IK target is refitted every 8 physics ticks during closing. Once a finger touches, each refit jerks the forming grip; many failures held support for exactly 7 of the 24 required ticks.

    FruitStreamOptions now exposes both as opt-in settings (defaults unchanged, so other reports are unaffected): grasp_alignment_tolerance (0.005-0.05 m, default 0.012) and hold_grasp_target, which stops re-aiming once the finger reports contact. A sweep over 10 streams of 120 s showed that a 20 mm tolerance with the target held raised correct sorts from 88 to 106 (grasp rate 0.49 to 0.59); wider tolerances gave no further gain. A larger lead or a refit on every tick made results worse.

    ## Native scheduler training

    With the corrected skill we trained schedulers natively for 96 windows of about 120 s each (3.3 simulated hours per run): DQN, double DQN (two seeds), dueling double DQN and PPO, under the default reward and under a reshaped reward with higher miss and wrong-bin costs and a queue-waiting cost. Arrival rates were drawn from 0.1-0.45 fruit/s. The program below is kept for reproducibility but is not run when this page is rebuilt (eval="no"). Run it with train 0 A 96 7 build/fruit_native_train and similar for the other methods.

    Every checkpoint (every 16 windows, 36 in all) was scored on six validation streams of 90 s at 0.4 fruit/s, separate from the test streams used below. Training rewards stayed flat, and no checkpoint beat the simple greedy rule (intercept whenever any option is feasible, preferring the leading fruit late):

    Scheduler (validation, 6 x 90 s)AttemptsGraspedCorrect

    The learned schedulers wait more often than the greedy rule. Given the AUC result, waiting buys little: an attempt that is never made can never succeed. The best native checkpoint and its training log are kept in figures/ and enter the test comparison.

    ## Wrist classifier for the native camera

    The comparison above uses synthetic labels. The deployed system instead classifies each fruit from one wrist-camera crop taken just before the arm descends, and that single label chooses the bin. The MobileNet used so far was adapted to 256 px crops from MuJoCo runs (256 training crops, all at the inspection moment). The native port uses a 128 px camera, so its inputs never matched that training data. On held-out native streams the old model labelled only 72% of inspection crops correctly; apples were close to chance. In the video, where the model is rerun on every frame through the grasp, lift and drop, its label flipped between consecutive frames 27% of the time.

    We therefore collected training data from the deployed setup: 224 native streams of 120 s at 0.4 fruit/s, run by the greedy scheduler with the corrected grasp. Each crop comes from the 128 px camera, labelled by the true fruit class. The data hold the 4,188 inspection crops plus 4 fps views of the current target (or next belt fruit) through approach, grasp, lift and release, 70,186 crops in all. Streams are split by seed: 80% train, 10% validation (selects the epoch), 10% test. A MobileNet twice as wide as before (width 0.25) was trained from scratch on every training crop and its mirror image, with inspection crops weighted three times. The program below collects the data in 28 worker processes (about 2 h on 32 cores), trains for 25 epochs (under an hour; validation selects epoch 16) and writes figures/fruit_native_port_mobile.npz. Like the scheduler training, it is not run when this page is rebuilt (eval="no"). Run it from this directory with vision 28 8 120 25 build/fruit_native_vision.

    Held-out test streams (24 streams, 585 inspections, 9,597 trajectory frames). "Confident wrong" counts frames where the wrong class gets probability above 0.9; "frame flips" counts consecutive frames of one fruit whose label changes:

    A quarter of the old system's sorts went into the wrong bin; with the retrained classifier none did. Most remaining frame errors are on the belt during the approach, where the fruit is still small in the image. Mirror averaging at test time and the narrower width-0.125 network were also tried; neither gave a clear gain over this model. The training log records each epoch.

    ## Native video: greedy scheduler with the corrected grasp

    The final run combines the greedy rule with the corrected grasp skill for 60 seconds at 0.4 fruit/s, with the retrained MobileNet classifying each wrist-camera crop. The camera inset shows the current class and probability beside the foreground box; labels are drawn after inference and do not enter the model or controller.

    Native vsim, 960 Hz, 60 s: 19 arrivals, 6 correct, 1 wrong, 11 missed.

    The corrected grasp is the change that matters. With the same greedy scheduler, it raises correct sorts on the eight test streams from 49 to 68 (+39%) and stable grasps from 55 to 75, while attempts barely change. Both learned schedulers gain less from it: the MuJoCo-trained DQN rises only from 42 to 50, and the natively trained DQN reaches 40 because it attempts far fewer fruit (71 vs 118). Arrivals exceed arm capacity about threefold, so most fruit are still missed by every scheduler. Further gains must come from a faster pick cycle or a better grasp skill, not from scheduling.

    The 60-second native run above (seed 2026113001) has 19 arrivals: 6 correct sorts, 1 wrong bin and 11 misses, with the rest still on the belt. The old 20-second transfer clip had zero sorts. The wrong bin is most likely a perception error (MobileNet on rendered wrist RGB), which the comparison table removes by using synthetic labels. Maximum contact penetration is 1.6 mm; peak actuator effort fraction is 1.0. The measured run record retains the options, seed, timings and backend provenance. Separate analytic tests verify actual contact-driven lifting.
