    Naive Bayes: Classifying with Independent Evidence

    Abstract

    How should one classify an object from several measurements, some numeric and some categorical, when there are too few data to model how they vary together? Naive Bayes assumes that the measurements are independent within each class. Each class is then described by one Gaussian per numeric predictor and one smoothed frequency table per categorical predictor, and Bayes' theorem combines them. This report derives the model, its decision boundaries and an exact leave-one-out estimate of its accuracy. It works a five-row example by hand, including the zero-frequency problem that Laplace smoothing solves, and a one-dimensional example whose boundary is quadratic with two roots. Simulations confirm that naive Bayes approaches the Bayes error when its assumption holds. They also show the two ways it fails: copied predictors make its probabilities overconfident and move its threshold, and correlated predictors leave its error at the level of a single predictor while linear discriminant analysis follows the Bayes error. On the Palmer penguins, six predictors give 97.9% leave-one-out accuracy against 99.4% for linear discriminant analysis, which models the within-species correlation of the bill measurements. Every accuracy agrees exactly with scikit-learn.

    The problem

    We want to assign an object to one of \(K\) classes from a mixture of measurements: some numeric (a penguin's bill length, its body mass), some categorical (the island where it was seen, its sex). The optimal rule is known: predict the class with the largest posterior probability \(P(c\mid x)\propto\pi_c\,p(x\mid c)\). But it needs the joint density of all the measurements within each class. With \(p\) numeric predictors a full Gaussian needs \(p(p+3)/2\) parameters per class; a table over \(p\) categorical predictors with \(L\) levels each needs \(L^p-1\) probabilities per class. Few data sets can estimate those, and mixing the two kinds needs a joint model of both.

    Naive Bayes makes one strong assumption: given the class, the predictors are independent. Then the joint density is a product of one-dimensional pieces, each estimated on its own: \(2p\) parameters for Gaussian predictors and \(p(L-1)\) probabilities for categorical ones, per class. The questions are what this costs when the assumption is false, and why the method often classifies well even then.

    The method

    Bayes' theorem with independent predictors

    Let \(x=(x_1,\dots,x_p)\) and let \(\pi_c\) be the prior probability of class \(c\). Under conditional independence,

    \[P(c\mid x)=\frac{\pi_c\prod_{j=1}^pp_j(x_j\mid c)}{\sum_{c'}\pi_{c'}\prod_{j=1}^pp_j(x_j\mid c')},\qquad \hat c(x)=\arg\max_c\Big[\log\pi_c+\sum_{j=1}^p\log p_j(x_j\mid c)\Big].\]

    Each predictor adds its own log-likelihood term, so the evidence from different predictors simply adds up. posterior_terms(newdata, row) lists these terms for one row. The priors are the class frequencies unless NaiveBayesClassifierOptions::priors sets them.

    Numeric predictors: one Gaussian per class

    A numeric predictor gets the density \(N(\mu_{cj},\sigma^2_{cj})\) in class \(c\), with the maximum-likelihood estimates

    \[\hat\mu_{cj}=\frac1{n_c}\sum_{i:y_i=c}x_{ij},\qquad \hat\sigma^2_{cj}=\frac1{n_c}\sum_{i:y_i=c}(x_{ij}-\hat\mu_{cj})^2+\epsilon,\]

    where \(\epsilon\) is var_smoothing (\(10^{-9}\)) times the largest overall predictor variance, a floor that keeps a constant predictor from giving zero variance. Both conventions are scikit-learn's GaussianNB. gaussian_frame() returns the estimates.

    Categorical predictors: smoothed frequency tables

    A categorical predictor with levels \(1,\dots,L_j\) gets a probability table per class,

    \[\hat P(x_j=\ell\mid c)=\frac{n_{c\ell}+\alpha}{n_c+\alpha L_j},\]

    where \(n_{c\ell}\) counts the class-\(c\) rows at level \(\ell\) and \(\alpha\) is laplace_smoothing. With \(\alpha=0\) these are raw frequencies. A level never seen in a class then has probability zero, and a single such observation vetoes the class whatever the other predictors say. \(\alpha=1\) (Laplace's rule of succession) adds one imaginary observation per level. The predictor is one variable with \(L_j\) levels, not \(L_j\) separate 0/1 dummy columns, which would not be independent of each other. These are the conventions of scikit-learn's CategoricalNB. categorical_frame() returns the tables.

    The shape of the decision boundary

    For two classes and Gaussian predictors the log posterior odds are

    \[\log\frac{P(1\mid x)}{P(0\mid x)}=\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}+\sum_j\Big[\log\frac{\sigma_{0j}}{\sigma_{1j}} -\frac{(x_j-\mu_{1j})^2}{2\sigma^2_{1j}}+\frac{(x_j-\mu_{0j})^2}{2\sigma^2_{0j}}\Big],\]

    a sum of one quadratic per predictor, with no cross terms \(x_jx_k\). The boundary is a quadric whose axes are aligned with the predictors. If the two classes have equal variances for every predictor, the squares cancel and the log odds are linear in \(x\). The model is then the same as linear discriminant analysis with a diagonal covariance matrix. Linear discriminant analysis estimates the full covariance and can tilt its boundary to follow correlations between predictors; naive Bayes cannot.

    What independence costs

    Suppose predictor \(x_1\) is copied \(m\) times. The copies are perfectly dependent, but naive Bayes counts each as new evidence, so the log-likelihood ratio of \(x_1\) is multiplied by \(m\) while the prior term is not. The posterior probabilities become overconfident, and with unequal priors the decision threshold moves too. Correlated, non-identical predictors give a weaker version of the same double counting. The ranking of the classes can still be right when the probabilities are badly wrong: classification only needs the sign of the log odds, not its size. This is the explanation Domingos and Pazzani (1997) gave for naive Bayes' good accuracy under violated assumptions.

    Computation and exact leave-one-out

    Products of many densities underflow, so everything is computed in logs. The posterior is normalised with the log-sum-exp shift: subtract the largest log joint before exponentiating.

    training_accuracy() is the resubstitution accuracy. For an honest estimate, loo_accuracy() classifies every row by the model fitted without it. No refitting is needed: removing row \(i\) of class \(c\) changes only \(n_c\), the class-\(c\) means, sums of squares and counts, and the overall variances that set \(\epsilon\). Each is downdated in closed form, for example

    \[\hat\mu_{cj}^{(-i)}=\frac{n_c\hat\mu_{cj}-x_{ij}}{n_c-1},\qquad SS_{cj}^{(-i)}=SS_{cj}-\frac{n_c}{n_c-1}\big(x_{ij}-\hat\mu_{cj}\big)^2,\]

    so leave-one-out costs no more than resubstitution. The levels of a categorical predictor stay those of the full data.

    The first two tables reproduce the hand calculation: the standard deviations are \(\sqrt{2/3}=0.8165\) and 1, and the smoothed probabilities are 0.6, 0.4, 0.25 and 0.75. In the posterior terms, \(\log p(x\mid a)=-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\cdot\tfrac23)-\tfrac{(4-2)^2}{2\cdot2/3}=-3.7162\) and \(\log p(x\mid b)=-\tfrac12\log2\pi-\tfrac{(4-7)^2}2=-5.4189\). The numeric predictor favours a by 1.70 and the prior by 0.41; level v favours b by 0.63. That leaves a ahead by 1.48 in the log joint, a posterior of \(1/(1+e^{-1.48})=0.8145\).

    The last table shows the zero-frequency problem. The query \(x=4.5\) lies nearer class b in standardised distance, and on \(x\) alone the posterior of b is 0.72. But class b never had \(g=u\). With \(\alpha=0\) its probability is 0, and the posterior of b is exactly 0 however strongly \(x\) points to it. Any \(\alpha>0\) removes the veto. How much weight \(g\) keeps depends on \(\alpha\): at \(\alpha=0.01\) it still all but decides the answer (0.019), at \(\alpha=1\) the two predictors almost balance (0.52), and at \(\alpha=100\) both tables are close to \(\tfrac12\). Then \(g\) carries no information, and the posterior returns to 0.72 from \(x\) alone.

    A quadratic boundary in one dimension

    Two classes with equal priors, class a with \(\mu=0\), \(\sigma^2=1\) and class b with \(\mu=2\), \(\sigma^2=4\). They are fitted from the four points \(\{-1,1\}\) and \(\{0,4\}\), which have exactly these means and variances. The log odds of b against a are \(\log\tfrac12+\tfrac{x^2}2-\tfrac{(x-2)^2}8\). Setting them to zero and multiplying by 8 gives \(3x^2+4x-4-8\log2=0\), with roots

    \[x=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{16+12(4+8\log2)}}{6}=-2.5710\ \text{ and }\ 1.2377 .\]

    The wider class b wins on both sides: to the right, where its mean is, and far to the left, where the narrow class a's density falls off faster.

    Boundaries from the fitted model: -2.570917 and 1.237584; closed form: -2.570917 and 1.237584

    Bisection on the fitted model's posterior finds the boundaries at \(-2.570917\) and \(1.237584\). These agree with the closed form to all six printed digits, so the fitted variances and the posterior are exactly those of the derivation. The boundary is not a single threshold, as it would be with equal variances: on \((-2.57,1.24)\) the narrow class wins, and the wide class wins on both sides. The minimum of the posterior of b is about 0.2, so even at \(x=-0.7\), where a is most favoured, the model is not certain. Any Gaussian-based classifier with unequal variances has this behaviour; it is not caused by the independence assumption, which plays no part with one predictor.

    Checking the theory

    When the assumption holds, naive Bayes is the Bayes rule

    Two classes with priors 0.6 and 0.4 and two predictors that are independent within each class: \(x\mid a\sim N((0,0),\operatorname{diag}(1,1))\) and \(x\mid b\sim N((1.5,1),\operatorname{diag}(2.25,0.25))\). This is exactly the naive Bayes model, so as \(n\) grows the fitted rule should approach the Bayes rule. The Bayes rule's error is estimated on the same 20000 test points by classifying them with the true parameters. Training sets of \(n=10\) to 5000 are drawn (20 replicates each).

    Double counting. A second experiment copies one predictor. Classes \(N(0,1)\) and \(N(1.5,1)\), priors 0.7 and 0.3, and the predictor repeated \(m=1,2,4,8\) times. From the derivation above, the decision threshold moves from the Bayes value \(0.75+\log(7/3)/1.5=1.315\) to \(0.75+\log(7/3)/(1.5m)\).

    The Bayes rule, using the true parameters, misclassifies 17.73% of the test points. Naive Bayes trained on 10 points has a mean test error of 25.9%, with a standard deviation of 5.1 points between training sets. By \(n=1000\) it is 17.78%, and at \(n=5000\) it is 17.71%, slightly below the Bayes rule's error on the same finite test set. The spread between training sets shrinks roughly as \(1/n\), from 0.0082 at \(n=100\) to 0.0008 at \(n=1000\): near the optimum the excess error is quadratic in the estimation error. The leave-one-out error, computed from each training set alone, tracks the test error to within about a point from \(n=30\) on. At \(n=10\) it is pessimistic (31.5% against 25.9%), because removing one of ten rows changes the fit a good deal.

    The second table shows double counting. Accuracy barely moves as the copies are added (80.7% to 78.9%): the copies leave the order of the test points along \(x\) unchanged, and only the threshold moves. The probabilities change a lot. The mean confidence of the predicted class rises from 0.80 (close to the accuracy of 0.81, so well calibrated) to 0.97 with eight copies, while the accuracy stays near 0.79. The log loss more than triples, from 0.42 to 1.50. The fitted thresholds follow \(0.75+\log(7/3)/(1.5m)\) towards the midpoint of the means. Each \(m\) has its own training set, and the gaps of \(-0.022\) to \(+0.031\) are estimation error in the fitted means and variances. The thresholds move towards the minority class b: copying the evidence shrinks the share of the decision the prior controls.

    Correlated predictors: naive Bayes against LDA

    Two classes with equal priors and a shared covariance with correlation \(\rho\): \(x\mid a\sim N((0,0),\Sigma)\), \(x\mid b\sim N((1,0),\Sigma)\), \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\). Only \(x_1\) differs between the classes, but \(x_2\) is informative through the correlation: it tells how much of \(x_1\) is noise. The Bayes rule is linear with Mahalanobis separation \(\Delta^2=(1,0)\Sigma^{-1}(1,0)^\top=1/(1-\rho^2)\) and error \(\Phi(-\Delta/2)\). Naive Bayes sees two predictors whose class-conditional variances are equal and whose second mean does not differ, so asymptotically it uses \(x_1\) alone, with error \(\Phi(-\tfrac12)=0.3085\) whatever \(\rho\). LDA estimates \(\Sigma\) and should follow the Bayes error.

    Naive Bayes' error stays between 0.302 and 0.318 for every \(\rho\), around its limit of 0.3085. LDA's error follows the Bayes error to within 0.006 and falls to 0.054 at \(\rho=0.95\), a sixth of naive Bayes' error. In the left panel the naive Bayes boundary is close to the vertical line \(x_1=\tfrac12\), curved slightly by the small differences between the fitted class variances. The Bayes boundary \(x_1-0.8x_2=\tfrac12\) runs along the diagonal of the point clouds. Here the information is in a direction neither predictor measures alone: \(x_1-\rho x_2\) removes the part of \(x_1\)'s noise that \(x_2\) predicts. Naive Bayes cannot use this, because within each class it treats \(x_2\) as unrelated to \(x_1\). This failure costs accuracy, not only calibration, unlike double counting. It is also where naive Bayes differs most from LDA, which has exactly the right model here.

    Accuracy. The two bill measurements alone classify 93.3% of the penguins, with leave-one-out accuracy equal to resubstitution. The four numeric measurements give 97.1%. Island and sex alone give only 70.3%: island identifies Gentoo (Biscoe) and Chinstrap (Dream) penguins but cannot separate Adélies on Biscoe or Dream from them. All six predictors give 98.2% resubstitution and 97.9% leave-one-out accuracy. For every model, LDA's leave-one-out accuracy is higher: 95.9%, 98.5% and 99.4%. scikit-learn (GaussianNB plus CategoricalNB with \(\alpha=1\) for the mixed model, and LinearDiscriminantAnalysis with dummy-coded island and sex) agrees with every accuracy here, including the leave-one-out ones, to the last digit.

    Parameters. The Gaussian table shows which measurement separates which species. Bill length separates Adélie (mean 38.8 mm) from the other two (47.6 and 48.8 mm). Bill depth, flipper length and body mass separate Gentoo (15.0 mm, 217 mm, 5092 g) from the other two, which are close on all three. Chinstrap and Adélie are therefore told apart mainly by bill length and island. The island table shows Laplace smoothing at work. No Chinstrap was seen on Biscoe, but the estimated probability is \((0+1)/(68+3)=0.0141\), not zero. A Chinstrap later seen on Biscoe can still be classified correctly if its measurements are clear enough.

    Errors. The leave-one-out confusion matrix has seven errors, all between Adélie and Chinstrap; every Gentoo is classified correctly. The posterior terms show why row 38, a male Adélie from Dream, is called a Chinstrap. The prior favours Adélie by 0.76 and body mass by 0.20. Island favours Chinstrap by 0.95, because all 68 Chinstraps but only 55 of the 146 Adélies were on Dream. Bill length favours Chinstrap by 0.73, flipper length by 0.33 and bill depth by 0.05. The sum puts Chinstrap ahead by 1.10, a posterior of 0.75. With independent terms the evidence is easy to read: here a long-billed Adélie on the Chinstraps' island.

    Correlation. Within each species, bill length and depth are positively correlated (0.39 for Adélie, about 0.65 for the others): bigger penguins have bigger bills. Naive Bayes ignores this, so its boundaries in the right panel are curves with no tilt along the correlation. LDA's pooled covariance uses it. This is the situation of the correlated-predictor simulation, and the likely source of LDA's advantage: with bills only, LDA misclassifies 14 penguins and naive Bayes 23.

    Conclusion

    Naive Bayes replaces one joint model per class by a product of one-dimensional ones. It fits any mix of numeric and categorical predictors in one pass, and its posterior splits into one readable term per predictor. Its leave-one-out accuracy costs no more than its fit. When the predictors are independent within each class it is the Bayes rule with estimated parameters, and it converges to the Bayes error. Laplace smoothing is not optional for categorical predictors: without it a single unseen level vetoes a class.

    Its assumption fails in two ways. Redundant predictors are counted as independent evidence, which leaves accuracy nearly intact but makes the probabilities overconfident: with eight copies the log loss more than tripled. This is why naive Bayes often classifies well even when its probabilities are poor (Domingos and Pazzani, 1997; Hand and Yu, 2001). Correlation that carries information is a harder failure: naive Bayes cannot use the direction \(x_1-\rho x_2\), and its error stays at that of \(x_1\) alone while LDA's falls to the Bayes error. On the penguins the cost is small but visible, 97.9% against 99.4%, because bill length and depth are correlated within species.

    Use naive Bayes when there are many predictors relative to the data, when predictors are of mixed types, or when per-predictor evidence is wanted. Ng and Jordan (2002) showed that it then approaches its asymptotic error with far fewer samples than logistic regression. When the numeric predictors are correlated and the data are enough to estimate a covariance matrix, LDA or logistic regression will usually be more accurate. In either case, use the leave-one-out accuracy, not the resubstitution accuracy, and treat the probabilities with caution.
