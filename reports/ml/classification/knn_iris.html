    k-Nearest Neighbours: Classification and Regression by Proximity

    Abstract

    Given labelled training points, we want to predict the label of a new point without assuming any form for the relation between predictors and label. The \(k\)-nearest-neighbour rule lets the \(k\) closest training points vote (classification) or averages them (regression). Its behaviour is decided by the distance, the scaling of the predictors, the voting scheme and \(k\). We derive the rule, exact leave-one-out (LOO) evaluation with the standardisation recomputed for every left-out row, the Cover–Hart large-sample risk and the bias–variance decomposition of k-NN regression, and check each against datamunge's stats::KNNClassifier and stats::KNNRegressor. On six points the predictions for every metric, \(k\) and weighting match the hand calculation, including the tie rules. On two Gaussian classes the test error of 1-, 5- and 25-NN settles near the predicted limits 0.225, 0.180 and 0.163 (Bayes risk 0.159), and LOO error tracks test error. Adding 128 noise predictors raises the 15-NN error from 0.165 to 0.381. The simulated mean squared error of k-NN regression matches the exact bias-plus-variance prediction at every \(k\), with its minimum at \(k=18\). On iris, LOO accuracy reaches 96.7% from the petal measurements alone; on wine, standardising the 13 predictors raises LOO accuracy from 70–77% to 95–98%. The LOO accuracy paths agree exactly with scikit-learn refitted with each row left out (checked for \(k\le25\) on all four measurements).

    The problem

    We have \(n\) training points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb R^p\) with labels \(y_1,\dots,y_n\): a class out of a finite set, or a number. We want to predict the label of a new point \(x\) without assuming any form for the relation between \(x\) and \(y\): no linear boundary, no Gaussian classes, no polynomial trend. The only assumption is smoothness: points that are close in \(\mathbb R^p\) tend to have similar labels. The \(k\)-nearest-neighbour (k-NN) rule turns that assumption directly into a prediction. It finds the \(k\) training points closest to \(x\) and lets them vote (for a class) or averages them (for a number).

    The difficulty is that everything rests on "close". Distance depends on the units of each predictor, on predictors that carry no information, and on the dimension \(p\). The number of neighbours \(k\) trades a jagged, high-variance rule (\(k=1\)) against an over-smoothed, biased one (\(k\) large). There is also no training error to report, since with \(k=1\) every training point predicts itself perfectly.

    The method

    The rule

    Let \(d\) be a distance on \(\mathbb R^p\) and let \(N_k(x)\) be the indices of the \(k\) training points nearest to \(x\). For a class label, the vote share of class \(c\) is

    \[\hat p_c(x)=\frac{\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}w_i\,[y_i=c]}{\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}w_i},\qquad \hat y(x)=\arg\max_c\hat p_c(x),\]

    and for a numeric label the prediction is the weighted mean \(\hat f(x)=\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}w_iy_i\big/\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}w_i\). With uniform weights \(w_i=1\) every neighbour counts equally; with inverse-distance weights \(w_i=1/d(x,x_i)\) closer neighbours count more, and a training point at distance zero outvotes all others.

    Two kinds of tie need a rule. Neighbours at equal distance are ordered by their training-row position, and a tied vote goes to the first class in sorted order. Both conventions are scikit-learn's, so datamunge's predictions can be compared with it one for one.

    Distances

    DistanceMetric::Euclidean is \(\lVert a-b\rVert_2=\sqrt{\sum_j(a_j-b_j)^2}\) and DistanceMetric::Manhattan is \(\lVert a-b\rVert_1=\sum_j|a_j-b_j|\). Their unit balls are a disc and a diamond. Euclidean distance lets one large difference dominate, while Manhattan distance adds the coordinates' differences as they are, so the two can rank neighbours differently: from the origin, \((2,0)\) and \((1.2,1.2)\) are at Euclidean distances 2 and 1.70, but at Manhattan distances 2 and 2.4.

    Standardisation

    A distance adds up differences in different units. If one predictor is measured in grams and another in kilograms, the first one decides the ranking almost alone. The usual remedy is to rescale each predictor to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, \(z_{ij}=(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)/s_j\), with \(s_j\) the standard deviation of predictor \(j\) (divisor \(n\), as scikit-learn's StandardScaler). Then each predictor has the same spread. This is the default (standardize = true). datamunge computes each standardised difference as the raw difference times \(1/s_j\), so points that are exactly tied in the raw data stay exactly tied. It is a modelling choice, not a law: it gives a noise predictor the same weight as an informative one.

    Leave-one-out evaluation

    The training error of k-NN is meaningless: with \(k=1\) each training point is its own nearest neighbour and is classified correctly. KNNClassifier therefore reports leave-one-out (LOO) results: row \(i\) is classified by the other \(n-1\) rows. The standardisation is also recomputed without row \(i\), so the result equals refitting the model \(n\) times. Removing \(x_i\) changes only the scales (the means cancel in differences), and the centred sum of squares downdates as

    \[SS_{-i,j}=SS_j-\frac{n}{n-1}\,(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2,\qquad s_{-i,j}=\sqrt{SS_{-i,j}/(n-1)},\]

    so each standardised difference is multiplied by \(s_j/s_{-i,j}\) and no refit is needed. Because sorting the other rows by distance is the expensive part, loo_accuracy_path(K) sorts once per row and returns the LOO accuracy for every \(k\le K\) at the cost of a single evaluation (loo_rmse_path for the regressor).

    What k-NN estimates: the Cover–Hart limit

    For two classes write \(\eta(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)\). The best possible rule predicts class 1 when \(\eta(x)>\tfrac12\) and has the Bayes risk \(R^*=E[\min(\eta,1-\eta)]\). As \(n\to\infty\) with \(k\) fixed, the \(k\) neighbours of \(x\) crowd in on \(x\), so their labels become independent Bernoulli(\(\eta(x)\)) draws. With \(B\sim\text{Bin}(k,\eta)\) votes for class 1, the rule errs with conditional probability

    \[r_k(\eta)=\eta\,P(B<k/2)+(1-\eta)\,P(B>k/2)+\tfrac12P(B=k/2),\qquad R_k=E\big[r_k(\eta(X))\big],\]

    which stats::knn_asymptotic_risk(eta, k) evaluates. For \(k=1\), \(r_1=2\eta(1-\eta)\). Since \(\min(\eta,1-\eta)\le2\eta(1-\eta)\le2\min(\eta,1-\eta)\), the 1-NN rule is never worse than twice Bayes; the sharper bound of Cover and Hart (1967) is \(R^*\le R_1\le2R^*(1-R^*)\). As \(k\) grows, \(r_k(\eta)\to\min(\eta,1-\eta)\) and \(R_k\to R^*\). In practice \(k\) must grow more slowly than \(n\) so the neighbours stay local (\(k\to\infty\), \(k/n\to0\)); then k-NN is universally consistent (Stone, 1977).

    Regression: bias and variance

    For \(y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i\) with independent noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), and with the design points held fixed, the uniform k-NN estimate at \(x_0\) has

    \[E\,\hat f(x_0)=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x_0)}f(x_i),\qquad \operatorname{Var}\hat f(x_0)=\frac{\sigma^2}{k},\]

    because the neighbour set depends on the \(x\)'s only. The variance falls as \(1/k\) while the bias \(E\hat f(x_0)-f(x_0)\) grows as the neighbours spread out, so the mean squared error is U-shaped in \(k\) (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, 2009, section 7.3).

    The curse of dimensionality

    In \(p\) dimensions a neighbourhood holding a fixed fraction \(r\) of uniformly spread data must span a fraction \(r^{1/p}\) of each axis: 10% of the data in 10 dimensions needs 79% of each range. Neighbours stop being local. Distances also concentrate: for independent coordinates the distances from a query to all training points have mean of order \(\sqrt p\) and a spread of order 1, so the nearest and farthest points differ by a vanishing fraction. Every uninformative predictor adds noise to every distance.

The 1-NN regions are unions of Voronoi cells, so their boundaries are pieces of the perpendicular bisectors between an a point and a b point. The query sits just on the b side of the bisector of rows 0 and 1, consistent with \(1.118<1.5\).

    Regression on a line, and leave-one-out by hand

    Six points \(x=1,\dots,6\) with \(y=(2,4,3,7,6,9)\). With \(k=1\) the prediction is the \(y\) of the nearest \(x\), a step function that jumps halfway between neighbouring \(x\)'s. With \(k=3\) it is the mean of three. The leave-one-out prediction of each point uses only the other five. For \(k=1\), \(x=1\)'s nearest other point is \(x=2\), so it is predicted as 4. \(x=2\) is equidistant from \(x=1\) and \(x=3\), and row order picks \(x=1\), predicting 2. Continuing, the \(k=1\) leave-one-out predictions are \((4,2,4,3,7,6)\), with errors \((-2,2,-1,4,-1,3)\), so the leave-one-out RMSE is \(\sqrt{35/6}=2.415\).

    The \(k=1\) column is the hand calculation \((4,2,4,3,7,6)\), and its RMSE is \(\sqrt{35/6}=2.415\). The \(k=1\) fit is the data's own staircase. The \(k=3\) fit flattens at both ends, because a point near an end has all its neighbours on one side: at \(x\le2.5\) the three neighbours are \(x=1,2,3\), and the fit is their mean 3 although \(y\) is rising. This boundary bias is a general weakness of local averaging. On these six points the leave-one-out RMSE is lowest at \(k=2\).

    Checking the theory

    Approaching the Cover–Hart limit

    Two classes with equal priors, \(X\mid Y=0\sim N((0,0),I)\) and \(X\mid Y=1\sim N((2,0),I)\). Then \(\eta(x)=1/(1+e^{-(2x_1-2)})\) depends on \(x_1\) only, the Bayes rule is \(x_1>1\), and \(R^*=\Phi(-1)=0.1587\). The asymptotic risks \(R_k=E[r_k(\eta(X))]\) are one-dimensional integrals over the mixture density of \(x_1\), evaluated with knn_asymptotic_risk on a fine grid. Training sets of \(n=50\) to 6400 points are drawn (5 replicates each) and classified on 4000 fresh test points with \(k=1\), 5 and 25, from one fit per training set: kneighbors returns the 25 nearest training rows of every test point, and the votes of the first \(k\) give each rule. The leave-one-out accuracy of each training set is recorded alongside.

    The asymptotic risks lie between the Bayes risk 0.159 and the Cover–Hart bound 0.267: \(R_1=0.225\), \(R_5=0.180\), \(R_{25}=0.163\). The simulated test errors settle near them. The test set's own sampling error is about 0.006, so the 1-NN and 5-NN errors (0.227 and 0.188 at \(n=6400\)) are within about one standard error of their limits. The 25-NN error stays about 0.005 above its limit, because at these sample sizes 25 neighbours still reach some way from the query, where \(\eta\) differs. For this smooth problem the 1-NN error is already at its limit at \(n=50\). More data does not make 1-NN better; only a larger \(k\) moves the error towards the Bayes risk. The leave-one-out error of each training set tracks the test error. Its largest deviations, up to 0.02, are at \(n\le400\), where a training set of a few hundred points is itself noisy.

    Noise predictors. With standardisation, each noise predictor has the same weight as the informative one, so its random differences are added to every distance. With 8 noise predictors the error has risen from 0.165 to 0.207, with 128 to 0.381, not far from guessing. The relative contrast shows why: with the two informative predictors alone the farthest training point is 65 times as far as the nearest, with 128 noise predictors only 1.38 times. The nearest neighbour is then hardly nearer than any other point.

    Bias and variance. The exact prediction (squared bias computed from the neighbour sets, plus \(\sigma^2/k\)) and the average over 400 simulated data sets agree at every \(k\), within 3% at \(k=1\) and better elsewhere. The squared bias is negligible until the neighbourhood, \(k/100\) of the interval, reaches a noticeable part of a period of the sine. Beyond that it grows quickly: from 0.0004 at \(k=12\) to 0.030 at \(k=40\). The best \(k\) is 18, with error 0.0067, against 0.090 for \(k=1\).

    Iris and wine

    Iris. Setosa is separated from the other species by a wide margin, so the whole difficulty is the versicolor–virginica border, and the petal measurements carry nearly all the information. With the petals alone the LOO accuracy is 96–96.7% for almost every \(k\) up to 50. With all four measurements the sepals add noise to the distances: the accuracy is lower for small \(k\) and falls to 87.3% by \(k=46\), where the neighbourhoods are almost a third of a class. The best \(k\) for all four (96.7% at \(k=13\) to 18) only matches the petals. Choosing \(k\) by maximising the LOO accuracy is itself optimistic, since the maximum of 50 noisy estimates is biased upwards; a gap of one flower (0.67%) is noise. With \(k=15\) on the petals, all six LOO errors are on the versicolor–virginica border (two versicolor called virginica, four the other way round), as the region plot shows. The four-measurement path for \(k=1,\dots,25\) was checked against scikit-learn's KNeighborsClassifier refitted with each row left out (and its StandardScaler refitted too): all 25 values agree exactly.

    Wine. Without standardisation the distances are dominated by proline, whose standard deviation (314) is 22 times that of magnesium, the next largest, and over 90 times that of any other predictor. The 13-predictor accuracy (70–77%) is then close to that of proline alone (66–70%). Standardised, the same 13 predictors reach 95–98%. Here the fix is free because every predictor carries some information. With noise predictors, as above, standardisation would give the noise full weight.

    Conclusion

    The \(k\)-nearest-neighbour rule needs no model, only a distance, and the theory says what that buys. With \(k\) fixed, the large-sample error is a known function of \(P(Y=1\mid x)\): at most twice the Bayes risk for \(k=1\), and close to it for large \(k\). In regression the error is exactly the squared bias of averaging \(f\) over the neighbourhood plus \(\sigma^2/k\). Both were reproduced here by simulation. The cost is that "near" must mean something. Predictors must be on comparable scales: on wine, standardising lifted the accuracy from about 72% to 97%. Uninformative predictors must be left out: 128 noise predictors pushed the error from 0.165 to 0.381 as distances concentrated. Leave-one-out evaluation is the honest error estimate for k-NN, and datamunge computes it exactly, rescaling for every left-out row, and for all \(k\) at once from one sort per row. On iris it agrees with scikit-learn to the last flower.
