    The Iris Data: Summaries, Pictures and LDA

    Abstract

    A first look at Fisher's iris data with datamunge: we load the 150 flowers, summarize the four measurements overall and by species, draw a pairs plot and per-species boxplots, and then fit a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) with stats::LDA. Every table, number and figure below is the output of a small C++ program in this document, compiled against the installed library when the report is run.

    The data

    dm::datasets::iris() returns R's datasets::iris: 150 flowers, 50 each of setosa, versicolor and virginica, measured in centimetres on sepal length and width and petal length and width. Each block in this report is its own program, so each one loads the data again. Every block starts with the single line #include <dm/dm.hpp>, which brings in the whole library under the short namespace dm (and the standard headers a short program needs). The first block just prints the size of the frame and its first rows. DataFrame::to_md() writes a frame as a Markdown table, like knitr's kable(): numbers are right-aligned, and each numeric column gets the fewest decimals (at most three by default) that show all of its values exactly.

    The frame has 150 rows and 5 columns.

    Summary statistics

    The first table summarizes each measurement over all 150 flowers. It is a dplyr-style pipeline: pivot_longer() stacks the four measurement columns into one cm column with a Measurement column naming the source, group_by() and summarise() then reduce each measurement to its mean, standard deviation (with the \(n-1\) denominator), minimum, median and maximum, and to_md() ends the pipeline with the table. The dm::agg helpers name the aggregations as dplyr does, with the output column name second.

    The sepals of the three species overlap a good deal, but their petals do not: setosa's petals are about a quarter the length of virginica's, and versicolor sits between the two.

    Pictures

    A pairs plot (graphics::Splom, after R's pairs() and GGally's ggpairs()) shows every pair of measurements at once. group_by("Species") splits every panel by species. The diagonal shows each measurement's kernel density estimate. Above it, each panel shows the flowers with a smooth estimate of the mean (a local linear regression, like R's loess()) and a shaded 95% prediction band, so the band shows where a new flower of that species would fall. Below it, each panel shows contours of a two-dimensional kernel density estimate, drawn at the levels that enclose 25%, 50%, 75% and 95% of each species' flowers. The structure is plain: setosa is a cluster of its own in every panel that involves a petal measurement, while versicolor and virginica touch.

    Boxplots put the same information one measurement at a time. Petal length and width separate all three species with little overlap; sepal width is the only measurement in which setosa is the largest.

    Linear discriminant analysis

    LDA models each species as a multivariate normal distribution with its own mean \(\mu_k\) and a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) shared by all three. Under that model the most probable species for a flower \(x\) is the \(k\) that maximizes the linear discriminant function

    \[\delta_k(x) = x^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu_k - \tfrac12\,\mu_k^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu_k + \log \pi_k ,\]

    where \(\pi_k\) is the prior probability of species \(k\). The same model also gives Fisher's discriminant axes: the directions \(w\) that maximize the ratio of between-species to within-species variance. With three species there are at most two such axes, LD1 and LD2.

    Fitting the model

    stats::LDA takes a data frame and an R-style formula. to_md() reports the fit as Markdown tables with the same content as R's MASS::lda() printout: the priors, the group means, the coefficients of each discriminant axis, and the proportion of trace, which says how much of the separation each axis carries. (print_summary() gives the same report as plain text.)

    How well it classifies

    The confusion matrix compares each flower's species with the one the fitted model predicts for it (rows are the true species, columns the predicted ones). confusion_frame() returns it as a data frame, so it pipes into rename() and to_md() like any other.

    Accuracy measured on the flowers the model was fitted to is optimistic. A fairer estimate is five-fold cross-validation: each flower goes into one of five folds (every fifth row, so each fold holds ten flowers of each species), the model is fitted to the other four folds, and the held-out fold is predicted.

    The discriminant space

    Projecting every flower onto LD1 and LD2 turns the fitted model into a picture. dm::plot() draws an object's default picture and returns a graphics::Figure, which is titled, sized and saved the same way whatever is in it (save_svg(), save_png(), save_pdf(), or to_svg() for the text). For an LDA fit it draws that projection, coloured by the true species, over the model's decision regions: each shaded area is where the model predicts that species, and the dark lines are the boundaries between them. In discriminant space LDA assigns a flower to the species whose mean is nearest (after a small adjustment for the priors), so the boundaries are straight lines and each region is a convex polygon. With three species the two axes carry the whole model, so these regions are exactly the model's predictions.

    The three groups line up along LD1, with setosa far from the other two. The flowers on the wrong side of the versicolor / virginica boundary are the three misclassified in the confusion matrix.

    Boundaries on the petals

    Decision regions can also be drawn in the measurements themselves when the model uses two of them. The petals carry most of the separation, so a model with only petal length and width is a natural choice. For a model with two predictors dm::plot() gives two panels: the discriminant space as before, and the predictor space, shaded by the species the model predicts at each point. The boundaries are straight lines in both, because LDA's discriminant functions are linear in the measurements.

    The two petal measurements alone classify 96% of the training flowers correctly, against 98% for all four; the six errors all sit near the line between versicolor and virginica.

    Conclusion

    The four measurements separate setosa from the other species completely, and the petal measurements do most of the work. LDA confirms this: one discriminant axis carries almost all of the separation, the fitted model misclassifies only three versicolor and virginica flowers, and five-fold cross-validation gives the same 98% accuracy, so the fit is not an artefact of testing on the training data. A model on the petals alone does nearly as well, and its decision regions show why: two straight lines in the petal plane separate the species almost perfectly. The longer report in examples/mepml/lda_iris_analysis.mepml derives LDA in detail and predicts new flowers.
