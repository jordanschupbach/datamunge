    Winnow: A Sparse Rule Among Correlated Pixels

    Abstract

    Winnow learns Boolean threshold rules by multiplying active weights on mistakes. We use the 8 by 8 measurements in dm::datasets::digits() to construct a known two-pixel OR target, then hide those two attributes among other thresholded pixels. This is a sparse-concept experiment, not digit recognition. We derive a logarithmic mistake bound using target-weight promotions and a total-weight potential, inspect the actual shared training loop's trace, and evaluate a frozen rule on held-out images. Counterfactual Boolean probes show why perfect accuracy on observed images need not mean the learned rule equals the target on every Boolean vector.

    The data and the constructed target

    The dataset contains 1797 handwritten images with 64 integer intensities from 0 to 16 and a numeric digit label. Pixel indices are row-major on the 8 by 8 grid. We deliberately ignore the original digit response. Set a Boolean attribute to one when its intensity strictly exceeds eight, and define the target as the OR of pixel_27 and pixel_28. These adjacent central pixels are useful for a reproducible sparse-rule demonstration. Neither the cutoff nor the target is estimated from training outcomes.

    The method: mistake-driven multiplication

    With \(d\) Boolean features, initialise \(w_j=1\), choose threshold \(\theta=d\), and predict

    \[\widehat y=\mathbf1\!\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_jx_j>\theta\right\},\qquad w_j\leftarrow \begin{cases} 2w_j & x_j=1,\ \widehat y=0,\ y=1,\\ w_j/2 & x_j=1,\ \widehat y=1,\ y=0,\\ w_j & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\]

    A score exactly equal to the threshold predicts zero. The library allows other finite update factors greater than one and other nonnegative thresholds; the following bound is for factor two and threshold d. A complete pass with no online mistakes sets converged=true. That certifies classification of the supplied training rows by the unchanged weights, not agreement with the target on every possible input.

    A potential argument for logarithmic dependence on dimension

    Assume exact arithmetic and labels from a fixed monotone OR of \(k\) relevant Boolean attributes among \(d\ge1\). Let \(P\) and \(F\) be the numbers of false negatives and false positives in any presented sequence, including repeated passes.

    On a negative example all relevant attributes are zero, so relevant weights are never demoted. Every false negative activates at least one relevant attribute and promotes it. Just before that update its weight is at most the score, which is at most \(d\); afterwards it is at most \(2d\). Thus the sum of base-two logarithms of relevant weights starts at zero, increases by at least one per false negative, and cannot exceed \(k\log_2(2d)\):

    \[P\le k\log_2(2d).\]

    Now use total weight \(W=\sum_j w_j\), initially \(d\). A false negative increases it by the active score, at most \(d\). A false positive decreases it by half the active score, more than \(d/2\). Since total weight stays positive,

    \[0<W_{\mathrm{final}}\le d+dP-\frac d2F, \qquad F<2+2P, \qquad M=P+F<2+3k\log_2(2d).\]

    This simple bound is sufficient for the experiment, rather than a claim of the sharpest constants in Littlestone's analysis. It does not assume independence between attributes. Irrelevant attributes can be promoted on positive examples; their individual promotions and demotions need not cancel. The potential controls the aggregate behaviour instead.

    The actual training trace and a frozen held-out evaluation

    The DataFrame adapter validates Boolean values and calls the same update loop as winnow_train(). Its trace distinguishes online mistakes during a pass from accuracy of the final weights after that pass. These can differ: the last update in a pass may already fix every training row, while a later zero-mistake pass is needed to confirm convergence.

    Both relevant weights are 64, exactly equal to the threshold. A Boolean vector activating only one of them has target one but is predicted zero. Activating all other attributes while both relevant ones are zero gives score 104.875, above 64, producing a false positive for the specified OR. These probes are valid Boolean vectors but need not resemble real digit images. They demonstrate that perfect observed accuracy has not identified the target function on its entire Boolean domain. If such examples were presented for further learning, Winnow would update again; the reported probes deliberately freeze the model.

    Adding attributes while keeping the target fixed

    The next experiment orders the two relevant pixels first and then adds other pixel columns. Each fit uses the same training rows, row order, cutoff, target and update factor. Its threshold is the selected dimension. The test data remain the same reserved images. Changing the dimension changes the algorithm as specified; this is a controlled descriptive experiment, not a search for a best test-set configuration.

    This bound depends on Boolean inputs, a consistent monotone-disjunction target and the stated update/threshold choices. Arbitrary noisy labels or continuous feature values do not inherit it. Training visits every attribute of every presented row, so logarithmic mistake dependence does not mean logarithmic runtime: at most \(O(End)\) work for \(E\) passes over \(n\) rows and \(d\) attributes. The core stores \(O(d)\) weights; the DataFrame adapter additionally materialises \(O(nd)\) Boolean data and \(O(E)\) history. It rejects invalid shapes/values and reports floating-point overflow or positive-weight underflow instead of silently changing the mathematical rule.

    Conclusion

    A potential argument explains why a sparse realizable OR can be learned with only logarithmic dependence of online mistakes on total dimension. On thresholded image measurements, the shared Winnow loop needs fifteen updates and gets every observed held-out target right. Its learned weights still fail simple out-of-support Boolean probes. The experiment therefore separates mistake bounds during learning, accuracy on observed data, and identification of a function over its entire input domain.
