    Support Vector Machines: Margins, Kernels and Iris Classification

    Abstract

    Given labelled measurements, we want to classify a new observation even when the classes cannot be separated perfectly by a straight line. The difficulty is to choose a boundary that separates useful structure without following every training point. A support vector machine balances the width of a separating margin against the cost of violating it. We derive the soft-margin primal and dual, the kernel construction and the two-coordinate SMO update. Four collinear points expose the support coefficients; two points give an exact cost path; four corners show how a kernel separates XOR. A scale-and-cost sweep checks the computed coefficients and objectives against closed forms. Finally, iris provides a three-class example with training-only standardisation and a reproducible held-out evaluation: the default RBF model correctly labels 44 of 45 held-out flowers. Pairwise optimality residuals accompany the accuracy figures: reaching a sweep limit is not a proof that the numerical optimiser has converged.

    The method

    Separating labelled points with a margin

    Start with observations \((x_i,y_i)\), where \(x_i\in\mathbb R^p\) and \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\). A hyperplane has decision value \(f(x)=w^\top x+b\). Its perpendicular distance from \(x\) is \(|f(x)|/\lVert w\rVert\). For separable data we can rescale \(w,b\) so the nearest points have \(y_if(x_i)=1\). The planes \(f=+1\) and \(f=-1\) then have full separation \(2/\lVert w\rVert\). Maximising this width gives

    \[\min_{w,b}\frac12\lVert w\rVert^2, \qquad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\geq1.\]

    This fixes an arbitrary scale of the decision function; the sign alone would give the same classifications for any positive rescaling. In the code, the alphabetically first of each pair of classes is \(+1\); the second is \(-1\). A zero decision votes for the first.

    Soft margins and hinge loss

    Overlapping data cannot satisfy every hard constraint. Allow slack \(\xi_i\geq0\) and penalise its sum with \(C>0\). At the optimum slack is the hinge loss:

    \[\begin{aligned} P &= \min_{w,b,\xi}\left\{\frac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\right\}, & y_if(x_i)&\geq1-\xi_i,\quad\xi_i\geq0,\\ \xi_i&=\max(0,1-y_if(x_i)), & P(w,b)&=\frac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\max(0,1-y_if(x_i)). \end{aligned}\]

    A point with margin \(y_if(x_i)>1\) is outside the margin; one with margin between 0 and 1 is correctly classified but inside it; a negative margin is misclassified. Smaller \(C\) makes violations cheaper. Larger \(C\) does not guarantee better predictions on new observations. This implementation uses a sum of losses, so duplicating the training set changes the relative strength of the penalty even at fixed \(C\).

    Deriving the dual and the support vectors

    Introduce multipliers \(\alpha_i\geq0\) for the margin constraints and \(\mu_i\geq0\) for the non-negative slacks. The Lagrangian is

    \[L=\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i+ \sum_i\alpha_i(1-\xi_i-y_i(w^\top x_i+b))-\sum_i\mu_i\xi_i.\]

    Stationarity gives \(w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i\), \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0\) and \(C-\alpha_i-\mu_i=0\). Eliminating \(w,b,\xi\) leaves

    \[D=\max_\alpha\left\{\sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jK(x_i,x_j)\right\}, \qquad 0\leq\alpha_i\leq C,\quad \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0.\]

    For the linear model \(K(x,z)=x^\top z\). Complementary slackness gives the KKT rules:

    \[\alpha_i=0\Rightarrow y_if_i\geq1,\qquad 0<\alpha_i<C\Rightarrow y_if_i=1,\qquad \alpha_i=C\Rightarrow y_if_i\leq1.\]

    Only non-zero coefficients contribute to \(f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_iK(x_i,x)+b\). These observations are the support vectors. An observation exactly on the margin need not have a positive coefficient when the solution is degenerate. training_details() returns all pair rows, including zeros; num_support_vectors() counts distinct observations across pairs with coefficients above \(10^{-8}\), the threshold used for prediction. The current implementation retains the training design as well, so sparse predictions do not imply sparse training storage.

    For positive-semidefinite kernels the primal is convex and admits strictly feasible slacks, so strong duality holds. Any feasible primal and dual iterates obey \(P\geq P^*\geq D\). Consequently the gap \(P-D\) bounds objective suboptimality, provided the equality and box constraints are also satisfied numerically. diagnostics() reports the gap, the absolute equality residual and the largest violation of the KKT rules. For that residual it treats coefficients within \(\min(10^{-8},10^{-6}C)\) of a box face as on the face, avoiding misleading residuals from round-off coefficients. It does not clip a negative gap.

    Kernels: a linear separator in another space

    If \(K(x,z)=\phi(x)^\top\phi(z)\), the dual and decision need only the kernel; the feature map never has to be materialised. The available kernels are

    \[\begin{aligned} K_{\rm lin}(x,z)&=x^\top z, & K_{\rm poly}(x,z)&=(\gamma x^\top z+r)^d,\\ K_{\rm RBF}(x,z)&=\exp(-\gamma\lVert x-z\rVert^2), & K_{\rm sig}(x,z)&=\tanh(\gamma x^\top z+r). \end{aligned}\]

    Linear and RBF kernels are positive semidefinite; so is the polynomial kernel for positive \(\gamma\), non-negative \(r\) and positive integer \(d\). Arbitrary sigmoid parameters do not have that guarantee, so its gap is not generally an optimality certificate. We keep the sigmoid option in the iris comparison to show that sharing the same interface does not make every kernel choice a convex SVM.

    The defaults are RBF, \(C=1\), \(\gamma=1/p\), \(r=0\), \(d=3\). gamma <= 0 requests the automatic positive value; it does not fit a negative-gamma RBF. Numeric design columns are standardised by the training mean and sample standard deviation (divisor \(n-1\)); near-constant columns use scale 1. Scaling is disabled in the hand examples so all coordinates and margins are in their stated units. Automatic gamma is based on the number of design columns, not on the variance as in some other libraries' defaults.

    Two-coordinate SMO updates and implementation limits

    Changing just one \(\alpha_i\) would break \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0\). SMO changes two. With \(E_i=f_i-y_i\) and \(\eta=K_{ii}+K_{jj}-2K_{ij}>0\), the unconstrained step and equality-preserving partner are

    \[\alpha_j' = \operatorname{clip}_{[L,H]} \left(\alpha_j+\frac{y_j(E_i-E_j)}{\eta}\right),\qquad \alpha_i'=\alpha_i+y_iy_j(\alpha_j-\alpha_j').\]

    The interval comes from intersecting both boxes with the equality line:

    \[[L,H]=\begin{cases} [\max(0,\alpha_j-\alpha_i),\min(C,C+\alpha_j-\alpha_i)],&y_i\ne y_j,\\ [\max(0,\alpha_i+\alpha_j-C),\min(C,\alpha_i+\alpha_j)],&y_i=y_j. \end{cases}\]

    For two points \(-a,+a\), labels \(+1,-1\), starting at zero with a linear kernel, \(E_i=-1,E_j=+1,\eta=4a^2\); the first step is already the exact solution \(\alpha_i=\alpha_j=\min(C,1/(2a^2))\). The worked cost path below checks this.

    The library uses simplified SMO with deterministic random partner selection, not Platt's full working-set heuristics. It skips non-positive-curvature pairs and changes below \(10^{-7}\). Fitting stops after max_passes unchanged sweeps, or after \(\max(200,20\,\texttt{max_passes})\) total sweeps. Either can occur before all KKT conditions meet tolerance; residuals must be inspected. Identical mapped observations, indefinite kernels and large cost values deserve particular care.

    For a binary problem with \(m\) rows and \(p\) predictors, the stored Gram matrix costs \(O(m^2)\) space and \(O(m^2p)\) construction time. Each sweep costs \(O(m^2)\) since decisions are recomputed from all coefficients; there is no error cache. This is a small-data implementation. Prediction costs \(O(sp)\) per pair with \(s\) support vectors. For \(k\) classes there are \(k(k-1)/2\) models; they vote for the final class, with ties resolved in sorted class order. Votes are counts, not posterior probabilities.

    Simple examples

    Four points: which observations hold up the margin?

    Take points \((-2,0),(-1,0)\) in class left and \((1,0),(2,0)\) in class right. The largest-margin boundary is \(x=0\), with \(w=(-1,0)\), \(b=0\). Only the inner two points need coefficients: \(\alpha=(0,1/2,1/2,0)\). Thus \(\lVert w\rVert^2=1\), full margin width 2 and primal and dual objectives both \(1/2\). Moving either outer point farther away leaves that optimum unchanged.

    Two points: a soft margin need not cause a classification error

    With just \((-1,+1)\) and \((+1,-1)\) in one predictor, the equality constraint requires both coefficients to equal \(t\). The dual is \(D(t)=2t-2t^2\), whose unconstrained maximum is \(t=1/2\). At cost \(C=0.1\), clipping gives \(t=0.1\), \(w=-0.2,b=0\). Both signed decisions are 0.2 and both slacks 0.8. The primal is \(0.02+0.1(1.6)=0.18\), exactly the dual \(0.2-0.02\). Both points are still classified correctly. The formal planes \(f=\pm1\) now lie at \(x=\mp5\): they enclose the training points rather than touch them.

    Four corners: why XOR needs a feature map

    Put class same at \((-1,-1),(1,1)\), and class unlike at \((-1,1),(1,-1)\). A linear separator cannot classify all four: the midpoint of each pair is the origin, which would have to lie strictly on both sides. For the degree-two kernel \(K(x,z)=(x^\top z)^2\), an explicit map is \(\phi(x)=(x_1^2,\sqrt2x_1x_2,x_2^2)\). The coordinate \(x_1x_2\) alone separates them. A maximum-margin solution is \(f(x)=x_1x_2\), with \(\lVert w_\phi\rVert^2=1/2\) and objective \(1/4\).

    For RBF with \(\gamma=1\), symmetry permits equal coefficients. Each signed decision is \(t(1+e^{-8}-2e^{-4})\). Set it to one to obtain

    \[t=\frac1{(1-e^{-4})^2},\qquad \lVert w_\phi\rVert^2=4t,\qquad P=D=2t.\]

    The polynomial map sends opposite corners to identical features, so its individual coefficients are non-unique; only their sum within each class is fixed at \(1/4\). SMO can distribute that sum unevenly. This is why the table checks the norm and objective, not an invented unique coefficient of \(1/8\) at every corner.

    Checking the theory

    A closed form over scales and costs

    For points \(-a,+a\) the exact solution is

    \[\alpha^*=\min(C,1/(2a^2)),\quad w=-2a\alpha^*,\quad b=0,\quad \lVert w\rVert^2=4a^2(\alpha^*)^2,\quad y_if_i=2a^2\alpha^*.\]

    The cost at which the hard-margin solution becomes feasible is \(C=1/(2a^2)\). Scaling the coordinates by \(a\) moves this transition quadratically. We check four scales and 25 logarithmically spaced costs for each scale. Markers are fitted coefficients; lines are the analytic formula. This is a solver check, not a test of statistical generalisation.

    Conclusion

    The hand examples connect each coefficient to the geometry: zero coefficients at the outer points, non-zero support coefficients at the margin, and saturated coefficients inside it. The two-point solution shows exactly how changing units shifts the cost threshold. XOR makes the kernel construction tangible, and also exposes non-unique coefficients in a degenerate feature map. The computed objectives and residuals let us check those statements without treating the optimiser as a black box.

    Iris extends the same binary rule through three pairwise votes. Its training accuracy is a fit diagnostic; the held-out errors measure performance on different observations. The small holdout does not establish a universal kernel ranking, nor does a cost sweep by itself prove overfitting. Scaling, kernel parameters and the numerical stopping rule remain part of the model specification. This dense-Gram, simplified-SMO implementation suits small datasets; its residuals and memory cost matter before using it on larger problems.
