    Random Forests: Many Decorrelated Trees

    Abstract

    A classification tree is quick to grow and easy to read, but unstable: a slightly different training set gives a different tree, and that variance is most of its error. A random forest grows many deep trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the rows and with a random subset of the predictors tried at each split, and lets them vote. The rows a tree never saw estimate the forest's error without a test set. This report derives why voting helps: the variance of the vote share falls to \(\rho\sigma^2\), where \(\rho\) is the correlation between trees, and Breiman's bound ties the error to tree strength and correlation. It works a three-stump forest by hand and checks the theory by simulation. The bootstrap leaves out \(e^{-1}\) of the rows, as predicted, and the vote variance follows \(\rho\sigma^2+(1-\rho)\sigma^2/B\). One predictor per split lowers \(\rho\) from 0.19 to 0.15. Out-of-bag error is too high with few trees and matches the test error once the forest is large. On Breiman's twonorm benchmark the forest's test error rises from 3.5% to 7.5% as the predictors tried per split go from 1 to all 20, because the trees' correlation grows while their strength stays flat. Single trees have 23% error. On Fisher's iris data the forest's out-of-bag accuracy is 95.3%, against 95.0% on held-out flowers and 94.3% for a single tree. Impurity importance gives a pure-noise predictor 3% of the credit; permutation importance correctly gives it none. Along the way the tree's split search became a sort-and-sweep (identical trees, three times faster), and a misalignment bug that corrupted out-of-bag results whenever rows had missing values was fixed. Out-of-bag votes, margins, strength and correlation, and permutation importance were added. The forest agrees with scikit-learn's.

    The problem

    We have \(n\) labelled examples \((x_i,y_i)\), with \(x_i\in\mathbb R^p\) and \(y_i\) one of \(K\) classes, and want a rule that classifies new examples well. A classification tree is an attractive rule: it needs no scaling of the predictors, handles interactions and nonlinear boundaries, and is fast. Grown deep, it has little bias. But it is unstable: a small change in the training data changes the early splits, and with them the whole tree. Its predictions have a high variance, and that variance is most of its error.

    A random forest (Breiman, 2001) keeps the tree and attacks the variance. It grows many deep trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the rows and each restricted, at every split, to a random subset of the predictors, and lets them vote. Two questions follow. Why does the vote help, and by how much? And how good is the forest on new data, when every row was used to train it? The rows each tree did not see answer the second question without a held-out test set.

    The method

    Trees as building blocks

    A classification tree splits the predictor space into rectangles by recursive binary splits \(x_j\le c\). At a node holding rows with class shares \(\hat p_1,\dots,\hat p_K\), the Gini impurity is \(G=1-\sum_k\hat p_k^2\), and the split chosen is the one with the largest decrease

    \[\Delta(j,c)=G-\frac{n_L}{n}G_L-\frac{n_R}{n}G_R ,\]

    where \(n_L\), \(n_R\) and \(G_L\), \(G_R\) are the sizes and impurities of the two children. The candidate thresholds \(c\) are the midpoints between consecutive distinct values of \(x_j\). Sorting the node's rows by \(x_j\) once and moving them one at a time from the right child to the left gives every candidate's class counts in \(O(n\log n)\) per predictor. (Recounting all rows at each threshold, as DecisionTreeClassifier did before this report, costs \(O(n\cdot U)\) for \(U\) distinct values; the two give identical trees.) Growing continues until a node is pure, too small or at max_depth. A leaf predicts its majority class.

    Bagging and the variance of a vote

    Bagging (bootstrap aggregating; Breiman, 1996) fits \(B\) trees \(h_1,\dots,h_B\), each to a bootstrap sample: \(n\) rows drawn with replacement. The forest predicts by majority vote, using the vote shares

    \[Q_j(x)=\frac1B\sum_{t=1}^B\mathbf 1\{h_t(x)=j\},\qquad \hat f(x)=\arg\max_jQ_j(x).\]

    Fix a point \(x\) and a class \(j\), and let \(V_t=\mathbf 1\{h_t(x)=j\}\). Over the randomness of the training data and of the samples, the \(V_t\) have a common variance \(\sigma^2\), and any two of them have the same correlation \(\rho\), because the trees are grown by the same random recipe from the same data. Then

    \[\mathrm{Var}\,Q_j(x)=\frac1{B^2}\Big(B\sigma^2+B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\Big)=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 .\]

    Adding trees removes the second term, but not the first. As \(B\to\infty\) the variance falls to \(\rho\sigma^2\), set by how similar the trees are. Bagging leaves each tree's bias unchanged, so the whole gain is in variance, and it is limited by \(\rho\).

    Random predictor subsets

    The trees of a bagged ensemble are similar: if one predictor is the best splitter, nearly every bootstrap sample puts it at the root. A random forest lowers \(\rho\) by choosing, at every node, \(m\) of the \(p\) predictors at random and splitting only on those (max_features). Each tree becomes a little worse, because it sometimes has to split on a weaker predictor, but the trees are less alike. With \(m=p\) the forest is bagging; the usual default for classification is \(m=\lfloor\sqrt p\rfloor\).

    Out-of-bag estimation

    A bootstrap sample misses row \(i\) with probability

    \[q_n=\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)^n\;\longrightarrow\;e^{-1}=0.3679 .\]

    Row \(i\) is then out of bag for that tree. The number of trees for which a row is out of bag is binomial, \(\mathrm{Bin}(B,q_n)\), with mean \(Bq_n\); a row is never out of bag with probability \((1-q_n)^B\). The out-of-bag prediction of row \(i\) is the majority vote of the trees that did not train on it, and the out-of-bag error is the share of rows whose out-of-bag prediction is wrong. Each of these votes is a fair test: the trees involved never saw the row. Two biases remain. Each row is voted on by only about \(0.37B\) trees, so the out-of-bag error estimates the error of a forest of about \(0.37B\) trees and is too pessimistic when \(B\) is small. And those trees were fit to \(n-1\) rows' worth of data rather than \(n\), which matters little.

    Strength, correlation and Breiman's bound

    Let \(P_\Theta\) denote probability over the random tree recipe \(\Theta\) (sample and predictor subsets) with the training data fixed. The margin of an example is

    \[\mathrm{mr}(x,y)=P_\Theta\big(h(x,\Theta)=y\big)-\max_{j\ne y}P_\Theta\big(h(x,\Theta)=j\big),\]

    the limiting vote share of the true class minus that of the strongest wrong class. The infinite forest errs exactly when the margin is negative, so its error is \(\mathrm{PE}^*=P_{X,Y}(\mathrm{mr}<0)\). Call \(s=E_{X,Y}\,\mathrm{mr}\) the strength of the forest. If \(s>0\), Chebyshev's inequality gives \(\mathrm{PE}^*\le\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{mr})/s^2\).

    Breiman (2001) splits \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{mr})\) into tree strength and tree correlation. Write \(\hat\jmath(x,y)\) for the strongest wrong class and \(\mathrm{rmg}(\Theta,x,y)=\mathbf 1\{h(x,\Theta)=y\}-\mathbf 1\{h(x,\Theta)=\hat\jmath\}\) for the raw margin of one tree, so that \(\mathrm{mr}=E_\Theta\,\mathrm{rmg}\). For two independent recipes \(\Theta,\Theta'\), \(\mathrm{mr}^2=E_{\Theta,\Theta'}[\mathrm{rmg}(\Theta)\,\mathrm{rmg}(\Theta')]\), hence

    \[\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{mr})=E_{\Theta,\Theta'}\big[\rho(\Theta,\Theta')\,\mathrm{sd}(\Theta)\,\mathrm{sd}(\Theta')\big]=\bar\rho\,\big(E_\Theta\,\mathrm{sd}(\Theta)\big)^2 ,\]

    where \(\rho(\Theta,\Theta')\) is the correlation over \((X,Y)\) of the two trees' raw margins, \(\mathrm{sd}(\Theta)\) the standard deviation of one tree's raw margin, and \(\bar\rho\) their weighted mean correlation. Since \((E_\Theta\mathrm{sd})^2\le E_\Theta\mathrm{var}(\Theta)\le E_\Theta E_{X,Y}\mathrm{rmg}^2-s^2\le1-s^2\),

    \[\mathrm{PE}^*\le\frac{\bar\rho\,(1-s^2)}{s^2}.\]

    The bound is loose, but it names the trade-off that \(m\) controls: a smaller \(m\) lowers \(\bar\rho\) and, eventually, \(s\). Every quantity in it can be estimated out of bag: \(Q_j(x_i)\) from the out-of-bag votes gives the margins and \(s\), and for tree \(t\), with \(p_{1t}\) and \(p_{2t}\) the shares of its out-of-bag rows on which it votes for \(y_i\) and for \(\hat\jmath_i\), \(\mathrm{sd}_t=\sqrt{p_{1t}+p_{2t}-(p_{1t}-p_{2t})^2}\). strength_correlation() returns these estimates, oob_margins() the margins.

    Importance of a predictor

    The mean decrease in impurity (MDI) credits each split's weighted decrease \((n_{\text{node}}/n)\Delta\) to the predictor it uses, normalises the credits of each tree to sum to 1 and averages over trees (feature_importance()). It is computed on the rows each tree was trained on, so it rewards splits that only fit noise. A continuous predictor unrelated to the class offers \(n-1\) thresholds, and deep in a tree, among a handful of rows, some threshold on it always lowers the impurity.

    The permutation importance (Breiman, 2001) asks instead how much worse each tree does on its out-of-bag rows when the values of \(x_j\) are shuffled among them, breaking the link between \(x_j\) and the class while keeping its distribution (permutation_importance()). For a predictor independent of the class and the other predictors, shuffling changes nothing in distribution, so its expected importance is zero, whatever the trees did with it. The mean of the per-tree drops is the importance; their standard deviation over \(\sqrt B\) is its standard error.

    Computation

    With the sweep, growing a tree on \(n\) rows costs \(O(m\,n\log n)\) per level, and a prediction costs one root-to-leaf walk. The forest stores how many times each row was drawn into each tree and every tree's vote on every training row, so the out-of-bag votes, the accuracy after each tree (oob_accuracy_path()), the margins and the strength and correlation need no further tree evaluations. tree_votes(x) and vote_share(x) evaluate all trees at one point without building a data frame, which is what the decision-region plots and the simulations below use.

    Both importances rank the petal measurements first and sepal length third, but they disagree on the noise. The impurity importance gives noise 3.1% of the credit, about half of what it gives sepal width. That is the bias derived above: deep in a tree, a continuous predictor always offers some threshold that separates a few remaining rows. coin offers a single threshold and gets only 0.4%. The permutation importance of both is zero within its standard error (\(0.0001\pm0.0009\) and \(0.0000\pm0.0005\)), while all four measurements are many standard errors above zero. Shuffling petal length costs a tree 29 points of out-of-bag accuracy.

    The extra predictors also change the impurity shares of the real ones. Sepal length goes from 0.098 without them to 0.127 with them. With six predictors and \(m=2\), a node is offered neither petal measurement with probability \(\binom42/\binom62=0.4\), against \(1/\binom42=0.17\) with four, so the trees split on sepal measurements and noise more often. The out-of-bag accuracy is unchanged at 95.33%.

    Conclusion

    A random forest reduces the variance of deep trees by averaging them, and the averaging is only as good as the trees are different. The variance of the vote falls as \(\rho\sigma^2+(1-\rho)\sigma^2/B\). Trees beyond a few dozen change little, and the floor \(\rho\sigma^2\) is set by the correlation between trees. Random predictor subsets lower that correlation: by a fifth on the noisy disc, and enough on twonorm to halve the error relative to bagging, while the trees' strength hardly changed. The bootstrap gives a test set for free. About \(e^{-1}\) of the rows are out of bag for each tree, and the out-of-bag error matched the test error once the forest had about 50 trees, both on simulated data and on iris (95.1% against 95.0%).

    The method has limits that the examples showed. Deep trees fit label noise, and averaging removes only part of it: on the noisy disc the forest's error stayed at 0.21, against a Bayes error of 0.15. A forest also gives up the readability of a single tree. Its importances need care: the impurity importance credited a pure-noise predictor with 3% of the splits, and only the permutation importance, measured out of bag, put it at zero. On a problem as easy as iris, the forest beats a single tree by less than one point.
