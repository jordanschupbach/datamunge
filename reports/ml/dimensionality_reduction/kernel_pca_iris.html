    Kernel PCA: Centring, Nonlinear Features and New Observations

    Abstract

    A curved collection of observations may need more than a rotation of its original measurement axes to reveal useful low-dimensional structure. We want coordinates that retain variation in a chosen nonlinear feature space, together with evidence of what those coordinates discard. Kernel PCA obtains them from a centred matrix of pairwise kernel similarities. Three-point linear and Gaussian calculations and a four-point quadratic feature map make centring, normalization and rank explicit. Eight symmetric ring points show that radial information need not be the leading component at every scale. Iris then illustrates kernel sensitivity without choosing parameters from species labels, followed by projection of untouched observations using a training-only centre.

    The method

    Choose the geometry before seeking principal axes

    For raw numeric predictors \(x_i\in\mathbb R^p\), a positive semidefinite kernel represents inner products \(k(x_i,x_j)=\langle\phi(x_i),\phi(x_j)\rangle\). A linear kernel keeps the original geometry; a nonlinear kernel changes it before performing ordinary principal-component analysis in feature space. Here the choices are

    \[k_L(x,z)=x^Tz,\qquad k_R(x,z)=\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2),\qquad k_P(x,z)=(\gamma x^Tz+c)^d.\]

    The RBF parameter gamma is an inverse squared length scale, with \(\ell=(2\gamma)^{-1/2}\). Polynomial d is a positive integer, gamma is positive and c is nonnegative, giving a PSD kernel on arbitrary real vectors. Fractional powers and negative offsets are rejected rather than treated as valid feature maps by default. All active parameters and retained predictors must be finite; unrepresentable kernel values are rejected.

    KernelPCA uses predictors in their supplied units. It does not standardize them or select gamma. Uniformly rescaling x by a requires rescaling RBF gamma by \(1/a^2\) to preserve similarities. Changing only one column's units changes the metric in a way one scalar gamma cannot generally undo. In a train/test analysis, any external scaling must also be learned on training rows and frozen for the test rows.

    Centre features, not the original measurements alone

    Let \(K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)\), \(\mu=n^{-1}\sum_i\phi(x_i)\) and \(H=I-11^T/n\). The Gram matrix of centred feature vectors is

    \[K_c=HKH,\qquad (K_c)_{ij}=K_{ij}-\bar K_{i\cdot}-\bar K_{\cdot j}+\bar K_{\cdot\cdot}.\]

    Thus \(K_c1=0\), its rank is at most n−1, and an unchanged constant kernel has zero centred rank. Centering raw x suffices for a linear kernel, but does not generally centre nonlinear features. In the quadratic example below, the mean of x is zero while the mean of its squared feature is 2.5.

    For numerical stability the linear implementation forms centred input dot products. For RBF it centres expm1(-gamma*distance_squared) before restoring any common constant: the added all-ones matrix vanishes under H. This retains a tiny-gamma signal even when every entry in a double-precision raw kernel has rounded to one. kernel_matrix() exposes rounded raw K; centered_kernel_matrix() exposes the more accurately computed centred geometry.

    Normalize eigenvectors to obtain scores and feature-space variance

    For \(K_cv_j=\lambda_jv_j\), \(\|v_j\|=1\) and positive lambda, a unit feature axis and its training scores are

    \[u_j=\frac{\sum_i v_{ij}(\phi(x_i)-\mu)}{\sqrt{\lambda_j}},\qquad Z_{ij}=\langle\phi(x_i)-\mu,u_j\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda_j}v_{ij}.\]

    Consequently \(Z^TZ=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_q)\). The Gram eigenvalue is the sum of squared scores; its sample covariance variance is \(\lambda_j/(n-1)\). These are feature-space variances, with no general interpretation as centimetres squared of the original measurements. eigenvalues() preserves the retained Gram-scale API; all_eigenvalues() and spectrum() expose the full signed spectrum and the sample-variance normalization.

    Signs are arbitrary; the implementation fixes a largest-magnitude eigenvector entry positive. Repeated eigenvalues permit rotations of an entire eigenspace, so comparisons should use projectors or score Gram matrices, not signed columns. Truncating through a tied block can make even the selected subspace ambiguous. Numerical rank uses \(64n\epsilon\lambda_{\max}\); requests beyond it throw rather than promoting roundoff into a component. Materially negative eigenvalues also throw.

    For a PSD kernel, q leading axes minimize total training feature-space projection loss:

    \[\sum_i\|\phi(x_i)-\mu-\Pi_q(\phi(x_i)-\mu)\|^2 =\operatorname{tr}(K_c)-\sum_{j\le q}\lambda_j =\sum_{j>q}\lambda_j.\]

    spectrum() reports the tail energy and cumulative share within the chosen kernel. The numerical summary uses resolved eigenvalues above tolerance. A larger retained share across different kernels is not proof of a better map: those kernels define different feature spaces. Low-dimensional coordinates need not preserve original distances, classes or manifold geodesics. Nor is a feature-space projection an inverse transform back to raw measurements; this implementation does not solve a pre-image problem.

    Project new observations using the training mean

    For a new row x, centre its cross-kernel row against the frozen training frame:

    \[k_c(x,i)=k(x,x_i)-\frac1n\sum_jk(x,x_j)-\bar K_{\cdot i}+\bar K_{\cdot\cdot}, \qquad z_j(x)=\sum_i k_c(x,i)\frac{v_{ij}}{\sqrt{\lambda_j}}.\]

    The query-row mean is over training landmarks, not other test observations. Adding queries to a batch cannot change an existing row's coordinates. transform() uses this rule; refitting on the combined frame, or separately centring a test kernel matrix, would answer different questions.

    projection_details() additionally evaluates \(\|\phi(x)-\mu\|^2=k(x,x)-2\overline{k(x,X)}+\bar K_{\cdot\cdot}\) and subtracts the sum of retained squared scores. The remainder is a feature-space projection loss, not a predictive test error or calibrated anomaly score. Missing or nonfinite query predictors retain their row positions with NaN scores and a status; training nulls are omitted jointly and original positions remain in kept_row_indices().

    Dense fitting costs \(O(n^2p+n^3)\) time and \(O(n^2+np)\) storage. Projecting m new rows costs \(O(mn(p+q))\). The eigensolver is scaled before decomposition, so a small kernel scale is not confused with zero by an absolute stopping threshold.

    Simple examples

    Three linear points expose centring and out-of-sample projection

    For x = (0,1,2), the raw Gram matrix has rows (0,0,0), (0,1,2), (0,2,4). The training mean is one, so the centred matrix has rows (1,0,−1), (0,0,0), (−1,0,1). Its only positive eigenvalue is two, scores are (−1,0,1) up to one common sign, and sample variance is one. A new x=3 projects to magnitude two on the same axis; it is not centred by itself to zero.

    Four quadratic points make the nonlinear features explicit

    Take x = (−2,−1,1,2) and \(k(x,z)=(xz+1)^2\). An explicit feature map is \(\phi(x)=(x^2,\sqrt2x,1)\), because its dot product expands to that kernel. The centred features are \((x^2-2.5,\sqrt2x,0)\). Their two nonzero columns are orthogonal over these four points, with squared norms 9 and 20. Therefore the leading score is \(\sqrt2x\), the second is \(x^2-2.5\), and Gram eigenvalues are 20 and 9, up to score signs. The second component joins opposite signs of x with the same magnitude. This demonstrates a nonlinear feature without claiming that principal components identify a class boundary.

    Three Gaussian points give two exact eigenvalues

    For x = (−1,0,1), write \(a=e^{-\gamma}\), \(b=e^{-4\gamma}\). The uncentred kernel rows are (1,a,b), (a,1,a), (b,a,1). Reflection symmetry produces the odd unit vector \((-1,0,1)/\sqrt2\) and the even unit vector \((1,-2,1)/\sqrt6\), both orthogonal to the constant direction. Their centred eigenvalues are \(\lambda_{odd}=1-b\) and \(\lambda_{even}=1-4a/3+b/3\). At gamma one, both are positive; retaining both recovers all centred feature energy of these three observations, without recovering a general raw-space inverse map.

    Checking the theory

    Small gamma approaches scaled linear PCA; large gamma loses a preferred axis

    Expanding the RBF kernel at small positive gamma gives \(K_{ij}=1-\gamma\|x_i-x_j\|^2+O(\gamma^2)\), hence \(K_c=2\gamma X_cX_c^T+O(\gamma^2)\). Leading eigenspaces approach linear PCA when separated by an eigengap; score magnitudes shrink as \(\sqrt{2\gamma}\). They do not approach raw linear scores without that rescaling. In the three-point example, the odd eigenvalue is approximately 4 gamma and the even one approximately \(2\gamma^2\); the tiny second mode eventually falls below numerical rank tolerance and is not requested in the sweep.

    For distinct points, gamma tending to infinity gives \(K\to I\) and \(K_c\to H\), with n−1 equal eigenvalues of one. It is H, not I, after centring. There is then no preferred ordering of directions, and a two-dimensional truncation captures only \(2/(n-1)\) of feature energy. Duplicates change this limit to blocks of identical kernel rows. A displayed direction at a nearly repeated eigenvalue should not be given a stable scientific interpretation.

    Iris measurements

    Raw-centimetre kernels and the linear-PCA identity

    dm::datasets::iris() contains 150 complete observations, four numeric measurements in centimetres and 50 flowers in each of three species. All four measurements enter the kernel; Species is reserved for descriptive colouring and the later split. No fitted classifier or species-based gamma selection is part of this analysis.

    The linear kernel must reproduce centred, unscaled PCA: its Gram eigenvalues are n−1 times sample covariance eigenvalues, and scores agree up to signs or repeated-subspace rotations. The program compares two-component score Gram matrices, avoiding sign choices. The RBF gamma values 0.13 and 10 are prespecified illustrations in the raw-centimetre metric, not claims of optimality. The polynomial kernel's explicit example already showed what its nonlinear map does; a label-ranked comparison of arbitrary kernels would add a supervised choice to an unsupervised illustration.

    Gamma sensitivity without using species to select it

    We scan declared values in the same raw-unit metric and report retained feature energy, mean projection loss and original-neighbourhood trustworthiness/continuity at k=10. Trustworthiness penalizes new false neighbours and continuity penalizes torn original neighbours. Both use original raw-feature distances as their reference, which is one chosen criterion, not every possible downstream purpose. This scan describes sensitivity; it does not select a winner or evaluate a supervised task. Feature-energy shares compare how much each kernel's own geometry compresses, rather than a common amount of original measurement variance.

    Fit on 120 flowers, project the remaining 30

    Use the prespecified train_test_split(iris,.2,42,"Species"), with ten test flowers per species, and fix gamma at 0.13 before this split analysis. Only the 120 training flowers define feature centring, the eigensystem and dual coefficients. Species is used to balance the split, not as a predictor; the frozen transform is unsupervised conditional on that sampling design. Projecting the 30 held-out rows does not refit any of these quantities.

    Training and test projection losses share the same feature-space definition. Their comparison checks how this fixed subspace represents other flowers on one small split; it is not regression accuracy, classification accuracy or a claim that a different kernel would generalize better. The historical data also do not define a representative population sampling experiment.

    Conclusion

    Kernel PCA performs ordinary variance maximization after choosing and centring a feature geometry. The exact examples distinguish raw and centred kernels, Gram eigenvalues and sample variances, and nonlinear structure from the ordering of principal axes. The RBF limits explain both scaled linear behaviour at small gamma and the loss of preferred axes at large gamma. Iris confirms the linear-PCA identity and exposes the dependence of compression and neighbourhood preservation on kernel scale. New observations use training centring and normalized dual coefficients; feature-space projection losses remain representation diagnostics rather than original-unit prediction errors.
