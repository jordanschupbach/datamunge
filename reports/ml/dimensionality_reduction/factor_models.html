    Factor Analysis: Common Variance, Rotation and Boundary Fits

    Abstract

    Several measured variables can move together because they share fewer unobserved sources of variation. We want to represent their covariance by common loadings and variable-specific variance, while checking whether that split is statistically and numerically plausible. An exact three-indicator example exposes the algebra and principal-axis iterations; a change of measurement units and an orthogonal rotation show what is invariant. Repeated synthetic samples check recovery of known common covariances and compare a rank-matched PCA. Finally, Iris demonstrates why a stopped iteration, a nonnegative parameter count and a small residual do not by themselves establish an admissible or scientifically interpretable factor model. Its one-factor fit stops with a negative raw uniqueness for petal length.

    The method

    Model common covariance and separate unique variance

    For a centred p-vector, k common factors and independent variable-specific errors,

    \[x=\Lambda f+\epsilon,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(f)=I_k,\quad \operatorname{Cov}(f,\epsilon)=0,\quad \operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon)=\Psi, \qquad \Sigma=\Lambda\Lambda^T+\Psi.\]

    Here Lambda is p by k and Psi is diagonal with nonnegative entries. No normality assumption is needed for this covariance identity. The generated studies below use Gaussian factors/errors, but the native estimator is principal-axis factoring (PAF), not Gaussian maximum likelihood. R's factanal and scikit-learn's FactorAnalysis therefore solve different estimation problems and need not return the same fit.

    For one factor, off-diagonal covariance is lambda_i*lambda_j. A unique error contributes only to its own diagonal. This restriction is what distinguishes the common-factor model from an unrestricted low-rank approximation.

    The concept diagram below is drawn by dm::graphics::Diagram. Its editable C++ source is the complete block below; regeneration uses the same datamunge toolchain as the numerical examples. Exports consume the saved local SVG.

    Communalities, uniquenesses and improper fits

    The common variance for feature j is the squared norm of its loading row:

    \[h_j^2=\sum_{c=1}^k\lambda_{jc}^2,\qquad \psi_j=C_{jj}-h_j^2,\qquad \text{common fraction}_j=\frac{h_j^2}{C_{jj}}.\]

    With standardized features, C is the sample correlation matrix and C_jj is one up to roundoff. Without standardization, communalities and uniquenesses are variances in each feature's units; dividing by C_jj produces a dimensionless fraction. A negative raw uniqueness is an improper solution, often called an ultra-Heywood case; a zero uniqueness is a boundary case. We use “Heywood violation” below for materially negative raw uniqueness. Such a violation can occur even when the parameter count is favourable.

    The existing uniquenesses() API clips negative raw values to zero for compatibility. The new variance_table() shows both values, actual squared loading norms and a Heywood flag. Consequently, reproduced_covariance() can exceed an observed diagonal: clipping is not a repair of an improper loading solution.

    Derive principal-axis updates and their stopping rule

    Let C be the covariance of the centred, optionally standardized complete rows. Sample standard deviations and C use the n−1 divisor. Initialize with squared multiple correlations, then repeatedly replace the diagonal by estimated common variances:

    \[h_{j,0}^2=C_{jj}\operatorname{clip}\!\left(1- \frac{1}{(C_{\mathrm{floor}}^{-1})_{jj}C_{jj}},0,1\right),\quad C_t=C-\operatorname{diag}(C)+\operatorname{diag}(h_t^2),\quad L_{t+1}=V_k\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sqrt{\max(\lambda_1,0)},\ldots, \sqrt{\max(\lambda_k,0)}\right).\]

    The next diagonal is clip(row_squared_norm(L),0,C_jj). The native implementation stops when the largest absolute change in these *clipped* variances is less than tol, or when max_iter is exhausted. The published loadings remain the actual eigenvectors times square roots, so their squared row norms need not equal the clipped diagonal. iteration_history() records every completed update and its residual/violation count; converged() reports only this extraction stopping condition, not validity or a global minimum. PAF is neither maximum likelihood nor a guaranteed minimum-residual optimizer.

    The inverse used for SMCs and scores floors eigenvalues at 1e-8 times the largest observed feature variance. This is a regularized inverse, not a Moore–Penrose inverse; near singularity, scores and initialization depend on that convention. Symmetric matrices are normalized before the Jacobi eigensolve. Forming covariance still loses relative accuracy in poorly conditioned directions. Each PAF step costs O(p cubed) for bounded Jacobi sweeps, after O(n*p squared) preprocessing; stored history adds O(iterations*p) space. Only finite complete rows with positive feature variances are accepted. Missing-feature rows are dropped together, and their original indices are retained for score/label alignment. Feature names must be numeric and distinct.

    Rotation changes the coordinates, not the common covariance

    For orthogonal Q,

    \[(\Lambda Q)(\Lambda Q)^T=\Lambda\Lambda^T,\qquad V(Q)=\sum_c\left[\sum_j q_{jc}^4- \frac{1}{p}\left(\sum_jq_{jc}^2\right)^2\right],\qquad q_{jc}=\frac{(\Lambda Q)_{jc}}{\sqrt{h_j^2}}.\]

    The implementation uses pairwise varimax sweeps, then orders columns by squared loading sums and makes each largest-magnitude loading positive. These are reporting conventions, not identification of physical causes. The rotation objective is computed on row-normalized loadings and the row norms are restored afterwards. A higher value can give a simpler loading pattern without improving covariance fit. Varimax retains orthogonal factors; correlated generating factors require oblique coordinates to retain their original simple pattern. The common covariance is a safer recovery target than individual loading entries when signs, permutations or rotations can change.

    Scores and parameter counting answer different questions

    For preprocessed rows Z, regression scores use

    \[\widehat F=ZC_{\mathrm{floor}}^{-1}\Lambda,\qquad \mathrm{df}=\frac{p(p+1)}2- \left(pk+p-\frac{k(k-1)}2\right) =\frac{(p-k)^2-p-k}{2}.\]

    The scores are linear estimates of unobserved factors. They are generally shrunken, not unit variance, and not guaranteed sample-orthogonal. Orthogonal rotation of a fit rotates its scores too. Nonnegative df is a necessary parameter-count check for generic identification, not a sufficient uniqueness or admissibility test. Negative df means the unrestricted model has more effective parameters than available covariance entries; the API allows such fits for inspection. These df are not a computed chi-square test, and this PAF report supplies no likelihood-based p-value.

    Simple examples

    Three indicators whose correlations are exactly one half

    Take eight centred rows, with a=sqrt(7)/2. The three columns below have four nonzero entries of magnitude a, so each sample variance is 4*a squared/7=1. Each pair has two matching nonzero entries, giving covariance 2*a squared/7=.5. For a one-factor model, lambda_1 squared=C_12*C_13/C_23=.5, and similarly for the other two indicators. The positive-sign loadings are therefore sqrt(.5), and each uniqueness is .5.

    The inverse correlation diagonal is 1.5, so every initial communality is 1/3. Symmetry reduces the PAF update to a scalar recurrence:

    \[h_{t+1}^2=\frac{h_t^2+1}{3},\qquad h_0^2=\frac13,\qquad h_t^2=\frac12-\frac{1}{6\cdot3^t}.\]

    The first update is 4/9. The table exposes that intermediate state rather than just the final answer. For this example the regression weights are sqrt(.5)/2 on each indicator, so a row's score is sqrt(.5)*(a+b+c)/2. Its sample score variance is .75, even though the latent factor's model variance is one.

    Change units without changing the common fraction

    Multiply all three observations by two. With standardization, the fitted correlation is unchanged. With covariance fitting, the entire covariance is four times larger: loadings double, and common/unique variances quadruple. The initial communalities must therefore be 4/3, not 1/3, and the first updated value is 16/9. The final common variance is two out of four, giving the same common fraction .5. This uniform change of units checks the covariance-mode initialization's dimensional consistency; arbitrary per-feature rescaling can also change eigenvalue flooring and iterative behaviour.

    Rotate a two-factor pattern and inspect the invariant fit

    Start with six unit-variance indicators, with loadings .85,.80,.75 on factor A and .70,.65,.60 on factor B, and unique variances one minus each squared loading. For this example let B=.6*A+.8*G, where A and G are independent standard Gaussians. The effective loadings on independent coordinates (A,G) are (.85,0),(.80,0),(.75,0), (.42,.56),(.39,.52),(.36,.48). This is a valid correlated-generating-factor example expressed in orthogonal coordinates, not an implemented oblique rotation.

    Checking the theory

    Recover common covariance across constructed samples

    Return to independent generating factors, with the two three-indicator blocks above. Their population common covariance is L*L transpose and each population feature variance is one. We vary n=100,500,2000 and use three declared seeds at each size. Recovery is measured against this known common covariance using off-diagonal RMS, so signs, permutations and orthogonal rotations cannot change the answer.

    For each exact same sample, also fit standardized PCA and reconstruct its covariance from the first two eigenpairs. Compare both models against the *sample* off-diagonal correlations. PCA optimizes a different criterion involving total variance, including the diagonal; a smaller PAF off-diagonal residual on these generated samples is not a general superiority claim or an optimum guarantee for PAF. Population recovery and sample fit are separate columns. Individual replications need not improve monotonically with n, and nine fits do not establish a sampling distribution or a confidence interval.

    Iris: inspect a strained factor model

    dm::datasets::iris() contains 150 rows, four numeric measurements in centimetres and species labels. Use all four numeric columns, standardize them, and keep species out of fitting. The first rows make the schema explicit. Iris is a familiar counterexample for fit diagnostics, rather than evidence that species are generated by these factors.

    For p=4, k=1 has parameter-count df=2, while k=2 has df=−1. Fit both with the same stopping controls and show what the implementation actually does. Report convergence, raw uniqueness and observed-versus-reproduced off-diagonal correlations together. A positive df does not prevent an improper fit, and a smaller covariance residual does not rescue an overparameterized or nonconverged one. Inspecting diagonal mismatch is necessary because the compatibility uniqueness output is clamped.

    The one-factor Iris iteration stops after 22 steps, yet petal length has communality 1.0324913 and raw uniqueness −.0324913. Its reproduced diagonal exceeds one by exactly that violation. The two-factor run reaches 1000 steps without convergence; despite its smaller off-diagonal RMS (.0054321 versus .0780916), it has negative parameter-count df and still has a negative raw uniqueness. These outcomes do not identify a single cause for the misfit and do not support treating the one-factor fit as a clean solution.

    Conclusion

    The exact three-indicator example connects the covariance model to loadings, unique variances, a scalar PAF recurrence and shrunken regression scores. A uniform change of units preserves common fractions, while orthogonal rotation preserves common covariance rather than individual loading entries. Constructed samples make rotation-invariant recovery checks possible and distinguish sample covariance fit from PCA's total-variance reconstruction objective.

    Iris illustrates why no single diagnostic certifies a factor model. Numerical stopping, parameter counting, nonnegative unique variance and residual fit are separate checks. Clamping a negative uniqueness does not make its loading solution admissible. This implementation supplies exploratory PAF with orthogonal varimax; it does not establish causal factors, perform oblique rotation, compute ML fit tests, or quantify sampling uncertainty from one fitted dataset.
