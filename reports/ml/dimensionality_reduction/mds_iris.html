    Multidimensional Scaling: Recovering Geometry from Distances

    Abstract

    Given only the pairwise dissimilarities between objects, we want coordinates for a map that shows their relationships. A two-dimensional map generally cannot preserve every distance, and some dissimilarities cannot be represented exactly in any Euclidean dimension. Classical multidimensional scaling recovers a centred inner-product matrix from squared distances and retains its leading positive eigenpairs. We derive this construction and its least-squares guarantee, which concerns inner products rather than distances. A four-point rectangle makes every centring step checkable; a four-cycle shows why retaining all positive inertia can still leave distance error. An analytic spectrum sweep checks where Euclidean representability fails. Finally, iris illustrates dimensionality choice, Euclidean versus Manhattan dissimilarities, and the exact equivalence with centred, unscaled PCA. On iris, two Euclidean axes retain 97.77% of positive inertia, while the relative distance error is 4.18%; the Manhattan map has a larger 5.79% distance error and a substantial negative spectrum. Labels are used only to colour the resulting map.

    The method

    What is observed, and what must be reconstructed?

    Let \(D=(d_{ij})\) be a symmetric, non-negative dissimilarity matrix on \(n\) objects, with \(d_{ii}=0\). We seek points \(y_i\in\mathbb R^k\) whose Euclidean distances \(\hat d_{ij}=\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert\) resemble \(d_{ij}\). The input may come from measured distances without feature vectors, or from a metric on observed features. MDS::from_distances(D,k) accepts the former; the DataFrame constructor builds Euclidean or Manhattan distances from selected numeric columns. Neither path standardises features or makes a non-Euclidean matrix Euclidean by adding a constant.

    The problem has an unavoidable ambiguity: translation, rotation and reflection preserve all distances. Repeated eigenvalues also permit arbitrary choices of basis within their subspace. Comparing coordinates one by one is therefore usually the wrong verification. Compare pair distances or the centred Gram matrix instead.

    Distances determine a centred Gram matrix

    If rows of \(X\) are Euclidean coordinates, write \(G=XX^\top\) and \(q_i=\lVert x_i\rVert^2\). Squared distances satisfy

    \[D^{(2)}=q\mathbf1^\top+\mathbf1q^\top-2XX^\top, \qquad J=I-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top.\]

    Since \(J\mathbf1=0\), multiplying on both sides by \(J\) eliminates the norm terms:

    \[B=-\tfrac12JD^{(2)}J=(JX)(JX)^\top, \qquad B_{ij}=-\tfrac12\left(d_{ij}^2-\overline{d^2}_{i\cdot} -\overline{d^2}_{\cdot j}+\overline{d^2}_{\cdot\cdot}\right).\]

    gram_matrix() exposes this intermediate matrix, not the truncated reconstruction. Its row sums vanish up to round-off. For Euclidean input it is positive semidefinite (PSD): \(z^\top Bz=\lVert X^\top Jz\rVert^2\geq0\). Conversely, if the double-centred \(B\) is PSD, factor it as \(YY^\top\); the corresponding squared distances reproduce the original hollow symmetric \(D^{(2)}\). To see the last claim, expand \(B_{ii}+B_{jj}-2B_{ij}\): all centring means cancel, leaving \(d_{ij}^2\). Thus non-negative Gram eigenvalues characterise exact Euclidean representability.

    The eigenvalue solution and what it optimises

    Let \(B=V\Lambda V^\top\), with eigenvalues in descending order. Classical MDS takes

    \[Y_k=V_{:,1:k}\operatorname{diag}\left(\sqrt{\max(\lambda_1,0)},\ldots, \sqrt{\max(\lambda_k,0)}\right).\]

    Its guarantee is about strain, the squared Frobenius error in the Gram matrix:

    \[\min_{Y\in\mathbb R^{n\times k}}\lVert B-YY^\top\rVert_F^2 =\sum_{j>k,\lambda_j>0}\lambda_j^2+\sum_{\lambda_j<0}\lambda_j^2.\]

    A proof sketch separates eigenvalues from orientation. The eigenvalues of \(YY^\top\) are non-negative with at most \(k\) non-zero entries. Expanding the squared error gives \(\operatorname{tr}(B^2)+\operatorname{tr}(A^2)-2\operatorname{tr}(BA)\), with \(A=YY^\top\). Aligning their largest eigenvalues maximises the cross term; minimising each remaining \((\lambda_j-a_j)^2\), subject to \(a_j\geq0\), retains the largest positive \(\lambda_j\) and sets the others to zero. Negative eigenvalues therefore leave an irreducible strain floor in every Euclidean embedding.

    This is not a guarantee of minimum squared error in distances. Two useful distance summaries, reported by dimension_diagnostics(), are

    \[\mathrm{RMSE}_k=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i<j}(\hat d_{ij,k}-d_{ij})^2}{\binom n2}}, \qquad E_k=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i<j}(\hat d_{ij,k}-d_{ij})^2}{\sum_{i<j}d_{ij}^2}}.\]

    The second column is named relative_distance_error: its denominator uses the input matrix, explicitly fixing the normalisation. We do not label it Kruskal Stress-1, whose normalisation can use fitted distances. Both summaries are computed over unordered pairs. pair_distances() exposes every residual; plot_shepard() plots \(\hat d\) against \(d\). On Euclidean input, truncated distances never exceed the originals and increase with added axes, so these distance errors decrease. That monotonicity need not hold for indefinite input.

    Two goodness-of-fit fractions

    The original goodness_of_fit() reports retained positive inertia. For comparison with R's cmdscale, the diagnostics expose both of its spectral fractions:

    \[G_+(k)=\frac{\sum_{j\leq k}\max(\lambda_j,0)}{\sum_j\max(\lambda_j,0)}, \qquad G_{|\cdot|}(k)=\frac{\sum_{j\leq k}\max(\lambda_j,0)}{\sum_j|\lambda_j|}.\]

    For PSD input these coincide. Otherwise \(G_+=1\) says only that every positive direction was retained: it ignores the negative spectrum. Neither fraction is a percentage of distances reproduced, nor a percentage of the strain removed. relative_strain instead reports the square root of the residual eigenvalue-square sum divided by \(\sum_j\lambda_j^2\). The all-zero matrix has no inertia; the implementation reports zero for its fit fractions and zero for its distortion measures. Tiny eigenvalues of either sign can be round-off; spectrum() flags a negative value only below \(-10^{-10}\max_j|\lambda_j|\).

    The exact relationship to PCA

    For Euclidean feature distances, centre \(X\) and write its singular value decomposition as \(X_c=U\Sigma V^\top\). Then

    \[B=X_cX_c^\top=U\Sigma^2U^\top,\qquad \frac{X_c^\top X_c}{n-1}=V\frac{\Sigma^2}{n-1}V^\top, \qquad Y_k=U_k\Sigma_k=X_cV_k.\]

    Thus the non-zero MDS eigenvalues are exactly \((n-1)\) times the PCA covariance eigenvalues; their positive-inertia proportions agree. The coordinates agree up to orthogonal choices of axes. This is an equality, not an approximate correlation claim. The comparison must use PCAOptions{true,false}: PCA centres and scales by default, whereas the feature MDS constructor uses the original units. Scaling changes the question.

    Computation and limits

    Feature distances cost \(O(n^2p)\) work, double-centring costs \(O(n^2)\), and dense storage costs \(O(n^2)\). The implementation uses cyclic Jacobi eigendecomposition, with \(O(n^3)\) work per sweep and a relative off-diagonal tolerance of \(10^{-12}\) (up to 100 sweeps). All \(n\) eigenvalues are retained for diagnostics. Prefix diagnostics reuse the fitted axes in \(O(n^2k)\) work, rather than refitting \(k\) models. This is classical scaling, not iterative stress minimisation or non-metric MDS. There is no out-of-sample transform in this API. Coordinates have the input distance units; plots use their canvas aspect ratio and should not be used as rulers.

    Simple examples

    A 4 by 3 rectangle: recover the hidden coordinates

    Take \(A=(0,0),B=(4,0),C=(4,3),D=(0,3)\). The squared-distance matrix has rows \((0,16,25,9)\), \((16,0,9,25)\), \((25,9,0,16)\), \((9,25,16,0)\). Every row mean and the grand mean equal \(50/4=12.5\). Consequently \(B_{AA}=-\tfrac12(0-12.5)=6.25\) and \(B_{AB}=-\tfrac12(16-12.5)=-1.75\). The entire intermediate matrix is printed below. Its two non-zero eigenvalues are 16 and 9, so two dimensions recover all distances. The axes may change sign, but the reconstructed rectangle is the same geometry.

    A four-cycle: perfect positive inertia is not perfect distance preservation

    Consider four objects around a cycle. Adjacent dissimilarities are 1; opposite ones are 2. These are the Manhattan distances between the corners of a unit square. They obey the triangle inequality but cannot be Euclidean distances with the same values. The full Gram spectrum is \((2,2,0,-1)\). Classical scaling drops the negative direction and returns a square of side \(\sqrt2\) and diagonal 2. It retains every positive eigenvalue, yet makes all four sides too long. The exact diagnostics are

    \[G_+(2)=1,\quad G_{|\cdot|}(2)=4/5,\quad \lVert B-Y_2Y_2^\top\rVert_F^2=1,\quad E_2=\frac{\sqrt2-1}{\sqrt3}.\]

    There is an explicit configuration with smaller distance loss. A square of side \(s\) has squared distance loss \(4(s-1)^2+2(\sqrt2s-2)^2\). Differentiating gives \(16s-8-8\sqrt2=0\), so \(s=(1+\sqrt2)/2\). Its loss is half that of the classical solution. This candidate suffices to disprove distance-loss optimality; we do not claim it is the optimum over all possible four-point configurations.

    Checking the theory

    Sweep through the boundary of Euclidean representability

    Generalise the preceding cycle so opposite dissimilarities are \(t\), while adjacent ones remain 1. For \(1\leq t\leq2\) these still form a metric. Its centred Gram eigenvectors can be chosen proportional to \((1,0,-1,0)\), \((0,1,0,-1)\), \((1,-1,1,-1)\) and \((1,1,1,1)\). Their eigenvalues are

    \[\{t^2/2,t^2/2,1-t^2/2,0\},\qquad \text{irreducible squared strain}=\left[\max(0,t^2/2-1)\right]^2.\]

    The third direction is positive below \(\sqrt2\), zero at \(\sqrt2\), and negative above it. Three dimensions exactly represent the first regime; two suffice at the boundary; no dimension can exactly represent the final regime. We check the complete spectrum and strain floor at 101 values of \(t\). Errors compare sorted spectra, so eigenvector non-uniqueness cannot disguise a failure.

    Euclidean and Manhattan maps answer different questions

    Both maps below use raw centimetres. Euclidean distance is the straight-line length in four-dimensional measurement space; Manhattan distance adds absolute differences in each measurement. Squaring the latter does not generally produce a PSD centred Gram matrix. Consequently its positive-inertia fraction alone cannot describe map quality.

    For these two-axis fits the Euclidean relative distance error is 4.18%, while the Manhattan value is 5.79%. The Manhattan positive-inertia fraction is 89.46%, but its absolute-inertia fraction is only 81.30%; 92 eigenvalues are negative beyond the stated tolerance. That difference explains why its positive-inertia fraction needs a separate residual check.

    A map can reveal separation without being a classifier. No supervised accuracy is estimated here, and these in-sample distance diagnostics do not measure prediction on new flowers. The colours interpret an unsupervised result; they did not guide its construction.

    How much is lost by keeping two dimensions?

    Four dimensions suffice for the Euclidean distances of four-feature rows. Fitting four axes once lets the library inspect every prefix. The positive-inertia proportion and normalised strain differ because one weights eigenvalues and the other their squares. Actual distance errors add a third, directly interpretable check.

    Check PCA through invariants, not a correlation of chosen axes

    The final check explicitly disables PCA scaling. The printed eigenvalue table tests \(\lambda_j^{\mathrm{MDS}}=149\lambda_j^{\mathrm{PCA}}\). The full reconstructed Gram matrices should agree as well; this check tolerates sign flips and rotations in repeated-eigenvalue subspaces. A default standardised PCA fit would instead analyse a different set of distances.

    The displayed distances, full spectra, reconstructed Gram matrices and goodness-of-fit fractions were checked independently with R 4.5.2 cmdscale (without an additive correction), and the Euclidean geometry with prcomp(center=TRUE,scale.=FALSE). NumPy independently reconstructs the matrices and all prefix residuals from exported inputs and coordinates. All 101 cycle spectra also match the analytic eigenvalues. These checks compare geometric invariants; no coordinate sign is chosen merely to make two plots look alike.

    Conclusion

    The rectangle exposes every step between distances and coordinates: squaring, removing means, diagonalising a Gram matrix and dropping an axis. Its retained inertia, strain and distance loss are three different quantities with different values. The cycle then shows why a perfect positive-inertia fraction can coexist with unavoidable distortion, and supplies a concrete configuration with smaller distance loss than the classical solution. The spectrum sweep locates exactly where the obstruction appears.

    Iris behaves as the Euclidean derivation predicts: its classical map is unscaled PCA in another orientation, and its four-feature distances require at most four axes. Manhattan input poses a different geometry, so negative eigenvalues and direct residuals must be examined alongside retained positive inertia. A useful two-dimensional picture is evidence about the chosen dissimilarities; its appearance alone is neither a proof of exact distance recovery nor a validation of a predictive model.
