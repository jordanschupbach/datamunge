    PCA on Iris: Axes, Metric and Reconstruction

    Abstract

    Several correlated measurements can describe nearly the same variation. We want a smaller set of coordinates, with a measurable account of what their projection loses. PCA chooses orthogonal directions that retain the most squared variation in a specified feature metric. Four points make its axes and reconstruction error exact; a unit-change example separates standardized error from original-unit error, and a wide two-row example separates returned axes from numerical rank. Iris then illustrates score plots, loadings, scaling choices and the eigenvalue-tail identity. A final split projects new flowers through training-only means, scales and axes. Species colours are descriptive, with no classification accuracy or biological importance inferred from variance shares.

    The method

    Choose the origin and the feature metric

    Let X have n rows and p numeric columns. Fit preprocessing once:

    \[Z_{ij}=\frac{X_{ij}-\mu_j}{s_j},\qquad C=\frac{Z^TZ}{n-1}.\]

    With centring enabled, mu is the training mean. Without scaling, s=1 and C is the sample covariance matrix. With scaling, s is the sample standard deviation, using n−1, and C is the correlation matrix for nonconstant columns. Exactly constant centred columns retain scale one and zero energy. They contribute no variance; they do not magically become unit-variance features. The library defaults to centring and scaling. R prcomp defaults to centring without scaling and rejects constant scaled columns.

    If centring is disabled, mu=0 and C is a second-moment matrix rather than covariance. Scaling then uses sqrt(sum(x_j squared)/(n−1)), not a centred standard deviation. We use centred fits throughout the numerical examples. Changing a column's units changes raw covariance PCA; standardizing each nonconstant column cancels positive unit rescalings. Neither convention is universally more meaningful: scaling selects a weighted squared-error objective, and small-variance noise can be amplified too.

    Maximize variance along a unit axis

    A unit vector v has projected sample variance v-transpose C v. A Lagrange multiplier for v-transpose v=1 yields Cv=lambda v. Maximizing that Rayleigh quotient chooses the largest eigenvalue; repeating in the orthogonal complement selects the next axes. For orthonormal columns V and descending eigenvalues lambda,

    \[CV=V\Lambda,\qquad V^TV=I,\qquad T=ZV,\qquad \frac{T^TT}{n-1}=\Lambda.\]

    The rotation columns are often called loadings, but they are not automatically variable-component correlations. For standardized nonconstant variables those correlations are V_jc*sqrt(lambda_c), when lambda_c is positive. Scores are not whitened: their variances remain lambda. Eigenvector signs are arbitrary; a repeated eigenvalue allows any orthonormal basis of its eigenspace. Compare projectors or reconstructed geometry when checking implementations. Cutting through a tied eigenspace can give different equally optimal projections, without identifying a preferred direction.

    The native covariance eigensolver returns a full p-axis basis, including zero-energy columns. It does not return only min(n−1,p) nontrivial components. Centred data have rank at most min(n−1,p); numerical_rank() uses a scale-relative Gram-eigenvalue threshold, 64*max(n,p)*machine_epsilon*lambda_1. All-zero energy has rank zero and zero variance shares. Numerical rank is distinct from a scientific choice of k.

    Derive projection loss from discarded variance

    For the first k axes, the fitted affine reconstruction is

    \[\widehat Z_k=ZV_kV_k^T,\qquad \widehat X_{k,ij}=\mu_j+s_j\widehat Z_{k,ij},\qquad \|Z-\widehat Z_k\|_F^2=(n-1)\sum_{c>k}\lambda_c.\]

    Write Z in its orthonormal axis basis. Each omitted score column contributes its squared norm, (n−1)*lambda_c, with no cross terms. Retaining the largest such norms minimizes rank-k squared error: the Eckart–Young result. Divide by n*p and take a square root for RMS per entry, rather than dividing by n−1 again. spectrum() reports the training tail; reconstruction_curve(data) measures residuals against the actual input coordinates. All p axes reconstruct within floating-point error, not literal exact-zero arithmetic. k=0 returns the fitted origin.

    This optimality concerns preprocessed error. In original units it weights squared coordinate residuals by s_j squared; a scaled fit need not be the best raw-unit rank-k fit. A new query row can even have increasing original-unit error when one more axis is retained, while its preprocessed error decreases. The tail identity uses the training sample; query errors need not equal that same training eigenvalue tail.

    Preprocessing and numerical limits are part of the fitted model

    Fitting drops rows with a null selected feature and records their original indices. Retained predictors must be finite. Transform/reconstruction require every query row to be complete and finite, and use only the frozen training schema, means, scales and axes. An individual row projects identically alone or as part of a batch. Missing selected columns or mismatched types are rejected; extra columns are ignored.

    Covariance is formed with extended-precision accumulation and normalized before the Jacobi eigensolve, avoiding the eigensolver norm's overflow/underflow for representable very large/small covariance scales. Forming a Gram matrix still squares the condition number; near-null directions do not have SVD-level relative accuracy. Small negative computed eigenvalues are clipped to zero. For n observations and p features the main work is O(np2+p3) with bounded Jacobi sweeps and storage O(np+p2); projecting m new rows or sweeping their reconstruction prefixes costs O(mp2).

    Simple examples

    Four points give an exact eighty/twenty split

    Use (-3,-1), (-1,-3), (1,3), (3,1), with no scaling. Their mean is zero, covariance diagonal 20/3 and off-diagonal four. The axes lie along (1,1) and (1,-1), with eigenvalues 32/3 and 8/3. Keeping the diagonal axis maps the negative pair to (-2,-2) and the positive pair to (2,2). The four residuals have squared norm two each: SSE=8 and RMS=sqrt(8/8)=1. Zero axes gives SSE=40; both axes reconstruct to roundoff.

    A metric change can reverse the meaning of a smaller error

    Multiplying only y by 100 changes raw covariance PCA but leaves the standardized correlation matrix and its .8/.2 variance shares unchanged. This is a unit transformation, not new evidence about which variable matters. For a separate query, fit the same four-point pattern with y multiplied by ten and scaling enabled. Choose the query's standardized coordinates as (1,0). The leading standardized axis is the diagonal: its residual is (.5,-.5), half the preprocessed squared error of the zero-axis fit.     Checking rank and normalization

    Three returned axes can contain only one varying direction

    Two rows (0,0,5) and (1,2,5) give three feature axes but centred rank at most one. Sample standardization makes the first two columns equal and the third zero, so the spectrum is (2,0,0). The constant column has offset five and divisor one. A full basis is useful for an invertible coordinate description, but its null axes have no training variance to explain. For entirely constant centred data every variance share is zero; there is no meaningful one-hundred-percent leading axis.

    Iris measurements

    All 150 rows have four complete numeric flower measurements in centimetres. The four features enter every fit; Species is withheld and supplies colours only afterwards. Each species has fifty rows. The full-data fits are descriptive, not held-out estimates of prediction quality. No species label is used to select axes or a retained dimension.

    Scaled scores and rotation coefficients describe one metric

    This baseline centres and sample-standardizes all four measurements. The score axes mix them linearly; the spectrum table defines their variance shares and the loadings table preserves each coefficient. A large coefficient means a geometric contribution under this scaling convention, not a causal or biological importance. The species separation visible in the scores does not validate a classifier. Even uncorrelated component scores can be statistically dependent.

    Measure reconstruction and compare raw versus scaled fits

    The standardized training fit's discarded eigenvalues predict the measured preprocessed error at every retained prefix. k=0 is the mean-only reconstruction, and k=4 leaves only floating-point residuals. A raw covariance fit uses the same centred observations but minimizes error in centimetres rather than feature-standardized units. All four Iris features share those units, so their pooled original-unit RMS is interpretable in centimetres. The raw fit cannot have worse training raw-unit error than a scaled fit at the same k, apart from numerical tolerance. Its larger leading variance share reflects a different objective, not a more informative representation.

    Project thirty new flowers without fitting their centre

    A fixed row-index split holds out every fifth observation: 120 training rows and 30 test rows. This deterministic split is not a population sampling study. The training rows alone determine means, sample scales and axes. Test scores use those same axes; adding another query to their batch cannot change an individual projection. We compare one test row alone against its projection in the full test batch. Reconstruction curves measure all test rows against their actual measurements, with no query-centre fit and no training-tail equality assumed for the test data. The k=2 error describes this one representation and split, not classification accuracy or a tuned dimension choice.

    Conclusion

    The first two standardized Iris components retain 95.8132% of training energy, with RMS reconstruction error .203933 in standardized coordinates and .188513 cm in original units. The raw covariance fit retains 97.7685% of its own energy and has .159189 cm RMS error at k=2. Those percentages concern different metrics. On the fixed split, k=2 RMS in the training metric is .202648 for training rows and .226351 for test rows; this one comparison does not select a generally optimal k.

    The four-point calculation connects axes, eigenvalues and perpendicular projection error. Unit changes and the new-row counterexample show why variance shares and reconstruction loss need their metric. Full axis count is distinct from rank, and null or repeated directions limit interpretations of individual loadings. Iris then checks the same energy identity in four dimensions and applies frozen preprocessing to separate rows. These are geometric representation diagnostics, not proof of a biological signal, an optimal classifier or a universal retained-variance threshold.
