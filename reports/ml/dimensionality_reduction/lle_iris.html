    Locally Linear Embedding: Unrolling Manifolds

    Abstract

    Data measured in many dimensions often vary in only a few: a sheet of points rolled up in 3-D, or images of a digit that change by a handful of strokes. We want low-dimensional coordinates that follow such a curved surface, keeping neighbours together, where a linear projection such as PCA would fold distant parts of the surface on top of each other. Locally Linear Embedding (LLE) does this with two linear-algebra steps: it describes each point as a weighted average of its nearest neighbours, then finds the coordinates that the same weights reconstruct best, as the bottom eigenvectors of a sparse matrix. We derive both steps and check them by hand on four points, on seven points on a line, where the answer is exact, and on a spiral. On a flat sheet LLE recovers the true coordinates exactly. On the Swiss roll it unrolls the long direction (\(R^2=0.99\) against arc length) for a band of neighbourhood sizes, where PCA gets 0.08. Two failure modes are explained by the theory. With too little regularisation the weights reconstruct the raw coordinates exactly, and LLE returns a linear projection. With too many neighbours, edges bridge the layers of the roll. A third limit is that the short side of a long sheet competes with harmonics of the long side. On iris the neighbour graph falls into two pieces, which LLE embeds on separate axes. On handwritten digits it separates six classes better than PCA.

    The problem

    We have \(n\) points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb R^D\) that lie on or near a smooth surface of dimension \(d\ll D\), a manifold. We want points \(y_1,\dots,y_n\in\mathbb R^d\), one per \(x_i\), such that points close on the manifold stay close, and points far apart on the manifold stay apart. The second half is where linear methods fail. PCA projects onto the \(d\)-dimensional plane of largest variance. If the manifold is curved, a projection lays its distant parts on top of each other: the end of a rolled sheet lands on its middle.

    Nothing about the manifold is given except the sample. LLE uses one assumption: each small neighbourhood of the manifold is nearly flat, so each point is nearly an affine combination of its neighbours. These local descriptions overlap, and a global coordinate system that respects all of them at once is the embedding.

    The method

    Step 1: neighbours

    For each point, find its \(k\) nearest neighbours \(\mathcal N(i)\) in Euclidean distance (ties broken by index). \(k\) sets the scale at which the manifold is assumed flat: large enough that the neighbours span the \(d\) local directions, small enough that they stay on the same part of the manifold. Collect the edges \(i\to j\), \(j\in\mathcal N(i)\), into the neighbour graph.

    Step 2: reconstruction weights

    Choose weights \(w_{ij}\) (zero unless \(j\in\mathcal N(i)\)) that reconstruct each point from its neighbours as an affine combination:

    \[\min_{w_i}\ \varepsilon_i(w_i)=\Big\lVert x_i-\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}w_{ij}x_j\Big\rVert^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{j}w_{ij}=1.\]

    Because the weights sum to one, \(x_i-\sum_jw_{ij}x_j=\sum_jw_{ij}(x_i-x_j)\), so the error is a quadratic form \(\varepsilon_i=w_i^\top Cw_i\) in the local Gram matrix \(C_{ab}=(x_i-n_a)^\top(x_i-n_b)\) of the neighbour offsets \(n_a\in\mathcal N(i)\). The Lagrangian \(w^\top Cw-2\lambda(\mathbf 1^\top w-1)\) is stationary where \(Cw=\lambda\mathbf 1\), and the constraint fixes \(\lambda\):

    \[w_i=\frac{C^{-1}\mathbf 1}{\mathbf 1^\top C^{-1}\mathbf 1}.\]

    The weights are unchanged if the neighbourhood is rotated (\(C\) is unchanged), scaled (\(C\) is multiplied by a constant that cancels) or translated (the offsets do not change, and the constraint makes translation of \(x_i\) and its neighbours together harmless). So they describe the shape of the neighbourhood, not where it sits or how it is turned. If the manifold is locally flat, the same weights should reconstruct \(y_i\) from its neighbours' \(y_j\) in the manifold's own coordinates, which is the idea behind step 3. Weights may be negative: a point outside the convex hull of its neighbours is an extrapolation.

    The ridge. \(C\) is a \(k\times k\) Gram matrix of vectors in \(\mathbb R^D\), so its rank is at most \(\min(k,D)\). With more neighbours than dimensions (\(k>D\)), \(C\) is singular and many weight vectors reconstruct \(x_i\) exactly. LLE adds a ridge before solving:

    \[C\ \leftarrow\ C+r\operatorname{tr}(C)\,I .\]

    This solves \(\min_w\ \varepsilon_i(w)+r\operatorname{tr}(C)\lVert w\rVert^2\) under the same constraint. As \(r\to0\) it picks the exact reconstruction with the smallest weights. As \(r\to\infty\) every weight tends to \(1/k\), the plain neighbour average. Scaling by \(\operatorname{tr}(C)\) makes \(r\) dimensionless. datamunge's regularization is \(r\), with scikit-learn's meaning (its reg) and default \(10^{-3}\).

    Step 3: the embedding is a set of eigenvectors

    Keep the weights fixed and choose the low-dimensional points they reconstruct best. Write \(Y\) for the \(n\times d\) matrix with rows \(y_i^\top\) and \(W\) for the sparse matrix of weights:

    \[\Phi(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\lVert y_i-\sum_jw_{ij}y_j\Big\rVert^2=\lVert(I-W)Y\rVert_F^2=\operatorname{tr}\big(Y^\top MY\big), \qquad M=(I-W)^\top(I-W).\]

    \(\Phi\) is unchanged by translating every \(y_i\) (rows of \(W\) sum to one), and \(Y=0\) is a trivial minimiser. To rule both out, fix the centre and the scale: \(\mathbf 1^\top Y=0\) and \(Y^\top Y=I\). \(M\) is symmetric positive semi-definite. Because \((I-W)\mathbf 1=0\), the constant vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0. By the Rayleigh–Ritz theorem, the minimum of \(\operatorname{tr}(Y^\top MY)\) over orthonormal \(Y\) orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\) is attained by the eigenvectors of the \(d\) smallest remaining eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_d\), and equals their sum. Each column is an eigenvector, and its eigenvalue is its own cost, \(v^\top Mv\). LLE::cost() evaluates this cost for any candidate coordinate, so the arc length of a roll can be compared with what LLE found.

    Disconnected neighbour graphs. Weights only link a point to its neighbours. If the neighbour graph splits into \(c\) connected components, \(M\) is block diagonal (after reordering) and the indicator vector of every component is a null vector: \(M\) has \(c\) zero eigenvalues, not one. They are all discarded. The remaining eigenvectors are orthogonal to every indicator, so each coordinate sums to zero on every component. Generically, each coordinate is an eigenvector of one block only, so different components are embedded on different axes and nothing relates their positions. connected_components() reports \(c\).

    Why the ridge is not optional. Suppose every point is reconstructed exactly, as happens when \(k>D\) and \(r=0\). Then for any linear function \(\ell(x)=a^\top x\),

    \[\ell(x_i)-\sum_jw_{ij}\ell(x_j)=a^\top\Big(x_i-\sum_jw_{ij}x_j\Big)=0,\]

    so each of the \(D\) raw coordinates (centred) costs nothing. LLE then returns some linear projection of the data. For a flat sheet that is exactly right, but for a curved one it is no better than PCA. A ridge makes the reconstruction slightly inexact, so the raw coordinates acquire a cost while the manifold's own coordinates stay cheap. The ridge is how LLE tells the two apart.

    Computation

    Neighbours take \(O(n^2D)\) by brute force, the weights \(O(nk^3)\), and \(M\) has \(O(nk^2)\) non-zeros. datamunge computes the full eigendecomposition of \(M\) with linalg::symmetric_eigen() (Householder tridiagonalisation and implicit QL, \(O(n^3)\), about one second at \(n=1000\)). The trivial eigenvectors are known in advance (the component indicators), so they are moved above the spectrum by adding \(\sigma\,e e^\top/|e|\) for each indicator \(e\), with \(\sigma\) above a Gershgorin bound. This leaves the other eigenpairs unchanged. Without it, a near-zero embedding eigenvalue can mix with the constant vector, and the coordinate no longer sums to zero. Each column has unit length, and its sign is fixed so that its largest entry is positive. On the Swiss roll below the embedding agrees with scikit-learn's LocallyLinearEmbedding(eigen_solver="dense") to \(2\times10^{-9}\).

    Simple examples

    Reconstruction weights by hand

    Three neighbours \((1,0),(0,1),(-1,-1)\) around the origin: their mean is the origin, so \(w=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)\) reconstructs it exactly. With \(k=3>D=2\) the matrix \(C\) is singular (rank 2), so the exact reconstruction is unique only because \(\mathbf 1^\top w=1\) adds the third equation.

    Four neighbours \(n_1=(1,0)\), \(n_2=(-1,0)\), \(n_3=(0,1)\), \(n_4=(0,-2)\) of \(x=(0,0)\) admit a line of exact reconstructions: \(w_1=w_2\) (from the first coordinate), \(w_3=2w_4\) (from the second), and \(2w_1+3w_4=1\). The small ridge picks the one with the smallest \(\lVert w\rVert^2=2w_1^2+5w_4^2\). Minimising it on the line gives \(4w_1=2\mu\) and \(10w_4=3\mu\), so \(w_1=w_2=\tfrac5{19}=0.263\), \(w_3=\tfrac6{19}=0.316\) and \(w_4=\tfrac3{19}=0.158\). The far neighbour gets the smallest weight, and the near one on the same axis gets twice as much to balance it.

    Seven points on a line

    Take seven equally spaced points \(x_i=(1+0.6i,\,2+0.8i)\), \(i=0,\dots,6\), on a line in the plane, with \(k=2\). An interior point is the midpoint of its two neighbours, so its weights are \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\). An end point, \(x_0\), has neighbours \(x_1,x_2\) on the same side and is the extrapolation \(x_0=2x_1-x_2\), so its weights are \((2,-1)\). Both reconstructions are exact and are also exact for any affine function of the position on the line, in particular for the index \(i\). So \(M\) has a two-dimensional null space, spanned by \(\mathbf 1\) and \(i\). The embedding is the centred and normalised index, \(\pm(i-3)/\sqrt{28}\) (since \(\sum(i-3)^2=28\)). The sign is arbitrary; datamunge makes the largest entry positive, which on this symmetric example is the first point's.

    Points on a spiral

    The next example is curved. Sixty points on the spiral \((t\cos t,\ t\sin t)\), \(t\in[1.5\pi,4.5\pi]\), evenly spaced in \(t\), are the Swiss roll seen end on. The arc length \(s(t)=\int_0^t\sqrt{1+\tau^2}\,d\tau=\tfrac12(t\sqrt{1+t^2}+\operatorname{asinh}t)\) is the manifold's own coordinate. Successive points are 1.5 apart on the inside and 4.5 apart on the outside, so even spacing in \(t\) is far from even in \(s\). With \(k=2=D\) the matrix \(C\) is invertible and no reconstruction is exact. With \(k=4>D\) every point is reconstructed exactly, the situation of Why the ridge is not optional.

    On the spiral with \(k=2\), the coordinate is a linear function of the arc length (\(R^2=1.0000\)), not of \(t\), although the points are evenly spaced in \(t\). Near each point the spiral is almost a straight segment. The weights place \(x_i\) between its neighbours in proportion to the distances, and that is exactly what reconstructs \(s\). The cost table shows the mechanism: \(s\) costs \(10^{-7}\), the raw coordinates \(x\) and \(z\) about \(2\times10^{-4}\), so the cheapest coordinate is \(s\). With \(k=4\) the order flips. The four weights now reconstruct every point exactly in the plane, so \(x\) and \(z\) become nearly free (they cost only what the ridge allows), and \(s\), which the spiral's curvature stops from being reconstructed by a four-point stencil, costs more. More than half of the embedding's variance is now a linear function of \(x\) and \(z\) (\(R^2=0.54\)), and it folds back on itself, so it no longer orders the points along the spiral. The same competition decides the Swiss roll's regularisation below.

    Checking the theory

    A flat sheet is recovered exactly

    If the data lie on a \(d\)-dimensional plane in \(\mathbb R^D\), every intrinsic coordinate is an affine function of the raw coordinates. The argument of Why the ridge is not optional then works in LLE's favour: as \(r\to0\) the weights reconstruct each point exactly, the plane's own coordinates \((u,v)\) are null vectors of \(M\), and the embedding spans exactly \(\{u-\bar u,\ v-\bar v\}\). The check: 500 points uniform on a \(10\times6\) rectangle, mapped into \(\mathbb R^3\) by an affine map of rank 2, embedded with \(k=10\).

The default ridge \(10^{-3}\) makes the reconstructions slightly inexact. The two eigenvalues become \(10^{-8}\) and \(10^{-7}\), still 40 times below the third, and \(R^2\) drops only to 0.999. A large ridge, \(10^{-1}\), pulls the weights towards plain averages, which bend the coordinates near the edges of the sheet (\(R^2\) 0.98 and 0.95). The constraints hold to \(10^{-14}\) in every case.

    The Swiss roll

    The Swiss roll (dm::datasets::swiss_roll) is the spiral of the last section extruded to height 21: \((t\cos t,\ h,\ t\sin t)\) with \(t\) uniform on \([1.5\pi,4.5\pi]\) and \(h\) uniform on \([0,21]\). Unrolled, it is a flat \(89\times21\) rectangle with coordinates (arc length \(s\), height \(h\)), and the generator returns both. We embed 1000 points into two dimensions. Each method is scored three ways. The \(R^2\) of \(s\) and of \(h\), each regressed on \((Y_1,Y_2)\), is 1 for an unrolling up to an affine map. Trustworthiness and continuity \(T(10)\) and \(C(10)\) (stats::trustworthiness, Venna and Kaski 2001) range over \([0,1]\). \(T\) penalises points that are neighbours in the embedding but not in the data (folds), and \(C\) penalises the reverse (tears).

    There is a band of good \(k\), from 6 to 15, where the arc length is recovered with \(R^2\ge0.98\) and trustworthiness is about 0.996. Below it, \(k=5\) gives too few neighbours to span the two local directions robustly. Above it, the number of bridging edges grows, from 8 at \(k=12\) to 46 at \(k=20\) and 251 at \(k=30\), and the unrolling collapses: a bridge tells LLE that two layers are the same place. A few bridges are tolerated, because each point has several other neighbours on its own layer. Isomap is the contrast. It embeds the shortest-path distances of the neighbour graph, so on a clean graph (\(k=6\), no bridges) it unrolls the sheet almost perfectly, recovering both \(s\) and \(h\) (\(R^2\) 1.000 and 0.973), far better than LLE. But one bridge shortcuts every path that uses it: at \(k=10\) four bridges lower its \(R^2\) of \(s\) to 0.84 and lose the height.     Which eigenvectors come first

    LLE returns the cheapest coordinates, which need not be the ones we hope for. The bottom eigenvectors of \(M\) behave like the smooth vibration modes of the sheet (Belkin and Niyogi 2003 relate \(M\) to the square of the manifold's Laplacian). On an \(L_1\times L_2\) rectangle, the low modes are \(\cos(m\pi s/L_1)\cos(n\pi h/L_2)\), ordered by \((m/L_1)^2+(n/L_2)^2\). The Swiss roll's sheet is \(89\times21\), about 4.25 times longer than tall, so on this ordering the first few harmonics along \(s\), \(\cos(m\pi s/L_1)\) for \(m\le4\), are cheaper than the first mode across, \(\cos(\pi h/L_2)\). The picture is only a heuristic for LLE, but it predicts the competition: the second coordinate can be \(h\) or \(\cos(2\pi s/L_1)\), a function of \(s\) that carries no new information. To see what LLE actually finds, we compute five coordinates and regress each on \(s\), on the harmonics \(\cos(m\pi u)\) with \(u=(s-s_{\min})/(s_{\max}-s_{\min})\), and on \(h\). Then we stretch the roll's height to make the sheet less elongated.

    LLE with \(k=10\) separates the six digits into compact groups, and its trustworthiness (0.943) is well above PCA's (0.868): its neighbours in the plane are mostly neighbours in pixel space. Each group is squeezed into a short curve or a dot (all the zeros land on nearly one point). LLE's unit-variance constraint lets the cheapest solution put whole clusters almost on top of each other along one direction, a known weakness that modified variants (Hessian LLE, LTSA, modified LLE) address. With \(k=30\) neighbourhoods span several digits, and trustworthiness drops below PCA's.

    Conclusion

    LLE reduces nonlinear dimensionality reduction to linear algebra: a small constrained least squares problem per point, then the bottom eigenvectors of one sparse matrix. Each step was checked here against a closed form: the minimum-norm weights \((5,5,6,3)/19\), the line's embedding \((3-i)/\sqrt{28}\), and the exact recovery of a flat sheet. The theory also explains the failures. With \(k\) larger than the input dimension and too small a ridge, the raw coordinates become free and LLE returns a linear projection, so the ridge is essential and has a window of about one decade. Too large a \(k\) bridges separate parts of the manifold. A disconnected neighbour graph embeds its components on unrelated axes. On a long thin sheet, the short direction competes with harmonics of the long one, so the coordinate we want may not be among the first two. When these are respected, LLE unrolls the Swiss roll's long direction (\(R^2=0.99\)), where PCA gets 0.08, and separates handwritten digits more faithfully than PCA. It needs no distances along the manifold. That makes it far less sensitive than Isomap to a few bridging edges, although Isomap on a clean neighbour graph unrolls the roll better. It does need care with \(k\) and \(r\), and its output is a distorted, unit-variance picture, not a map that preserves distances.
