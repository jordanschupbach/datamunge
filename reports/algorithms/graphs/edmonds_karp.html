    Edmonds–Karp: Actual Shortest Paths, Cancellations and Cut Witnesses

    Abstract

    Given a directed network with capacity limits, we want to send as much flow as possible from a source to a sink while conserving flow elsewhere. An earlier routing decision may need to be undone to make room for a better one. Edmonds–Karp repeatedly finds a shortest residual path and pushes its bottleneck. We inspect a tiny bottleneck and an actual reverse cancellation, preserve the original six-vertex example, and check exact search counts against a blocking-flow method. Owned execution evidence, cut certificates and explicit numerical limits make the results checkable.

    Start with one bottleneck and a matching cut

    Take s=0, an intermediate vertex 1 and t=2, with capacities three and two along the chain. A first BFS finds 0→1→2. Its bottleneck is min(3,2)=2: pushing two leaves residual capacities one and zero. The final BFS still reaches vertex 1, but cannot reach the sink. Thus S={0,1}, and its only outgoing capacity is two. Flow and cut agree.

    Derive residual augmentation

    For each original input edge e=(u,v) of capacity c_e, keep its flow f_e with 0<=f_e<=c_e. At every vertex except the terminals,

    \[\sum_{e\text{ leaving }v}f_e-\sum_{e\text{ entering }v}f_e=0.\]

    The source's net outgoing flow is the objective value. Every input edge owns a forward residual arc of capacity c_e−f_e and a reverse cancellation arc of capacity f_e. Parallel edges keep separate pairs; an original v→u edge is distinct from the cancellation arc of u→v. A self edge does not increase source-to-sink value and is never part of a simple augmenting path.

    BFS traverses strictly positive residual arcs. Its parent chain to the sink is a shortest path by edge count. Let delta be the minimum used residual. Decrease each used residual by delta and increase its paired opposite by delta. A forward use increases the original flow; a reverse use decreases it. Internal path vertices gain and lose equal amounts, so conservation remains true, while the source value increases by delta. The bottleneck ensures capacity bounds remain satisfied in exact arithmetic.

    The kernel completes every BFS, even after discovering the sink. This keeps all actual distances and first-discovery parent arc ids available. It preserves the original first sink path because the graph does not change during a search. Oriented ids 2e and 2e+1 identify an input edge's forward and reverse arcs; SIZE_MAX means no parent. Unreachable distance is V. The final failed search directly supplies the cut, with no uncounted second reachability traversal.

    If S is the final reachable set, no positive residual crosses from S to its complement. Every original S→complement edge is therefore saturated, and every complement→S edge has zero flow: otherwise its cancellation arc would leave S. Summing conservation over S gives flow value equal to outgoing cut capacity. Any feasible flow is bounded above by any separating cut, so these matching witnesses establish optimality in exact arithmetic. The numerical certificate reports feasibility errors and the signed duality gap separately; nonzero tolerance would mean numerical agreement, rather than an exact proof.

    Why shortest paths bound the work

    After an augmentation, residual source distances cannot decrease. Any newly positive arc is a reverse of an arc used on the shortest path; it points from level d+1 back to level d. Such an arc cannot create a shorter source path. Induction along a hypothetical first shortened path makes this contradiction precise. Complete BFS records let us inspect all distances, including vertices that become unreachable.

    Each augmentation saturates at least one oriented residual arc (u,v). For that same arc to be saturated again, it must first regain capacity through reverse use. Between those events, its tail distance increases by at least two. A vertex has fewer than V finite distance levels, so an oriented arc can be charged O(V) times. Summing over the 2E residual arcs gives O(VE) augmentations, independently of capacity magnitudes in exact arithmetic.

    Writing A for actual augmentations, the implementation costs O((A+1)(V+E)) for full array initialization and BFS, plus path work O(AV). Ordinary storage is O(V+E). The usual bound can be written O(V+E+VE²), including the initial/final search for empty graphs. To account for many isolated vertices, charge augmentations using W incident vertices: A=O(WE), with W<=min(V,2E) when a path exists. Then both terms WE·V and WE² are bounded by O(VE²). Trace copying adds its explicitly counted history cost. These bounds do not make checked double arithmetic an exact-real solver.

    Check an exact repeated-search family

    For k parallel source-to-sink edges of unit capacity, every path has one edge and exactly one edge saturates per push. Hence A=k and there are k+1 complete BFS calls. Each successful BFS reaches both vertices and inspects all 2k residual adjacency entries. The final failed BFS inspects the source's k entries, giving

    \[I_{EK}=2k²+k,\qquad L=k.\]

    Dinic instead sends all k units in one blocking phase. It still performs k path pushes, but needs only two BFS calls and 3k BFS inspections. This compares these logical searches on one controlled family, not wall-clock performance or a universal speed ranking. Scaling capacities by a positive power of two changes the flow values but not these counts.

    Boundary semantics, numerical progress and owned history

    All capacities must be finite and nonnegative, all endpoints in range, and terminals distinct and valid. Complete validation and checked residual/vector geometry precede adjacency allocation. Strictly positive residuals include subnormal capacities; no arbitrary epsilon discards them. Parallel and antiparallel edges retain their own pairs, while self edges remain unused by a simple source-to-sink path.

    Double arithmetic cannot represent every update. Each used residual must strictly decrease, each paired residual and the total must strictly increase, and sums must remain finite. A lost positive update raises an error; overflow raises an error. The original code accepted NaN/infinite capacities and recovered edge flow by subtracting nearly equal capacity and forward residual values. A saved probe with capacities 1e300 and unit bottlenecks reported total two but edge flows 0,1,1,0, violating internal conservation. This implementation returns no partial result when the required residual update cannot be represented. Recovering successful per-edge flows from cancellation residuals also avoids that nearly-equal subtraction. This is not arbitrary-precision arithmetic.

    The trace counts E original input records and one network header; each of P complete searches costs one header plus V levels and V parents; each of A pushes costs one header plus its path-arc records. Writing L for total path length,

    \[C=E+1+P(1+2V)+A+L.\]

    The minimum including the first BFS is checked before input copying or adjacency allocation. Later search/path costs are reserved before their traversal or push. The default cap is one million entries; zero disables only the optional quota. Result copies, working arrays, scalar metadata, vector capacity and allocator bytes are excluded, so C is not a peak-memory bound. Quota or arithmetic failure returns no partial trace. Ordinary/work calls retain no optional history and have no optional quota.

    The drawing helper validates finite shapes, cut ids and node positions, but does not certify arbitrary caller-edited flows or cuts. The independent certificate API is the appropriate place to inspect feasibility and gaps. Owned traces can also be edited; their records are evidence from a run, rather than a self-validating proof object.

    What the examples establish

    Shortest residual paths expose both the bottleneck and the cancellations needed to repair earlier routing. The original flow of 23 has matching primal and cut witnesses, while complete BFS evidence and controlled parallel edges make operation counts explicit. Checked finite progress and retained-history limits describe the implementation's practical boundary; they do not replace the exact-arithmetic theorem with an unqualified numerical guarantee.
