    Chien Search: Actual Coefficient States over a Prime Field

    Abstract

    Finding every root of a polynomial over a finite field requires covering the field without missing candidates or overflowing modular arithmetic. Chien search visits nonzero elements in primitive-root order and updates each coefficient's contribution by a fixed multiplier. We inspect actual states in a small example, retain the original GF(13) cubic and plot, and count the operations this native implementation really performs. Canonical C++ now records generator rejections, coefficient states and shortcut paths without replay, with explicit limits for field sweeps and generator discovery. Prime fields, binary extension fields, formal zero polynomials and repeated roots have different contracts; those distinctions matter before using root search inside a decoder.

    GF(7): roots in discovery order, not numeric order

    Take f(x)=x²+2x+6=(x−2)(x−3) over GF(7). The smallest primitive root is g=3. It visits [1,3,2,6,4,5], covering every nonzero element once. Starting with coefficient contributions [6,2,1], the fixed multipliers [1,3,2] produce [6,6,2] at x=3, then [6,4,4] at x=2. Both sums are zero modulo seven. The returned roots are therefore [3,2], rather than [2,3].

    For normalized coefficients c_j and point g^i, maintain

    \[t_j^{(i)}=c_jg^{ij},\qquad f(g^i)=\sum_jt_j^{(i)}\pmod p, \qquad t_j^{(i+1)}=t_j^{(i)}g^j\pmod p.\]

    At i=0, g0=1, so the initial terms are the normalized coefficients. Multiplying by gj preserves the displayed invariant by induction. A primitive generator has order p−1, so its p−1 powers are distinct and exhaust the nonzero field. Checking c0=f(0) separately completes the field coverage. The modular sum is therefore zero exactly at each returned candidate root. This proves the recurrence and coverage, not the existence of an error-locator polynomial or the correctness of a complete decoding pipeline.

    Preserve the GF(13) cubic and check its values in numeric order

    The original polynomial is x³+2x²+3x+4 over GF(13). Keep all thirteen values and the root x=2. The search visits powers of g=2; the table and plot below use numeric x order for readability. Native poly_evaluate_gf() supplies the separate Horner evaluation path, replacing the report's unchecked local multiply/add helper. Independent unsigned-arithmetic oracles are used in the native tests rather than treating two shared-library calls as independent proof.

    The old report said the polynomial takes every residue. It takes only nine of the thirteen possible values, with repetitions. Visiting every input does not imply producing every output. Connections between discrete plot points guide the eye; they do not express continuity, interpolation or an unpredictable random process over a finite field.

    A primitive root must pass its order tests; operation counts include the code actually run

    For each distinct prime divisor q of p−1, a candidate generator must satisfy g^((p−1)/q)≠1 modulo p. If its order were a proper divisor of p−1, at least one such exponent would be a multiple of that order, causing rejection. Passing every test therefore certifies full order. In GF(7), g=2 fails 2³=1 and cycles through only [1,2,4], missing both roots of the worked quadratic. The next candidate, g=3, passes the two required tests.

    The table records those actual tests. The degree sweep then counts the native implementation's coefficient-state products. For degree at least two, it updates all d+1 coefficient terms, including the constant, at every one of the p−1 nonzero points. It also preserves the final unused update that closes the orbit, advances the point, and computes each multiplier g^j separately. Consequently, a degree-only count is not the complete executed operation count.

    Original cubic: 3 generator steps, 7 generator products, 6 setup products, 48 term products, 12 point products, 48 sum additions.

    The original cubic performs 48 term updates, 12 point updates and six setup products, plus seven products in generator tests. It also makes 48 modular sum additions. Prime testing, normalization, allocations and integer operations while factoring p−1 are outside those named counters. A generator step means one trial-divisor attempt or one generator power test; repeated successful divisions and products within a test are not extra generator steps.

    The plot's shortcut discontinuity is deliberate: nonzero constants return no roots, and linears use an inverse rather than sweeping the field. The orange 12d formula models one nonconstant-term update per point; the blue sweep includes the constant term. Removing that identity multiply or the closing update would change the counts. No elapsed times or speedup are claimed. Fixed multipliers are useful to hardware architectures, but integer prime-field modular products here are not binary-extension-field gate counts.

    Zero polynomials, multiplicities and field representations

    Formal zero polynomial: legacy roots [], recorded points 0. Repeated polynomial x2 + 9x + 4: distinct roots [2], squarefree=false. Nonzero formal polynomial x13 + 12x: 13 distinct roots, all nonzero evaluations zero=true. GF(2) roots of x^2+x: [0, 1]; generator 1. Composite p=256 rejected: true.

    Mathematically the zero polynomial vanishes everywhere. The legacy API instead returns an empty root vector for its normalized zero representation; the trace marks that sentinel path explicitly. An empty result on that path does not mean there are no mathematical roots. Nonzero constants have none, while a linear polynomial has exactly one. The repeated quadratic (x−2)² returns root 2 once, without its multiplicity. Root search is not squarefree factorization.

    A nonzero formal polynomial of degree at least p can also vanish at every field element: x^p−x is an example. This does not contradict the degree bound on roots, since it has degree p and exactly p distinct roots. Its incremental trace is different from the formal-zero sentinel.

    The modulus here must be prime; arithmetic is ordinary residue arithmetic in GF(p). A binary extension field GF(2^m), with m>1, requires a polynomial basis and reduction polynomial (or an equivalent representation). Passing 256 as p does not request GF(256). This report constructs no syndrome, error-locator polynomial or inverse-power mapping from roots to codeword error positions. It demonstrates the root-finding recurrence on prime-field examples.

    Complete-call limits and the direct-linear path

    Exact caps succeed: roots [3, 2], 7 field checks, 18 term updates, 4 generator steps. One-below caps reject: points=true, term products=true, generator steps=true. Billion-point trace rejected by generator cap before giant record reservation: true. Near-INT64_MAX linear root [1], with 0 field-point checks and 0 generator steps.

    Positive point and term-product caps are forecast from normalized degree and checked before generator search or sweep allocation. Generator-step caps apply to the actual search; trace-state reservation follows successful generator discovery. Zero caps mean no explicit limit. All failed calls return no partial root set or trace and leave input coefficients unchanged. Linear shortcuts use inversion products, which are counted separately and do not consume sweep or generator budgets. Unbounded high-degree searches remain intended for small p.

    The nonzero sweep costs O((p−1)n) fixed-word operations for n coefficients, with trial-factoring/generator discovery and multiplier setup in addition. Ordinary calls retain O(n) state plus roots and factor-search metadata; tracing adds (p−1)n coefficient values and generator-test records. These are not arbitrary- precision bit-complexity or machine-time bounds. No observational dataset is needed for exact field arithmetic and controlled conformance examples.

    Native tests exhaust all 23,941 coefficient vectors through length five over GF(2), GF(3), GF(5) and GF(7), including zero/trailing-zero representations. An independent Horner oracle and a generator-order walk check values, coverage, root order, every actual term state and operation counters. Another 1,000 signed random polynomials and 1,000 near-limit linear cases test widened arithmetic. Exact budgets, early huge-sweep rejection, malformed fields, retained closing updates, copied ownership and the original polynomial/formatting cases remain regression checks. AddressSanitizer and UBSan exercise the new cases.
