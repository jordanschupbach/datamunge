    Extended Euclid: Actual Remainders, Bezout Witnesses and Safe Inverses

    Abstract

    Find the greatest common divisor of two integers and coefficients that express it as their integer combination. Those coefficients also identify modular inverses when the inputs are coprime. We begin with 240 and 46, inspect every actual quotient/coefficient row, and retain the original 100,000-pair check. Signed examples, a Fibonacci family and full-width boundary cases separate mathematical identities from machine-integer range limits. One widened native C++ kernel serves ordinary results, unsigned-gcd certificates and optional owned traces; inverse traces capture their actual solve in the same pass.

    Begin with 240 and 46

    Dividing 240 by 46 gives quotient five and remainder ten. Applying that same row operation to coefficients gives 10=1·240−5·46. Continuing produces remainders 6,4,2,0, and the last nonzero row is 2=−9·240+47·46. The zero row is still part of the actual execution: 0=23·240−120·46. The five quotients are 5,4,1,1,2.

    The following program reads the two initial rows and every derived row from the native trace. It checks s·a+t·b=r with widened arithmetic and renders the same stored values. The final sign-normalised result is separate from the raw rows; in this positive example no sign flip occurs.

    Why the row operations certify a greatest common divisor

    For any integer quotient q, a common divisor of r_old and r also divides r_old−q·r, and the reverse implication follows by adding q·r back. Thus the set of common divisors is unchanged. A nonzero divisor produces a remainder whose absolute value is smaller; repeated division terminates. The last nonzero remainder has the gcd's magnitude.

    Track two coefficient rows. Initially, 1·a+0·b=a and 0·a+1·b=b. When q=old_r/r is the actual truncating quotient, update

    \[r'=r_{old}-qr,\qquad s'=s_{old}-qs,\qquad t'=t_{old}-qt.\]

    Linearity preserves r'=s'a+t'b. At termination, negate the surviving remainder and both coefficients if that remainder is negative. The returned gcd is nonnegative and its coefficients satisfy a·x+b·y=gcd. The consecutive coefficient rows also retain determinant +1 or −1; each subtract-and-swap step reverses its sign, which tests their integer unimodular structure independently of a plotted remainder curve.

    The core's certificate checker verifies both the identity and that the claimed positive gcd divides both input magnitudes. If d divides a and b, then d divides a·x+b·y=g; together with g being a common divisor this certifies greatestness. An identity alone is insufficient: 6·1+10·0=6 does not make six a common divisor of six and ten. For two zero inputs, gcd zero is the explicit convention. Actual legacy coefficients are (1,0), correcting the old comment that promised (0,0).

    The implementation retains input order and C++ truncation toward zero, including an initial quotient zero when needed. It is a signed-input variant of the usual nonnegative ordered presentation. The Handbook of Applied Cryptography's algorithms 2.107 and 2.142 supply the standard extended-gcd and inverse construction; their input conventions differ from this native signed interface.

    Retain the original results and the 100,000-pair check

    The original compiled result is ExtendedGcd{2,−9,47}. Its three signed int64 fields and compiled signature remain. The original seeded random check below still uses 100,000 pairs in [−100000,100000]. That range is safe for std::gcd's signed-magnitude precondition. The native certificate checker handles the Bezout products without a report-local Euclid loop.

    The book's example 2.109, (4864,3458), supplies a second known answer: 38=32·4864−45·3458. Counts distinguish it from the original pair and show that reversed input order can add a genuine zero-quotient division.

    From the same-pass certificate to an inverse

    If a·x+m·y=1, reducing modulo m gives a·x congruent to one. Conversely, an inverse implies the gcd divides one, so coprimality is necessary and sufficient. Reduce the actual coefficient x into [0,m). The implementation first takes x%m, then adds m only if that remainder is negative, avoiding an overflowing unreduced x+m expression.

    modular_inverse_trace captures the actual Euclid rows during that inverse computation, rather than reconstructing them afterward. Positive m is validated before history admission. A nonunit throws when its gcd is known; use an ordinary gcd certificate to inspect that failure separately. The legacy API permits m=1 and returns its sole residue zero, including for a=0 or INT64_MIN. In that degenerate modulus, one and zero are congruent; it should not be checked by demanding a raw remainder of one.

    For 3 modulo 11, four modular additions of three reach residue one. The circle below is a conceptual residue check using the existing safe modular-multiplication API. The Euclidean trace, not the circle, computes the inverse. No claim about cryptographic constant-time execution is made.

    Signed quotients and sign normalisation are separate facts

    C++ division truncates toward zero: −240/46 gives quotient −5 and remainder −10. A floor quotient would be −6 with positive remainder six; that is a different row path. Both can express the gcd, but this API preserves the truncating path and its canonical coefficients.

    The actual signed remainder can change sign along a path. Its magnitude still follows the decreasing unsigned Euclidean sequence. A final sign flip occurs only when the surviving raw gcd is negative, not whenever any input is negative. The mixed-sign pair (−240,46) and the pair (−240,−46) illustrate that distinction. Raw trace rows are retained before normalisation; result coefficients are the sign-normalised certificate.

    Division counts, Fibonacci inputs and bit complexity

    Remainder magnitudes are nonnegative and decrease until zero. Over two successive positive remainder reductions, the magnitude falls by at least a factor of two: either the next remainder is already at most half the current one, or the following subtraction leaves less than half. This gives a logarithmic division count. Zero inputs are constant-work cases; an initial zero quotient can add one division when input order is reversed.

    Consecutive Fibonacci numbers approach the slowest shrinkage because most quotients are one. The native counts below include the terminal zero step. F_92=7540113804746346429 and F_91=4660046610375530309 fit signed int64; they require ninety divisions in that order and ninety-one in reverse. A similarly wide input paired with one needs only one division, showing that bit width alone does not determine a particular run's work.

    These are fixed-word operation counts on int64 inputs with signed128-bit intermediates. They are not an O(log n) bit-operation claim for unbounded integers: larger divisions have their own cost, and the Handbook gives a quadratic logarithmic bit bound for its basic arithmetic model. Ordinary calls use constant auxiliary state. An owned trace stores C=2+D whole row records for D divisions; metadata, capacities, allocator bytes and runtime temporaries are excluded, so C is not peak memory.

    Full int64 inputs, narrow results and terminal-row overflow

    INT64_MIN has magnitude 2^63, which is not representable as a positive int64. The legacy three-signed-field result therefore throws overflow for (INT64_MIN,0), (0,INT64_MIN) and (INT64_MIN,INT64_MIN). The new certificate stores its nonnegative gcd in uint64 and supports all three while retaining signed canonical coefficients. The certificate checker uses safe unsigned magnitudes and checked widened product addition, including malformed claims.

    Even a final gcd of one can require wider intermediate arithmetic. INT64_MIN divided by −1 has quotient +263. With divisor one, a terminal zero row has coefficient +263 even though the returned coefficient is small. Signed128-bit working rows avoid both failures, including updates that are discarded from the legacy result. Strict old-source sanitizer probes reproduce division, negation and terminal-coefficient overflows.

    Calling std::gcd on signed INT64_MIN violates its representable-magnitude precondition, even if the final gcd would be small. The boundary program uses the native certificate checker; independent tests use unsigned magnitudes and Python arbitrary-precision integers. Ordinary int64 multiplication a*x+b*y is also unsafe for general full-width certificates. Use widened arithmetic for actual bounded rows or the native checker for arbitrary claimed int64-coefficient certificates.

    Optional trace admission checks its initial two records before allocation and each next record before its division. The default row cap is one million; zero disables only that optional quota. Exact and one-short limits are checked below. Ordinary and work-only calls keep no optional history or quota. Exceptions expose no partial trace; returned rows own their values.
