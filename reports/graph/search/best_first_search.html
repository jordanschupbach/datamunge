    Greedy Best-First Search

    Abstract

    Greedy best-first search (GBFS) always expands the vertex whose heuristic \(h\) says it is closest to the goal, ignoring the cost already paid. We prove that with the perfect heuristic \(h^*\) it expands only the cells of the path it returns, and that the path can still be suboptimal. We also derive the high-water mark bounds: before reaching the goal, GBFS must flood every heuristic depression it starts in. On the volcano terrain, GBFS walks straight to the goal, expanding 76 cells against A*'s 1,732, but pays 17% more. With the perfect heuristic it pays 22% more, because it takes the steps that lower \(h^*\) fastest, not the cheapest. On a 10-nearest-neighbour graph of the handwritten digits, best-first search with a result list of size \(e\!f\) is the search loop of HNSW-style vector indexes. Greedy routing (\(e\!f=1\)) stops in a local minimum for 271 of 297 queries. The depressions it falls into hold 16 vertices at the median, and the escape bounds hold for every query. With \(e\!f=128\) the graph search finds the exact neighbour 99.7% of the time from 409 distance computations, beating a k-d tree at high recall but not at low budgets. New library pieces are algorithms::terrain_best_first, algorithms::high_water_mark and geometry::proximity_graph_search.

    The algorithm

    Best-first search keeps an open list of discovered but unexpanded vertices and always expands the one with the smallest evaluation \(f\). Greedy best-first search (GBFS) uses the heuristic alone, \(f(v)=h(v)\), an estimate of the remaining cost to the goal, and ignores the cost \(g(v)\) already paid to reach \(v\). A* uses \(f=g+h\). Writing \(f=\alpha g+h\) puts both on one scale: \(\alpha=0\) is greedy and \(\alpha=1\) is A*. algorithms::terrain_best_first takes \(\alpha\) and any table of heuristic values.

    The closed list makes GBFS complete on a finite graph. Each vertex is expanded at most once, so it always finds a path if one exists. But nothing makes the path cheap, and nothing bounds the work below "the whole graph". Both depend entirely on how \(h\) is shaped.

    With a perfect heuristic

    Let \(h=h^*\), the exact cost to go. If \(u\) is expanded and \(u'\) is its successor on an optimal path, then \(h^*(u')=h^*(u)-c(u,u')<h^*(u)\). Every vertex still in the open list was generated before \(u\) was chosen, so it has \(h^*\ge h^*(u)>h^*(u')\). The next vertex expanded is therefore a neighbour of \(u\) with smaller \(h^*\) than \(u\). The expansions form a single chain with strictly decreasing \(h^*\), ending at the goal. GBFS with \(h^*\) *expands exactly the cells of the path it returns, and nothing else*.

    The path need not be optimal, though. GBFS steps to the neighbour \(m\) with the smallest \(h^*(m)\), not the smallest \(c(u,m)+h^*(m)\). Its total cost exceeds the optimum by the sum of the per-step excesses:

    \[\operatorname{cost}-C^*=\sum_{i}\Big(c(u_i,u_{i+1})+h^*(u_{i+1})-h^*(u_i)\Big)\;\ge\;0 ,\]

    where each term vanishes exactly when the step lies on an optimal path from \(u_i\). A step can be expensive and still lower \(h^*\) the most, for example a steep climb straight towards the goal.

    The high-water mark

    With a weaker \(h\), GBFS can wander. Wilt and Ruml (2014) gave a sharp description of how far. Define the high-water mark

    \[H=\min_{\text{paths }P:\,s\to t}\;\max_{v\in P}h(v),\]

    the lowest "water level" at which some start–goal path stays dry. Then:

    • *GBFS never expands a vertex with \(h>H\).* Some path keeps \(h\le H\) all the way. Until the goal is expanded, one of its vertices is always in the open list, and it is preferred to any vertex with \(h>H\).
    • *GBFS expands every vertex reachable from the start through vertices with \(h<H\).* Call this region \(R\). The goal is not in \(R\), or \(H\) would be smaller. While part of \(R\) is unexpanded, the open list holds a vertex of \(R\) with \(h<H\), and it is preferred to every vertex with \(h\ge H\), the goal included.

    So \(|R|\le\) expansions \(\le|\{v\text{ reachable through }h\le H\}|\). A heuristic depression (a basin where \(h\) is low but the goal cannot be reached without climbing out) must be flooded in full before GBFS can leave it. algorithms::high_water_mark computes \(H\) by a minimax version of Dijkstra's algorithm and returns both region sizes. Since every path includes the start, \(H\ge h(s)\), and \(R\) is non-empty only when the start itself lies in a depression.

    On the volcano

    We reuse the walking problem of the A* and beam-search reports. dm::datasets::volcano() gives Maunga Whau's elevation on a 10 m grid. A step costs its horizontal length plus 8.3 m for each metre climbed (Naismith's rule), and the walk goes from the south-east foot of the cone to the north-west corner. The admissible heuristic OctileClimb is the 8-neighbour distance plus 8.3 times the net height still to gain. The perfect heuristic \(h^*\) comes from one backward Dijkstra run (terrain_cost_to_go). Between them we blend

    \[h_\lambda=(1-\lambda)\,h_{\text{octile}}+\lambda\,h^*,\qquad 0\le\lambda\le1 ,\]

    which stays admissible and consistent, since both ends are. We run GBFS and A* for each \(\lambda\).

    Greedy best-first search never backtracks on this terrain. With the octile heuristic it expands 76 cells and returns a path of exactly those 76 cells, for every \(\lambda\) up to 0.75. With \(\lambda\ge0.9\) it is 83 and 83. The octile-climb heuristic has no depressions here: from every cell some neighbour lowers \(h\). The map shows the cost of that single-mindedness. GBFS heads diagonally for the goal, climbs over the cone's south-eastern shoulder and pays 16.7% more than the optimum. The optimal path instead walks north along the low eastern edge.

    A better heuristic makes greedy search worse here: with \(h_{0.5}\) the excess is 19.3%, and with the perfect \(h^*\) it is 22.4%. This is the per-step excess derived above at work. \(h^*\) falls fastest along steep climbs towards the goal, which are expensive, while the cheap route first moves sideways. The same heuristics make A* steadily better. It always returns \(C^*\), and its expansions fall from 1,732 with the octile heuristic to 150 with \(h^*\), close to the 99 cells of an optimal path. GBFS uses \(h\) to choose a direction, A* to prune, and only pruning benefits from accuracy.

    Best-first search on a proximity graph

    Greedy best-first search has a second life as the search loop of the graph indexes behind modern vector databases (navigable small-world graphs and HNSW, Malkov et al. 2014, Malkov and Yashunin 2018). The points are vertices, edges join near neighbours, and the heuristic is the distance to the query, \(h(v)=\|v-q\|\). Here the goal is unknown: it is whichever vertex minimises \(h\). So the stopping rule changes. A result list keeps the \(e\!f\) closest vertices seen, and the search stops when the closest unexpanded vertex is farther than all of them. With \(e\!f=1\) this is greedy routing: move to the closest neighbour until none is closer. It stops at the first local minimum of \(h\), a heuristic depression of size one. A larger \(e\!f\) keeps more of the open list alive and climbs out of shallow depressions. geometry::proximity_graph_search implements this loop.

    The graph is connected, and each digit has 13.5 neighbours on average. Greedy routing from vertex 0 costs 36 distance computations and finds the exact nearest neighbour for only 8.8% of queries. On average it stops 2.3 times as far from the query as the true neighbour. Only 10.1% of its answers have the right digit, which is chance level: after about three hops it is still near the entry vertex. It stops in a local minimum of \(h\), a vertex all of whose neighbours are farther from the query. From such a vertex \(m\), GBFS towards the true neighbour has to flood the depression \(R(m)\) first: 16 vertices at the median and 24 on average. It then spends about as much again on the far side, 36 expansions at the median in total. The high-water bounds hold for all 271 stuck queries. The upper bound is loose (532 at the median), because once over the barrier, the goal's own basin of low \(h\) is large.

    Raising \(e\!f\) lets the search climb out of depressions. Recall reaches 0.66 at \(e\!f=32\), 0.93 at 64 and 0.997 at 128, and 1-NN accuracy reaches 94.3%, the exact value being 94.6%. Against Best Bin First the picture depends on the budget. Below about 250 distance computations the k-d tree is better: the graph search spends its first 30 or so computations just walking from vertex 0 towards the query's region. Above that budget the graph wins clearly, with 0.997 recall from 409 computations against the k-d tree's 0.936 from 510. HNSW addresses the entry-point cost with a hierarchy of sparser graphs that brings the search close to the query in a few long hops.

    Summary

    • Greedy best-first search orders the open list by \(h\) alone. It is complete with a closed list, but neither its cost nor its work is bounded.
    • With the perfect heuristic, its expansions are exactly its path. Its excess cost is \(\sum_i(c(u_i,u_{i+1})+h^*(u_{i+1})-h^*(u_i))\), which a step can make positive by lowering \(h^*\) quickly at high cost. On the volcano, \(h^*\) made GBFS 22% worse than optimal, against 17% with the octile heuristic, while it cut A*'s work from 1,732 cells to 150.
    • The high-water mark \(H=\min_P\max_{v\in P}h(v)\) brackets GBFS. It expands every vertex reachable through \(h<H\) and none with \(h>H\). Heuristic depressions must be flooded in full.
    • On a 10-NN graph of the digits, greedy routing gets stuck in a depression for 91% of queries. Best-first search with a result list of 128 finds the exact neighbour 99.7% of the time from 409 distance computations, better than a k-d tree at high recall. Its weakness is the walk from a fixed entry point, which HNSW's hierarchy removes.
