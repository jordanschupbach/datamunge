    Bidirectional Search

    Abstract

    Bidirectional search finds a shortest path by growing one search from the start and one from the goal until they meet. We derive the stopping rules that make it exact: finish the whole layer in breadth-first search, and stop when the heap tops sum to the best path seen in Dijkstra's algorithm. Stopping at the first contact is wrong, and the library's earlier implementation, which did so, returned invalid paths for 9% of random graphs. How much the method saves depends on how fast balls grow: the exponent is halved when growth is exponential, but the saving is only a constant \(2^{D-1}\) when growth is polynomial of degree \(D\). On a 10-nearest-neighbour graph of 1,797 handwritten digits, where balls grow by a factor of 15 then 5 per hop, bidirectional BFS does 4 to 20 times less work, and a ball-growth model predicts it. On the Maunga Whau volcano, a planar grid, the saving for short routes is the predicted factor 2. For routes across the grid it falls to about 1, because the map edge clips the single large search.

    The idea

    To find a shortest path from \(s\) to \(t\), breadth-first search and Dijkstra's algorithm grow a ball around \(s\) until it reaches \(t\). Bidirectional search (Pohl 1971) grows two balls at once, one around \(s\) along the edges and one around \(t\) along the reversed edges, and stops when they meet. Each ball only needs radius about \(d/2\), where \(d\) is the distance from \(s\) to \(t\).

    How much that saves depends on how ball sizes grow with radius. Let \(|B(r)|\) be the number of vertices within distance \(r\).

    • *Exponential growth*, \(|B(r)|\approx b^r\), as in a tree with branching factor \(b\). One ball of radius \(d\) costs \(b^d\), and two of radius \(d/2\) cost \(2b^{d/2}\). The saving is \(b^{d/2}/2\), so the exponent is halved.
    • *Polynomial growth*, \(|B(r)|\approx cr^D\), as in a \(D\)-dimensional grid or road network. The ratio is \(c\,d^D/\big(2c(d/2)^D\big)=2^{D-1}\), a constant: 2 in the plane and 4 in space, whatever the distance.

    The method does nothing about the work inside each ball. It only exploits the gap between one large ball and two small ones, so that gap is what the measurements below look at.

    Stopping correctly

    The searches must not simply stop the first time they touch.

    Unweighted graphs

    algorithms::bidirectional_bfs grows the side with the smaller frontier by one complete layer at a time. While expanding a layer it records every edge \(u\to v\) whose end \(v\) is already labelled by the other side, which offers a path of length \(d_s(u)+1+d_t(v)\). It stops after the first layer that finds any such edge and returns the best one.

    Why the whole layer? Suppose the forward side has completed layers up to depth \(k\) and the backward side up to \(j\), and none has met. Any path of length \(L\le k+j\) contains a vertex with \(d_s(v)\le k\) and \(d_t(v)\le j\): take the vertex \(\min(k,L)\) edges along it. That vertex would already carry both labels. So the shortest path has length at least \(k+j+1\). Expanding layer \(k+1\) finds every path of length exactly \(k+j+1\): each passes through an edge from forward layer \(k\) to a vertex at backward depth \(j\). The first edge found in the layer need not be the best, since it may reach a backward vertex at depth \(j\) while another edge reaches one at depth \(j-1\). That is why the layer is finished and the minimum taken.

    The rule matters in practice. The library's earlier bidirectional_search shared one queue between the two sides and stopped at the first edge that touched. On 3,000 random graphs it returned a path that was not valid in 9% of cases. It now calls bidirectional_bfs, and the tests check it against plain BFS on 3,000 random graphs, directed and undirected.

    Weighted graphs

    algorithms::bidirectional_dijkstra runs Dijkstra forward from \(s\) and backward from \(t\), each time advancing the side whose heap top \(k_s\) or \(k_t\) is smaller. Whenever it scans an edge \(u\to v\) with weight \(w\) and \(v\) has a label on the other side, it updates

    \[\mu=\min\big(\mu,\;d_s(u)+w+d_t(v)\big).\]

    It stops as soon as \(k_s+k_t\ge\mu\). The proof is a one-liner. A path not yet seen joins a vertex unsettled on the forward side to one unsettled on the backward side, so its length is at least \(k_s+k_t\ge\mu\).

    The natural alternative, stopping at the first vertex settled by both sides and returning the path through it, is wrong. Take \(s\) and \(t\) joined directly by an edge of length 5, and also through \(x\) by two edges of length 3. Both searches settle \(x\) at distance 3 before either settles the other terminal at 5, so \(x\) is the first vertex settled twice, and the path through it costs 6. The rule above finds the direct edge, because scanning \(s\)'s edges already set \(\mu=5\). One of the library tests encodes this example.

    A graph with fast growth: handwritten digits

    dm::datasets::digits() holds 1,797 images of handwritten digits as 64 pixel intensities. We join every image to its 10 nearest neighbours (geometry::knn_lists) and ignore edge direction. The result is a sparse graph in which a few hops connect any image to similar ones, and longer paths cross between digits. We measure ball growth from 50 random centres and run plain BFS and bidirectional BFS between 400 random pairs.

    The digit graph grows very fast near each vertex. One hop reaches 14 neighbours on average, because every image keeps its own 10 nearest and is also kept by others. Two hops reach 71 and three 183. The growth factor then falls as balls start to fill the 1,797 vertices, and ten hops reach almost everything. This is the exponential regime, cut short by the finite graph, and bidirectional search pays off accordingly. Both searches agree on every distance, and the bidirectional one expands 11 to 20 times fewer vertices for pairs 2 to 4 hops apart, and still 4 to 5 times fewer at 9 to 10 hops.

    The model columns predict the work from the mean ball sizes alone. BFS expands everything closer than the goal and, on average, half of the goal's layer. The bidirectional search expands \(B(a)\) around one end and \(B(b-1)\) around the other with \(a+b=d-1\). The model tracks the measurements up to 8 hops. For more distant pairs the measured saving is larger than the model predicts. Pairs that far apart lie at the edge of the graph, where balls are smaller than average, and always expanding the smaller frontier exploits the side that grows slowly. The model also explains why the ratio falls with distance. The saving \(|B(d)|/2|B(d/2)|\) needs \(B(d)\) to keep growing, and here it stops growing at the size of the graph.

    A graph with slow growth: walking on a volcano

    dm::datasets::volcano() gives the heights of Maunga Whau on an \(87\times61\) grid with 10 m spacing. algorithms::terrain_from_frame and terrain_edges turn it into a directed graph with an edge to each of the 8 neighbouring cells. An edge costs its length plus 8.3 m for every metre climbed (Naismith's rule), so climbing costs more than descending and the graph really is directed. This is a planar graph, so \(D=2\) and the predicted saving is about 2.

    Settled cells are those within cost 1290.9 of the start for Dijkstra (4449 cells), and within 645.3 of the start or 645.3 of the goal for the bidirectional search; 645.3 + 645.3 = 1290.6 \(\ge\) the optimum.

    Over 300 random pairs, all with equal costs from both searches: Dijkstra settles 2554 cells on average and bidirectional Dijkstra 1964, a ratio of 1.30. Per pair, the ratio has median 1.31 and quartiles 1.09 and 1.80; it is below 1 for 47 pairs. For the 64 pairs with optimal cost under 300 the median ratio is 2.04, and for the 61 above 900 it is 1.15.

    On the route across the cone the two searches find the same cost, 1,290.9, but the bidirectional one settles slightly more cells: 4,508 against 4,449. Alternating by the smaller heap top kept both radii at 645.3, half the optimum, as the stopping rule requires. The two half-cost regions are not smaller than the one full-cost region, because the full-cost region is clipped by the map. A one-directional search to cost 1,290.9 already covers 84% of the 5,307 cells, so it cannot grow any further, while the two half-searches have room to grow into corners that the route never visits.

    Over 300 random pairs, bidirectional Dijkstra saves 23% of the work on average (a ratio of 1.30). The ratio depends on the route length, as the planar model predicts. For the 64 pairs with optimal cost under 300, whose searches stay inside the map, the median ratio is 2.04, exactly the \(2^{D-1}\) of a two-dimensional graph. For the 61 pairs above 900 it is 1.15, and in 47 pairs the bidirectional search does more work. Uphill costs make the regions of equal cost lopsided, which probably explains why the ratios for short routes spread between 1 and 3.

    Summary

    • Searching from both ends replaces one ball of radius \(d\) by two of radius \(d/2\). With exponential growth \(b^r\) the work drops from \(b^d\) to \(2b^{d/2}\). With polynomial growth \(r^D\) the saving is a constant \(2^{D-1}\).
    • Exactness needs the right stopping rule. BFS must finish the layer in which the frontiers first touch, and Dijkstra must continue until \(k_s+k_t\ge\mu\), returning the best path \(\mu\) seen across any scanned edge, not the path through the first vertex settled twice. The library's earlier bidirectional_search stopped at the first contact and returned invalid paths for 9% of random graphs. It now uses bidirectional_bfs.
    • On the digit graph, where balls grow 15-fold and then 5-fold per hop, bidirectional BFS expands 4 to 20 times fewer vertices, and a model built only from mean ball sizes predicts the saving.
    • On the volcano grid the saving is the planar factor 2 for routes that stay inside the map. It disappears for routes across it, because the map boundary limits the single large search more than the two small ones.
    • algorithms::bidirectional_bfs, bidirectional_dijkstra, the baselines bfs_pair_search and dijkstra_pair_search, and ball_sizes are in <datamunge/algorithms/bidirectional_search.hpp>. All of them report their work counters.
