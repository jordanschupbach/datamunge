    Bayesian Optimization: Minimising Expensive Functions

    Abstract

    How can a function be minimised when each evaluation is so expensive that only a few dozen are affordable? Bayesian optimization models the function as a Gaussian process. It fits the process's length scale by maximising the marginal likelihood after every evaluation, and it evaluates next where the expected improvement over the best value so far is largest. This report derives the posterior, the profile likelihood and the expected improvement, and works a two-observation example by hand. The library's posterior agrees with scikit-learn to ten digits. On functions drawn from the prior, the posterior's 95% bands cover 95% of the truth when the hyperparameters are known. With hyperparameters fitted from five points they cover only 75%, and 94% with forty. On the Branin function, with 40 evaluations, expected improvement with a fitted length scale gets within 0.01 of the minimum in 92% of 25 runs. Random search and a particle swarm never do, and expected improvement with a badly fixed length scale rarely does. Tuning the two hyperparameters of a kernel ridge regression on the diabetes data, 30 evaluations come within 0.05 of the leave-one-out error of a 900-point grid in every run.

    The problem

    We want the minimum of a function \(f\) on a box \([a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}^d\) when each evaluation is expensive: a simulation that runs for an hour, a laboratory experiment, or the cross-validated error of a model that takes minutes to train. Only a few dozen evaluations are affordable. No gradient is available, and \(f\) may have several local minima. Methods that need thousands of evaluations, such as particle swarms, evolution strategies or a fine grid, are out of reach, and a coarse grid wastes most of its evaluations in regions that are clearly poor.

    Bayesian optimization treats the unknown \(f\) as a random function. After each evaluation it updates a probabilistic model of \(f\) (the surrogate), and it chooses the next point by trading off a low predicted value against high uncertainty. The surrogate costs little compared with an evaluation of \(f\), so all the effort goes into choosing where to evaluate.

    The method

    A Gaussian-process prior

    The surrogate is a Gaussian process (GP): any finite set of values \(f(x_1),\dots,f(x_n)\) is jointly normal with mean \(m\) and covariance given by the squared-exponential kernel

    \[k(x,x')=s\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}\Big).\]

    The signal variance \(s\) sets how far \(f\) strays from \(m\), and the length scale \(\ell\) sets how far apart two points must be before their values are nearly unrelated. Draws from this prior are smooth, infinitely differentiable functions that wiggle on the scale \(\ell\). Observations \(y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i\) carry noise of variance \(\sigma_n^2\); for a deterministic \(f\) this is a tiny nugget that keeps the matrices well conditioned.

    The posterior

    Write \(K\) for the \(n\times n\) matrix \(k(x_i,x_j)\), \(k_*\) for the vector \(k(x_*,x_i)\) and \(A=K+\sigma_n^2I\). Conditioning the joint normal distribution of \((y,f(x_*))\) on \(y\) gives

    \[\mu(x_*)=m+k_*^\top A^{-1}(y-m\mathbf 1),\qquad \sigma^2(x_*)=s-k_*^\top A^{-1}k_*.\]

    RBFGaussianProcessSurrogate::fit() factors \(A=LL^\top\) once and solves for \(\alpha=A^{-1}(y-m\mathbf 1)\). predict() then costs one forward substitution per point: the mean is \(m+k_*^\top\alpha\), and the variance is \(s-\lVert z\rVert^2\) with \(Lz=k_*\). The posterior interpolates exact observations (\(\sigma\to0\) at an observed point as \(\sigma_n\to0\)) and returns to the prior \(N(m,s)\) far from all of them.

    With normalize_values the values are first centred at their mean and divided by their standard deviation. The prior mean \(m\) is then the sample mean, and \(s\) is measured in units of the sample variance, so the same hyperparameter ranges suit any objective.

    As a check, take six points with \(\ell=0.6\), \(s=1.3\) and \(\sigma_n^2=0.01\), with normalisation. The posterior means, standard deviations and log marginal likelihood agree to ten digits with scikit-learn's GaussianProcessRegressor (kernel ConstantKernel * RBF + WhiteKernel, normalize_y=True). With the length scale fitted, both give \(\ell=0.513\) and the same maximised likelihood.

    Hyperparameters by marginal likelihood

    The prior's \(\ell\) matters more than anything else: too short and the model forgets each observation within a short distance, too long and it smooths over the minima. Rather than guess it, fit_hyperparameters maximises the log marginal likelihood of the observations,

    \[\log p(y\mid\ell,s)=-\tfrac12(y-m)^\top A^{-1}(y-m)-\tfrac12\log\lvert A\rvert-\tfrac n2\log2\pi .\]

    The first term rewards fit and the second penalises complexity: a short length scale makes \(A\) close to diagonal, with a large determinant. With the nugget held as a fixed ratio \(g=\sigma_n^2/s\), write \(A=s(R+gI)\). Setting the derivative in \(s\) to zero gives \(\hat s=(y-m)^\top(R+gI)^{-1}(y-m)/n\), and the profile likelihood

    \[\log p(y\mid\ell,\hat s)=-\tfrac n2\log(2\pi\hat s)-\tfrac n2-\tfrac12\log\lvert R+gI\rvert\]

    depends on \(\ell\) alone. It is maximised over a 40-point log grid from 0.01 to 10 times the widest coordinate range of the data, then refined by golden-section search. With fit_noise, the ratio \(g\) is searched as well, from \(10^{-8}\) to 1. log_marginal_likelihood() reports the result for the raw values, including the \(-n\log(\text{scale})\) Jacobian of the normalisation.

    Acquisition functions

    Let \(f^\star\) be the smallest value observed so far (the incumbent) and \(Y\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) the posterior at a candidate \(x\). The improvement is \(\max(f^\star-Y,0)\). Writing \(Y=\mu+\sigma Z\) and \(u=(f^\star-\mu)/\sigma\),

    \[\operatorname{EI}(x)=\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{u}(u-z)\varphi(z)\,dz=(f^\star-\mu)\Phi(u)+\sigma\varphi(u),\]

    using \(\int_{-\infty}^u z\varphi(z)\,dz=-\varphi(u)\). Expected improvement (EI) grows when the predicted mean is low (the first term, exploitation) and when the uncertainty is high (the second term, exploration); it is zero only where \(\sigma=0\) and \(\mu\ge f^\star\). Two simpler criteria are available through GaussianProcessSurrogateOptions::acquisition:

    • probability of improvement, \(\Phi(u)\), which ignores how large the improvement would be and so tends to take many tiny steps near the incumbent;
    • lower confidence bound, \(-(\mu-\kappa\sigma)\), the most optimistic plausible value; \(\kappa=0\) is greedy minimisation of the posterior mean, with no exploration at all.

    exploration (\(\xi\)) shifts the target of EI and PI to \(f^\star-\xi\).

    The loop

    BayesianOptimization::run() evaluates initial_samples uniform random points. Then, for max_iterations rounds, it refits the surrogate to all observations, scores candidates uniform random points by the acquisition, polishes the best one by compass search on the acquisition surface (steps of 10% of each side, halved refine_iterations times), and evaluates \(f\) there. The result keeps every point, value, running minimum and acquisition value. Each fit costs \(O(n^3)\) for the factorisation, repeated 80 times or so when fitting \(\ell\); with \(n\le100\) this is a few milliseconds, negligible next to an expensive \(f\).

    Simple examples

    Two observations by hand

    Fix \(\ell=1\), \(s=1\), \(m=0\) and no noise (the two-argument constructor), and observe \(f(0)=1\), \(f(2)=-1\). Then \(K=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{-2}\\e^{-2}&1\end{pmatrix}\). At \(x_*=1\), \(k_*=e^{-1/2}(1,1)\). By antisymmetry the mean is 0, and

    \[\sigma^2(1)=1-\frac{2e^{-1}}{1+e^{-2}}=0.35195,\qquad \sigma(1)=0.5933 .\]

    With incumbent \(f^\star=-1\), \(u=-1/0.5933=-1.6855\), and \(\operatorname{EI}(1)=-\Phi(-1.6855)+0.5933\,\varphi(1.6855)=-0.04594+0.05717=0.01123\).

    The three criteria disagree about where to go next. Expected improvement is largest at \(x=3\) (0.184), just beyond the better observation, where the mean is low (−0.69) and the uncertainty is substantial (0.79). It still values \(x=5\), where nothing is known, at 0.085, and between the two observations at \(x=0.5\) it is essentially zero, because a mean of 0.65 is unlikely to beat −1. Probability of improvement gives its largest value, 0.5, to \(x=2\), the incumbent itself, where there is nothing to gain. There the mean equals the incumbent and rounding leaves a standard deviation of about \(10^{-8}\), so \(u\) is 0/0. This shows PI's weakness: it rewards any chance of improvement, however small the gain.

    Checking the theory

    Calibration on functions drawn from the prior

    If \(f\) really is a draw from the GP prior and the hyperparameters are known, the posterior is exact: \((f(x)-\mu(x))/\sigma(x)\) is standard normal at every unobserved point, and the 95% band \(\mu\pm1.96\sigma\) covers \(f\) 95% of the time. With \(\ell\) and \(s\) estimated from the same few points, the plug-in posterior ignores their uncertainty and should cover less. Here \(f\) is drawn from the prior with \(\ell=0.1\), \(s=1\), \(m=0\) on a 300-point grid on \([0,1]\), observed exactly at \(n\) random grid points (300 draws for each \(n\)). The posterior is computed twice: with the true hyperparameters, and with normalisation and fitted \(\ell\), as the optimizer uses it.

    With the true hyperparameters, the coverage is 0.950 to 0.957 and the mean of \(z^2\) is 0.94 to 1.02 at every \(n\), as the theory requires. This confirms that the posterior mean and variance are computed correctly. (The simulated functions include the \(10^{-8}\) nugget at every grid point, so it is part of the predictive variance here.) With fitted hyperparameters, the plug-in bands are too narrow when data are scarce: 75% coverage with five points, 85% with ten, 90% with twenty and 94% with forty. The mean of \(z^2\) is 31.8 at \(n=5\), dominated by draws where the fitted length scale is far too long and the posterior is confidently wrong. The fitted length scale has a median of 0.087 at \(n=5\) with quartiles 0.048 and 0.122; by \(n=20\) the quartiles are 0.097 and 0.104 around the true 0.1.

    For optimization, this means the early iterations rely on a surrogate whose uncertainty is understated. EI then explores less than it should. Refitting at every step corrects this as data accumulate, but very small initial designs are risky.

    Fitted versus fixed length scale. With \(\ell\) fitted by marginal likelihood, EI reaches a median regret of 0.0018 after 40 evaluations and gets within 0.01 of the minimum in 23 of 25 runs (92%). Fixed at 15, the surrogate smooths over the three minima, and no run succeeds. A good fixed value exists, but it has to be found by trial, which costs evaluations. Fitting finds it from the data.

    Baselines. Random search reaches a median regret of 0.91 after 40 evaluations and never comes within 0.01. The particle swarm, an excellent method with thousands of evaluations, is no better than random search at this budget (median 0.26): 40 evaluations are five generations of eight particles.

    Tuning kernel ridge regression on the diabetes data

    A real use: choose the two hyperparameters of an RBF kernel ridge regression of disease progression on the ten baseline variables of the diabetes data (Efron et al., 2004; 442 patients). The objective is the exact leave-one-out root mean squared error (KernelRidgeRegression::rmse() with lambda and gamma fixed) as a function of \(\log_{10}\lambda\in[-4,2]\) and \(\log_{10}\gamma\in[-4,0]\). Each evaluation fits the model to all 442 rows. A 30 × 30 grid (900 evaluations) gives the reference minimum. Bayesian optimization (EI, fitted length scale, 6 random points and 24 iterations) and random search each get 30 evaluations, from 10 seeds.

    The 900-point grid finds a minimum leave-one-out RMSE of 54.0989 at \(\log_{10}\lambda=-0.10\), \(\log_{10}\gamma=-1.80\). The error surface is a curved valley: a wider kernel (smaller \(\gamma\)) needs less regularisation. With 30 evaluations, Bayesian optimization finds a best RMSE between 54.0985 and 54.1401 (median 54.104) over the ten seeds. One run, seed 203, ends slightly below the grid minimum, because it is not restricted to the grid. Random search with the same budget gets 54.138 to 54.229 (median 54.177) and does worse than Bayesian optimization on every seed. In the right panel the median excess over the grid minimum falls to 0.005 for Bayesian optimization and levels off near 0.08 for random search. In the left panel, the EI proposals of one run follow the valley to its lower end and cluster around the minimum; a few test the edges of the box.

    Two caveats. First, the differences are tiny compared with the RMSE itself: with 442 patients the leave-one-out RMSE has a sampling error of the order of 2 (about \(\text{RMSE}/\sqrt{2n}=1.8\) for normal errors). So anything in the dark valley is an equally good model, and here the optimizer mainly saves evaluations. Second, this objective has structure that a black-box method ignores. Given \(\gamma\), the leave-one-out error for every \(\lambda\) comes from a single eigendecomposition. KernelRidgeRegression's built-in selection uses this, sweeping 40 values of \(\lambda\) for each value of \(\gamma\) on its own grid, and it reaches 54.1171. Bayesian optimization is for objectives without such shortcuts.

    Conclusion

    Bayesian optimization spends computation to save evaluations. After each evaluation, it refits a Gaussian process and maximises an acquisition function over the box, then makes one evaluation where the model expects the most gain. The posterior is exact for functions drawn from the prior. Its length scale should be fitted by marginal likelihood at every step, not fixed. On Branin, fixed values of 0.5 and 15 made the method little better than random search, while fitting matched or beat the best fixed value without trial runs. Expected improvement balances a low mean against high uncertainty and was the most reliable criterion here. Probability of improvement over-exploits, and the greedy posterior mean is fast but stalls in some runs.

    The limits are as important. With very few points the fitted length scale is unreliable: three points gave 0.019, and five points gave bands that covered 75% instead of 95%. The initial design should not be tiny. The kernel is isotropic and the cost of a fit grows as \(n^3\), so the method suits low-dimensional boxes, inputs on comparable scales and budgets of tens to a few hundred evaluations. When an objective is cheap, or has exploitable structure, such as the eigenbasis trick for kernel ridge regression, a direct method will do better.
