    The Frank-Wolfe Algorithm

    Abstract

    Many estimation problems minimise a smooth convex function over a constraint set: weights that must be nonnegative and sum to one, coefficients with a bounded \(\ell_1\) norm, flows in a network. Projected gradient methods must project onto the set at every step, which can cost as much as the problem itself. The Frank-Wolfe algorithm (1956) needs only a linear minimisation over the set, which for these sets is a single scan for the best vertex. We derive its two key facts: a free certificate \(g_k\ge f(x_k)-f^\star\), and the rate \(f(x_k)-f^\star\le2C/(k+2)\). On a triangle we watch it zig-zag when the optimum lies on an edge, and watch the away-step and pairwise variants stop in four and three steps. On least squares over a 40-dimensional simplex, the vanilla method is still \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-2}\) from optimal after 2,000 steps, and exact line search makes it slower, while the variants reach \(10^{-12}\) within about 100 steps. Finally, the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimate of a Gaussian mixture for Old Faithful's waiting times is a Frank-Wolfe problem over the simplex: the pairwise variant finds a 7-atom solution with a certified gap of \(10^{-6}\), where EM keeps 11 weights above \(10^{-8}\).

    The problem

    Let \(C\subset\mathbb R^d\) be compact and convex, and \(f\) convex and differentiable with an \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient, so that

    \[f(y)\le f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac L2\lVert y-x\rVert^2\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in C.\]

    We want \(f^\star=\min_{x\in C}f(x)\). Projected gradient takes a gradient step and then finds the nearest point of \(C\), itself a quadratic programme for most sets. Frank and Wolfe (1956) replace the projection by a linear minimisation oracle (LMO), which minimises a linear function over \(C\). A linear function on a polytope attains its minimum at a vertex, and for the common sets the LMO is a scan:

    • the probability simplex \(\Delta_d=\{x\ge0,\ \sum_ix_i=1\}\): \(\min_{s\in\Delta_d}\langle g,s\rangle=\min_ig_i\), attained at the unit vector \(e_{i^\star}\) (simplex_lmo);
    • the \(\ell_1\) ball of radius \(r\): \(-r\,\operatorname{sign}(g_{i^\star})e_{i^\star}\) with \(i^\star=\arg\max_i|g_i|\) (l1_ball_lmo);
    • a polytope given by its vertices: the vertex with the smallest \(\langle g,v\rangle\) (vertex_lmo).

    Projecting onto the simplex needs a sort, and projecting onto the convex hull of a list of vertices is a quadratic programme. The LMOs above cost \(O(d)\) and \(O(dV)\).

    The method

    One step

    At \(x_k\), let \(g=\nabla f(x_k)\), ask the oracle for \(s_k=\arg\min_{s\in C}\langle g,s\rangle\), and move towards it:

    \[x_{k+1}=x_k+\gamma_k\,(s_k-x_k),\qquad\gamma_k\in[0,1].\]

    The new iterate is a convex combination of points of \(C\), so it stays feasible with no projection. Three step rules are implemented: the standard \(\gamma_k=2/(k+2)\), the short step \(\gamma_k=\min\{1,\ g_k/(L\lVert s_k-x_k\rVert^2)\}\) that minimises the quadratic upper bound, and an exact line search on \(f\).

    A free certificate

    Define the Frank-Wolfe gap \(g_k=\langle\nabla f(x_k),x_k-s_k\rangle\ge0\).

    Lemma 1. \(f(x_k)-f^\star\le g_k\).

    Proof. By convexity, for the minimiser \(x^\star\), \(f^\star\ge f(x_k)+\langle\nabla f(x_k),x^\star-x_k\rangle\ge f(x_k)+\min_{s\in C}\langle\nabla f(x_k),s-x_k\rangle=f(x_k)-g_k\). \(\blacksquare\)

    The oracle call that chooses the step also bounds the error, so Frank-Wolfe can stop when \(g_k\le\varepsilon\) and know that it is within \(\varepsilon\) of optimal. Projected gradient has no such certificate.

    The \(O(1/k)\) rate

    Let \(D=\max_{x,y\in C}\lVert x-y\rVert\) be the diameter and \(C_f=LD^2\). Write \(h_k=f(x_k)-f^\star\).

    Theorem. With \(\gamma_k=2/(k+2)\), \(h_k\le 2C_f/(k+2)\) for all \(k\ge1\). Short steps and line search do at least as well.

    Proof. Apply the smoothness bound with \(y=x_{k+1}\) and use \(g_k\ge h_k\):

    \[h_{k+1}\le h_k-\gamma_kg_k+\frac{\gamma_k^2}2LD^2\le(1-\gamma_k)h_k+\frac{\gamma_k^2}2C_f .\]

    For \(k=0\), \(\gamma_0=1\) gives \(h_1\le C_f/2\). If \(h_k\le2C_f/(k+2)\), then

    \[h_{k+1}\le\frac k{k+2}\cdot\frac{2C_f}{k+2}+\frac{2C_f}{(k+2)^2}=\frac{2C_f(k+1)}{(k+2)^2}\le\frac{2C_f}{k+3},\]

    since \((k+1)(k+3)\le(k+2)^2\). The short step minimises the middle expression over \(\gamma\) and line search minimises \(f\) itself, so each makes at least as much progress as \(\gamma_k=2/(k+2)\) does in the same inequality. \(\blacksquare\)

    Starting from a vertex, \(x_k\) is a convex combination of at most \(k+1\) vertices. Over the simplex this means at most \(k+1\) nonzero coordinates, so Frank-Wolfe produces sparse approximate solutions with a known accuracy.

    Why the rate is not better, and the cure

    The vanilla method does not converge linearly, even when \(f\) is strongly convex. Canon and Cullum (1968) showed that with exact line search, when the minimiser lies inside a face of a polytope and is not a vertex, \(h_k\) decreases more slowly than \(1/k^{1+\delta}\) for every \(\delta>0\). The open-loop step \(2/(k+2)\) can do somewhat better in this situation, up to \(O(1/k^2)\) (Wirth, Kerdreux and Pokutta 2023), but not linearly. The mechanism is visible in the update: a vertex that entered early and is not on the optimal face keeps its weight, multiplied by \((1-\gamma_k)\) at each step, and the only way to shrink it is to step towards other vertices. The iterate then zig-zags between the vertices of the optimal face.

    The away-step variant (Wolfe 1970) remembers the active set \(S_k\) of vertices with their weights \(\alpha_v\), and also considers the away direction \(x_k-v_k\), where \(v_k=\arg\max_{v\in S_k}\langle\nabla f(x_k),v\rangle\) is the worst active vertex. It takes whichever direction descends faster, with a step of at most \(\alpha_{v}/(1-\alpha_v)\) along the away direction; a full away step drops \(v_k\). The pairwise variant moves weight straight from \(v_k\) to \(s_k\), along \(s_k-v_k\), with a step of at most \(\alpha_{v_k}\). Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi (2015) proved that both converge linearly for strongly convex \(f\) over a polytope, \(h_{k+1}\le(1-\rho)h_k\) on all but a bounded fraction of steps, with \(\rho\) depending on the condition number and the polytope's geometry.

    algorithms::frank_wolfe implements all three variants on top of any LMO. Equal vertices from the oracle are recognised as the same atom, so the active set is exact.

    Simple examples

    A triangle with the optimum on an edge

    Minimise \(f(x)=\tfrac12\lVert x-c\rVert^2\) with \(c=(0.7,0.7)\) over the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). The minimiser is the projection of \(c\) onto the hypotenuse, \(x^\star=(0.5,0.5)\), with \(f^\star=0.04\): it lies inside an edge, the case where vanilla Frank-Wolfe is slow. Here \(L=1\) and \(D^2=2\), so \(C_f=2\). All runs start at \((0,0)\) and use exact line search.

    Vanilla Frank-Wolfe is still \(2.4\times10^{-4}\) from optimal after 1,000 steps, with every step a forward one. The two variants are exact to rounding after four and three steps.

    One drop step, by hand

    The away-step run can be checked by hand. From \(x_0=(0,0)\) the gradient is \((-0.7,-0.7)\), both other vertices tie, and the oracle returns \((1,0)\); line search along \((1,0)\) stops at \(x_1=(0.7,0)\). Next the oracle returns \((0,1)\), and line search gives a point inside the triangle, with the active set \(\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}\). Now the worst active vertex is \((0,0)\), where \(\langle\nabla f,v\rangle\) is largest, and moving away from it descends faster than moving towards a vertex. The full away step removes all of its weight, which puts the iterate on the hypotenuse: that is the drop. One more forward step along the hypotenuse lands on \(x^\star\). Vanilla Frank-Wolfe never removes the weight on \((0,0)\), so it can only approach the edge.

    Checking the theory

    Least squares over a simplex

    A larger test: \(f(x)=\tfrac12\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2\) over \(\Delta_{40}\), with \(A\) an \(80\times40\) standard normal matrix and \(b\) standard normal. With more rows than columns \(f\) is strongly convex, and the minimiser has 14 nonzero coordinates, so it lies inside a face of the simplex. Here \(L=\lambda_{\max}(A^\top A)\) (found by power iteration) and \(D^2=2\), so the theorem promises \(h_k\le4L/(k+2)\). The reference \(f^\star\) is the pairwise variant run to a gap of \(10^{-13}\).

    \(L=218.8\), \(f^\star=32.006462\) with 14 nonzero coordinates (reference: 141 pairwise steps). Violations of Lemma 1 or of the theorem's bound over all 8,000 iterates: 0.

    Every one of the 8,000 iterates obeys both Lemma 1 and the theorem's bound, which is loose by three to four orders of magnitude here. Between \(k=100\) and \(2{,}000\) the line-search errors fall with log-log slope about \(-0.8\), slower than \(1/k\), as Canon and Cullum's result predicts. The standard step does better, slope about \(-1.8\), in line with the faster open-loop rates of Wirth, Kerdreux and Pokutta. So line search made vanilla Frank-Wolfe slower: after 2,000 steps it is 180 times further from optimal. The theorem only promises that line search is no worse in the bound, not in practice. The away-step and pairwise errors fall on straight lines in the right panel, the linear convergence of Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi, reaching \(10^{-12}\) after 101 and 63 steps.

    A dataset example: the nonparametric MLE of a mixture

    dm::datasets::faithful() records 272 eruptions of the Old Faithful geyser; we model the waiting time before each eruption, in minutes, which is bimodal. A Gaussian location mixture with known \(\sigma\) has density \(\sum_j\pi_j\varphi_\sigma(x-\mu_j)\). The Kiefer-Wolfowitz nonparametric maximum likelihood estimate (NPMLE) leaves the mixing distribution free: fix a fine grid of locations \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_J\), and choose the weights \(\pi\in\Delta_J\) to maximise the likelihood. With \(\varphi_{ij}=\varphi_\sigma(x_i-\mu_j)\) and \(m_i(\pi)=\sum_j\pi_j\varphi_{ij}\) we minimise

    \[f(\pi)=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log m_i(\pi),\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial\pi_j}=-D_j(\pi),\quad D_j(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\varphi_{ij}}{m_i(\pi)} .\]

    This is convex over the simplex, so Frank-Wolfe applies with simplex_lmo. Since \(\sum_j\pi_jD_j(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_im_i/m_i=1\), the Frank-Wolfe gap is

    \[g(\pi)=\langle\nabla f(\pi),\pi-e_{j^\star}\rangle=\max_jD_j(\pi)-1 ,\]

    and Lemma 1 becomes Lindsay's (1983) optimality condition: \(\pi\) is the NPMLE exactly when \(D_j(\pi)\le1\) for every \(j\). The classical EM algorithm for the weights is \(\pi_j\leftarrow\pi_jD_j(\pi)\), which keeps the weights on the simplex by the same identity. EM never sets a weight to zero, while the NPMLE is known to be discrete with few atoms (Lindsay 1983). Frank-Wolfe with line search is the "vertex direction method" of this literature, and the pairwise variant is Böhning's (1986) "vertex exchange method".

    algorithms::gaussian_location_npmle starts from equal weights on every grid point, each its own atom, so that away and pairwise steps can drop grid points; gaussian_location_em runs EM from the same start. We use \(\sigma=5\) minutes, a little below the spread within each of the two clusters, a grid from 40 to 100 minutes in steps of one, a tolerance of \(10^{-6}\) on the gap and at most 20,000 iterations.

    All three converged methods agree on the log-likelihood to \(10^{-5}\), and the two that certify a gap below \(10^{-6}\) with an exact active set agree to all six decimals: \(-1032.391418\). Neither vanilla run reaches the tolerance in 20,000 steps. The line-search version is the vertex direction method, whose slowness is the reason Böhning proposed the exchange method; it is also stuck with every one of the 61 starting weights, since vanilla steps can only shrink them by factors \((1-\gamma)\). The standard step jumps to a single grid point at its first step (\(\gamma_0=1\)), so it starts sparse, but it still collects 24 points.

    The pairwise solution has seven atoms in four groups: 52 and 53, 62 and 63, 79 and 80, and 86 minutes. Adjacent pairs are the grid's way of representing an atom that lies between two grid points, so the mixing distribution has about four support points: the two clusters, each with a shoulder. EM reaches the same likelihood within \(10^{-5}\) but leaves 11 weights above \(10^{-8}\), because its multiplicative update shrinks unused weights geometrically and never sets them to zero. EM's iterations are cheaper than pairwise ones, which need a line search, so EM's 4.5 times as many iterations do not make it slower in proportion. The difference is the answer: pairwise Frank-Wolfe returns the sparse solution itself, with a certificate.

    Conclusion

    Frank-Wolfe replaces projection with a linear minimisation, which for simplices, \(\ell_1\) balls and vertex lists is a scan. The same oracle call gives a gap that bounds the error (Lemma 1), and the standard step guarantees \(f(x_k)-f^\star\le2C_f/(k+2)\). Both held on every iterate we checked. The rate is also the method's limit: when the optimum lies inside a face, the vanilla method zig-zags and stays sublinear, and exact line search did not help, in two experiments it made things worse. The away-step and pairwise variants fix this by keeping the active vertices and moving weight off the bad ones. They reached machine precision in tens of steps on the triangle and least-squares problems, and produced a sparse, certified NPMLE on the Old Faithful data.

    Limits: the guarantees need a smooth objective. The mixture likelihood is not globally smooth near the boundary of the simplex, which is why its vanilla runs are so slow. The away and pairwise variants need the active set, which costs memory proportional to the number of vertices used. And the LMO must be cheap: over sets where linear minimisation is as hard as projection, the method loses its advantage.
