    Gauss–Newton: Local Least Squares and Full-Step Limits

    Abstract

    Fitting a nonlinear curve means choosing parameters that make its predictions close to measured responses. A good starting guess can lead to an accurate fit in a few updates, while a poor one can make the error much larger. Gauss–Newton repeatedly solves a linear least-squares approximation without computing residual Hessians. We derive its direction, work through tiny exact examples, compare quadratic and linear local convergence, and show a full step that increases error dramatically. A three-parameter sinusoid retains the original report's generated-data check. NIST's observed 14-row adsorption dataset then provides certified parameter and SSE references. Analytic and scaled central-difference fits match those references; raw-rate differences reveal unit sensitivity. Local linear accuracy, numerical stopping, parameter identification and predictive validity are evaluated as separate questions.

    The method

    Linearize the residual vector

    Given coordinates \(x_i\), observed responses \(y_i\), model \(m(p,x)\) and parameter vector \(p\in\mathbb R^n\), define \(r_i(p)=m(p,x_i)-y_i\). For \(m\) observations, minimize the unweighted sum of squared residuals, or its half-scaled equivalent:

    \[S(p)=\sum_{i=1}^m r_i(p)^2,\quad \phi(p)=\tfrac12 S(p),\qquad \nabla\phi=J^Tr,\quad \nabla^2\phi=J^TJ+\sum_i r_i\nabla^2r_i, \quad J_{ij}=\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial p_j}.\]

    Full Newton uses both Hessian terms. Gauss–Newton replaces the residual function by its first-order approximation and solves

    \[\delta=\arg\min_d\|r+Jd\|_2^2,\qquad J^TJ\delta=-J^Tr,\qquad p_{k+1}=p_k+\delta_k.\]

    The normal equations describe the solution algebraically; the native implementation solves the rectangular problem by Householder QR. It first scales each nonzero Jacobian column to unit Euclidean norm, solves for a scaled step, then restores the original parameter units. This avoids explicitly forming \(J^TJ\), whose condition number is the square of that of a full-rank \(J\). Column normalization helps unit imbalance but cannot separate parallel columns or repair an uninformative experiment. Numerically rank-deficient scaled Jacobians are rejected explicitly.

    A descent direction can still produce an uphill full step

    For a full-column-rank Jacobian and a nonzero least-squares direction,

    \[\nabla\phi^T\delta=(J^Tr)^T\delta =-\delta^TJ^TJ\delta=-\|J\delta\|_2^2<0.\]

    This proves decrease for sufficiently small positive step lengths. It does not prove that the full multiplier one is sufficiently small. The basic routine takes full steps without a line search, trust region, damping, parameter bounds or acceptance test; increasing SSE remains visible in the trace. Levenberg–Marquardt modifies the local problem with damping, but that is a different algorithm and is not silently substituted here. This report concerns unweighted least squares; changing the loss or observation weights changes the problem.

    Local convergence depends on both rank and residual curvature

    Suppose \(p_*\) is a stationary solution with full-column-rank \(J_*\), and the residual Jacobian is sufficiently smooth nearby. For the exact-derivative iteration map,

    \[T'(p_*)=-(J_*^TJ_*)^{-1}C_*,\qquad C_*=\sum_i r_i(p_*)\nabla^2r_i(p_*).\]

    If the iteration derivative is a local contraction, convergence is locally linear. Small residuals help only when the Jacobian remains well-conditioned and residual curvature is controlled. At a zero-residual solution \(C_*=0\), giving local quadratic convergence under the usual smoothness and rank assumptions. A small residual alone is not a guarantee. Finite-difference errors and rounding eventually disturb these asymptotic rates. Nonzero residuals can also have \(C_*=0\); they do not invariably force a merely linear rate.

    Native contracts and numerical work

    gauss_newton(model,x,y,start,max_iter,tol) uses central finite differences. The analytic overload additionally accepts a callback returning one parameter-ordered Jacobian row. Coordinates, responses, parameters and model/derivative outputs must be finite; the input vectors are nonempty, lengths match, and observations must be at least as numerous as parameters. Missing values are rejected rather than omitted. Callbacks must return repeatable values and be defined at every visited point; callback exceptions propagate. Central differences also need both nearby perturbed parameters to remain in the domain. Their step is \(h_j=\sqrt[3]{\epsilon}\max(1,|p_j|)\), using the actual representable plus-minus span. Analytic derivatives avoid these perturbations and their truncation/cancellation error.

    The routine preserves the original call signature and the original params, iterations, sse result fields. gauss_newton_linearize() exposes the local residuals, Jacobian, proposed step, current gradient norm and predicted SSE. trace() records the initial state and every completed update. sse and residuals always correspond to the returned parameters, including when the update budget is exhausted. iterations counts completed updates; a zero budget evaluates only the initial state and its proposed next step.

    Stopping checks whether the proposed next-step norm is at most tol, a positive absolute tolerance in the supplied parameter units. step_tolerance_met names this specific event. A small step is not a proof of a small gradient, a minimum, unique parameters or a global optimum. Changing parameter units changes this stopping criterion even though the exact full least-squares direction transforms consistently. gradient_inf is the infinity norm of \(J^Tr\), the gradient of half-SSE in those units. This uses the supplied or approximate Jacobian: it is an approximate gradient with finite differences. The trace's step_norm describes the incoming completed update; next_step_norm and linearized_sse describe the proposed outgoing update at that row.

    After column normalization, QR uses rank threshold \(\max(m,n)\epsilon\max_j|R_{jj}|\). Its numerical-rank check concerns the local linearization, not global identification. A rank failure raises an exception even at a small residual. A nonfinite full-step evaluation is also reported as an error; there is no backtracking recovery.

    The existing QR implementation forms an explicit \(m\)-by-\(m\) orthogonal factor. Each evaluated state therefore costs \(O(mn^2+m^2n)\) linear-algebra work and \(O(m^2+mn)\) temporary storage, plus \(m(1+2n)\) scalar model calls for central finite differences, or \(m\) model and \(m\) analytic-row calls. The trace requires \(O((k+1)n)\) storage after \(k\) updates. A compact QR could avoid the explicit-Q cost for large datasets; the present implementation is suited to small dense fits.

    Simple examples

    Three exact points: linear least squares in one full update

    Let \(m(p,x)=p_0+p_1x\), coordinates \((-1,0,1)\), responses \((0,2,4)\), and start \((0,0)\). The residual is \((0,-2,-4)\), with Jacobian rows \((1,x_i)\). Therefore \(J^TJ=\operatorname{diag}(3,2)\) and \(-J^Tr=(6,4)\), so \(\delta=(2,2)\). The linearization is exact everywhere: one full update recovers the line, with zero residual in exact arithmetic. The trace evaluates the final state before reporting its SSE. Its proposed next step is essentially zero.

    Checking the theory

    Compare exact quadratic and linear error recurrences

    For the zero-residual square-root fit, the iteration is \(p_{k+1}=(p_k+2/p_k)/2\). With \(e_k=p_k-\sqrt2\), direct algebra gives \(e_{k+1}=e_k^2/(2p_k)\): the error is quadratic until rounding dominates.

    For a nonzero-residual example, fit model values \((p,p^2)\) at coordinates \((0,1)\) to observations \((0,1)\). The objective is \(S=p^2+(p^2-1)^2\), whose positive minimum is \(p_*=1/\sqrt2\) with \(S_*=3/4\). Its Jacobian is \((1,2p)^T\), and

    \[p_{k+1}=p_k-\frac{p_k(2p_k^2-1)}{1+4p_k^2},\qquad J_*^TJ_*=3,\quad C_*=-1,\quad T'(p_*)=\frac13.\]

    Both fits use exact derivatives and start at one. The first plot floors absolute parameter errors at 1e-16 only for logarithmic display. The second shows the nonzero case's observed error ratio before subtraction of nearly equal floating-point numbers makes that ratio unreliable. The native trace and printed errors are kept unfloored. These examples test explicit recurrences; they do not establish the rate for every nonlinear fit.

    Overshoot and local rank failure are different problems

    Fit \(p^2\) to one response one, starting from \(p=.1\). The direction is \((1-p^2)/(2p)=4.95\), so the full step reaches 5.05. The local linear residual is zero there, but the actual SSE jumps from .9801 to 600.37250625. The direction was a descent direction; the chosen full step was too long. Later full steps recover in this particular example, which is no guarantee for other starts or models.

    Starting at exactly zero is a different failure: the Jacobian vanishes, so the local least-squares direction is not identified. The native routine raises a rank error. Two identical Jacobian columns likewise prevent a unique full-rank step. Adding a stopping test or a more accurate linear solve does not resolve these cases.

    NIST Misra1a: observed data with a certified fit

    Model, units and starting values

    dm::datasets::misra1a() contains fourteen measured pressure/volume pairs from Misra's 1978 monomolecular-adsorption study, in NIST source order, with no missing values. The source does not specify measurement units, so we retain its numeric units without inventing labels. NIST uses the two-parameter saturation model

    \[y=A(1-e^{-b x})+\varepsilon,\qquad q=1000b,\quad m(A,q,x)=-A\,\operatorname{expm1}(-qx/1000),\qquad J=\left(1-e^{-qx/1000},\; A\frac{x}{1000}e^{-qx/1000}\right).\]

    Fitting \((A,q)\) avoids placing the tiny raw rate beside an amplitude near 239. The nonlinear model and unweighted objective are unchanged; the absolute parameter step tolerance is expressed in these chosen units. NIST's two starts become \((500,.1)\) and \((250,.5)\). The certified estimates are \(A=238.94212918\), \(b=.00055015643181\), with SSE .12455138894. Use all fourteen observations for numerical certification, with no test split and no parameter or model selection. Agreement with these reference numbers measures solver accuracy on this benchmark, not prediction on unseen experiments.

    The distant start can take an unphysical negative-amplitude intermediate step and greatly increase SSE. No positivity constraint or acceptance rule prevents that. Both analytic-start traces below are retained in full. Compare their final values with a central-difference fit from the second start. A stopped flag describes the specified step tolerance; inspect the gradient and certified objective alongside it.

    A fourth fit uses the raw rate b with default central differences. Here the fixed scale floor makes the finite-difference perturbation about 1.1% of b near the solution, versus about .0011% of q. Its amplitude differs by roughly 2.86e-5 from the certified value despite nearly identical fitted curves and SSE. QR column normalization cannot remove derivative truncation error already present in the Jacobian. Analytic derivatives or a suitable parameterization address it.

    Conclusion

    Gauss–Newton uses least-squares structure to obtain a local direction without residual Hessians. QR improves the numerical linear solve, while full-step overshoot and rank failure remain distinct nonlinear and identification concerns. The exact scalar recurrences show both quadratic and linear convergence under their stated conditions, and the generated sinusoid checks a multivariate parameter fit.

    NIST supplies observed data and certified parameter/SSE references for a direct numerical accuracy comparison. The scaled analytic fit returns A=238.94212917890, b=.00055015643181 and SSE=.12455138894441, matching the certified values to their reported precision. Default differences in raw rate units show a small derivative bias that becomes visible in the parameter table. The distant-start trace demonstrates why a successful final answer does not imply safe intermediate steps. Step-size stopping, low SSE, stationarity, unique parameters and predictive validity each require their own assessment. Difficult fits may need better parameterization, improved experimental information, or an explicitly safeguarded method.
