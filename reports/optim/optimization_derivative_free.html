    Derivative-Free Search: Simplexes and Covariance Adaptation

    Abstract

    A black-box objective may be inexpensive to evaluate but offer no usable derivatives. Search must then choose informative trial points and decide when its local geometry has stopped changing. We derive a hand-checkable Nelder–Mead step, compare simplex and covariance-adaptation searches on known quadratics, and retain local-basin and seed/population sensitivity on the original bumpy and Rastrigin objectives. Best observed trials are distinguished from global guarantees.

    The method

    Nelder–Mead stores p+one simplex vertices. It orders their values, computes the centroid of all but the worst, and reflects the worst through that centroid. A particularly good reflection can be expanded; an unsuccessful one can be contracted or followed by a simplex shrink. The initial displacement on axis j is initial_simplex_scale*max(one,abs(x0_j)), so the numerical starting point and units shape the initial search. The native stop test combines simplex diameter and objective spread; neither by itself proves stationarity. Simplex storage is O(p squared), while each comparison uses objective evaluations rather than gradients.

    The concept diagram below is drawn by dm::graphics::Diagram. Its editable C++ source is the complete block below; regeneration uses the same datamunge toolchain as the numerical examples. Exports consume the saved local SVG.

    CMA-ES samples x_i=m+sigma*B*D*z_i with standard-normal z and a covariance factor B*D. Ranked elites update m, evolution paths and covariance through weighted rank-one/rank-mu contributions; a separate path adapts sigma. In this dense implementation the default offspring count is four+floor(three*log(p)), six in two dimensions, and covariance eigensolves use numerical eigenvalue floors. It is unbounded and stochastic: covariance adaptation does not prevent a small population from collapsing into a local basin. Covariance storage is O(p squared), with dense eigensolve work in addition to population objective calls. Normal draws and seeded populations are reproducible in this build, not promised bit-identical across standard-library implementations.

    Small worked example

    One simplex iteration reaches a scalar quadratic minimum

    For f=(x-three)^2, start at zero and set initial_simplex_scale=one. Initial vertices zero and one have values nine and four. Their one-dimensional centroid excluding the worst is one. Reflection through it proposes two, value one; expansion proposes three, value zero, and wins. A single native iteration therefore returns three after four objective requests. This exact example checks the geometric update without using a derivative.

    Multimodal surfaces and local basins

    The bumpy surface is (x0-three)2+(x1+one)2+five*sin(x0)*cos(x1). It is bounded below by minus five, but the best value found by a finite search is not proved to be its global minimum. The figure uses this lower bound as its declared reference. Rastrigin is sum_j(x_j squared+ten*(one-cos(two*pi*x_j))); every term is nonnegative, so its global minimum zero at the origin is exact.

    Compare default simplex and seed-42 CMA-ES with seed-42 PSO on these two surfaces. The first start is (0,0), the second (3,-4). PSO spreads its 40 initial particles through a box, [-8,8] for bumpy and [-5.12,5.12] for Rastrigin; CMA starts with SD .5 around its mean, while the simplex is initially local. The domains, initial coverage, populations and budgets differ, so this illustrates search behaviour rather than a controlled universal ranking. Small best values and different basin endpoints are retained in the tables.

    Conclusions and references

    The one-step simplex calculation provides an exact implementation control. Rotated quadratics isolate geometry, while the bumpy and Rastrigin cases retain basin and initialization differences. The seed scan is complete but finite. Neither a collapsed simplex, a tiny CMA search scale, nor a best-trial envelope certifies a global optimum. These generated objectives need no external dataset; their exact minima or stated lower bounds make the limits of each claim visible.

    • J. A. Nelder and R. Mead (1965), [A Simplex Method for Function Minimization](https://doi.org/10.1093/comjnl/7.4.308).
    • N. Hansen and A. Ostermeier (2001), [Completely Derandomized Self-Adaptation in Evolution Strategies](https://doi.org/10.1162/106365601750190398).

    Reading the numerical evidence

    trace_optimize calls the configured native optimizer through its stated abstract interface. It records callback requests without inserting fitting evaluations. Value calls can be rejected trials; gradient calls can be look-ahead points. optimization_comparison explicitly re-evaluates selected points afterwards on these pure objectives. Those diagnostic calls are excluded from fitting counts. Callback indices are neither accepted iterations nor wall time. The plotted gap uses the declared reference (minimum or stated lower bound) and a display floor of 1e-14; that floor does not modify any optimizer update. Returned coordinates, checked objective and appropriate stationarity/feasibility checks carry more information than a return from a finite iteration budget. Native APIs do not expose a uniform stop-status flag. The recorder retains exceptions and rejects nonfinite requested values/gradients; this makes failures visible rather than letting an instrumented run continue on NaN.
