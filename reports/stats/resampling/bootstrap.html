    The Bootstrap: Standard Errors and Four Confidence Intervals

    Abstract

    The nonparametric bootstrap replaces an unknown distribution by the empirical distribution of the observations, then repeatedly samples from it with replacement. We show a small draw, check the mean against its exact conditional bootstrap variance, and retain the original iris Sepal.Length median experiment with 4,000 resamples. Native C++ computes the statistics, bias, standard error and percentile, basic, normal and BCa intervals. The final experiment separates Monte Carlo error from the difference between the bootstrap and textbook mean standard errors. Bootstrap validity depends on the statistic and sampling model; increasing the resample count cannot establish that validity.

    The empirical distribution and a possible draw

    For observations x1,...,xn, the empirical distribution puts mass 1/n on each row, including repeated values. An ordinary bootstrap sample consists of n independent draws from those rows with replacement. Evaluating the same statistic on each sample gives replicates theta-star1,...,theta-starB. This conditional distribution is used to approximate how the estimator would vary across new samples from the unknown population. Whether that substitution works requires assumptions beyond the computation itself.

    The diagram shows one possible index sequence for {1,2,4}: {0,2,2}. It is an illustration, rather than a trace of the random engine used below. Repeated selection of the third row is allowed, and the second row is absent. The program computes both statistics through the core API.

    Checking the theory on three observations

    Let s be the sample standard deviation with denominator n-1. Conditional on the observed sample, a single bootstrap draw has variance (n-1)s²/n. Averaging n independent such draws gives the exact identity

    \[\operatorname{Var}_*(\bar X^*)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2 =\frac{n-1}{n^2}s^2,\qquad \operatorname{se}_*(\bar X^*)=\frac{s\sqrt{n-1}}{n}.\]

    The usual estimated standard error s/sqrt(n) for a population mean is a different quantity. The bootstrap reference is smaller by sqrt((n-1)/n). For three observations the distinction is visible. The finite-B estimate is the sample standard deviation of the replicates and fluctuates around the conditional reference. No random seed can make it an exact identity.

    Bias, standard error and interval definitions

    Write theta-hat for the statistic on the original observations and m-star for the mean of the B replicates. The estimated bias is m-star minus theta-hat. The bootstrap SE uses the B-1 sample-variance denominator; B=1 is accepted with SE zero, although one replicate cannot estimate variability reliably. For level 1-alpha, let Q(p) denote a replicate quantile and z(p) a standard normal quantile. Four intervals are returned:

    • Percentile: [Q(alpha/2), Q(1-alpha/2)].
    • Basic: [2 theta-hat - Q(1-alpha/2), 2 theta-hat - Q(alpha/2)].
    • Normal: theta-hat - estimated bias, plus or minus z(1-alpha/2) times SE.
    • BCa: [Q(p-low), Q(p-high)], using the adjustment below.

    Quantiles linearly interpolate the sorted replicates at index (B-1)p, the R type-7 convention. Finite-sample interpolation and discrete statistics mean these endpoints do not give exact coverage simply by construction. Basic reflection does not remove bias automatically; the normal interval also assumes that a symmetric normal approximation is useful.

    For BCa, theta-(i) denotes the statistic with row i removed, and theta-dot their mean. The core uses strict replicate < theta-hat counts, so ties affect the bias correction:

    \[z_0=\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\#\{\theta_b^*<\hat\theta\}}{B}\right),\qquad a=\frac{\sum_i(\theta_{\cdot}-\theta_{(i)})^3} {6\left[\sum_i(\theta_{\cdot}-\theta_{(i)})^2\right]^{3/2}},\qquad p(q)=\Phi\left(z_0+\frac{z_0+z(q)}{1-a[z_0+z(q)]}\right).\]

    Use p(alpha/2) and p(1-alpha/2) for the two endpoints. A constant jackknife sequence uses a=0. BCa falls back to the percentile interval if the strict fraction is zero or one, fewer than two jackknife values are available, or the adjustment is nonfinite, singular, crosses its pole or reverses the endpoint probabilities. Its higher-order accuracy theory needs regularity conditions; this tied median example is not a demonstration of those conditions.

    Bootstrap::run owns the resamples and delegates interval construction to stats::bootstrap_summary, which also accepts externally generated finite replicates and optional jackknife values. Original, resampled and jackknife statistics must all be finite; observations themselves are left to the callback's missing-value policy. Scaled jackknife deviations and widened centred sums reduce numeric overflow, and unrepresentable summary endpoints raise an exception instead of silently returning infinities.

    Original experiment: the iris Sepal.Length median

    The built-in iris dataset supplies 150 complete sepal lengths in centimetres, pooled across three species. This preserves the original univariate experiment: B=4000, level=0.95 and seed=20240915. Treating the pooled rows as i.i.d. is an illustrative resampling model; the calculation neither models species structure nor establishes a sampling design for population inference. dm::median copies the values and selects the middle order statistics without changing the input. For even n it uses an overflow-safe midpoint.

    Calibration at increasing resample counts

    The mean provides a reference without needing a coverage simulation. Each row starts the same seeded engine and therefore uses a nested prefix of its replicates on this toolchain. The solid reference is the exact conditional bootstrap SE derived above. The second reference is s/sqrt(n), the usual estimated mean SE. Monte Carlo deviations need not shrink monotonically with B; their long-run limit is the conditional bootstrap reference.

    The default overload uses a local MT19937-64 and the standard library's uniform integer distribution. A fixed seed reproduces a run with the same implementation; different C++ standard libraries may map raw engine words to indices differently. A caller-owned RandomGenerator overload is also available. Neither overload automatically handles dependence, clusters, informative sampling or a statistic whose bootstrap distribution is inconsistent. Increasing B only improves the Monte Carlo approximation to the selected conditional law.
