    Baum–Welch: Learning Hidden Markov Models

    Abstract

    A hidden Markov model explains a sequence by an unobserved Markov chain of states, each emitting observations from its own distribution. The Baum–Welch algorithm learns the model from the observations alone. We derive it as expectation–maximisation: the E-step computes, by forward–backward, the expected number of times each state is visited and each transition is taken; the M-step re-estimates the parameters as ratios of those counts; and Jensen's inequality shows that the likelihood never decreases. On 3,000 simulated rolls of an "occasionally dishonest casino" it recovers the loaded die's bias as 0.464 (true 0.5) and decodes which die was used for 80.5% of the rolls; random restarts reveal local optima. On the eruption durations of Old Faithful in dm::datasets::faithful(), a two-state Gaussian HMM beats an independent two-component mixture by 32.8 in log-likelihood and finds that a short eruption is followed by a long one with probability 0.94.

    Hidden Markov models

    A hidden Markov model with \(N\) states has an initial distribution \(\pi_i=P(q_1=i)\), a transition matrix \(A_{ij}=P(q_{t+1}=j\mid q_t=i)\), and an emission distribution \(b_i(o)\) for each state: a probability table \(B_{ik}\) for discrete symbols, or a density such as \(N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)\) for real values. Only \(o_1,\dots,o_T\) is observed. The forward variables \(\alpha_t(i)=P(o_1..o_t,q_t=i)\) and backward variables \(\beta_t(i)=P(o_{t+1}..o_T\mid q_t=i)\) satisfy

    \[\alpha_{t+1}(j)=b_j(o_{t+1})\sum_i\alpha_t(i)A_{ij},\qquad \beta_t(i)=\sum_jA_{ij}\,b_j(o_{t+1})\,\beta_{t+1}(j),\]

    and give the likelihood \(L(\lambda)=\sum_i\alpha_T(i)\) and the posteriors

    \[\gamma_t(i)=P(q_t=i\mid o)=\frac{\alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i)}{L},\qquad \xi_t(i,j)=P(q_t=i,q_{t+1}=j\mid o)=\frac{\alpha_t(i)A_{ij}b_j(o_{t+1})\beta_{t+1}(j)}{L}.\]

    Both recursions are rescaled at every step so that nothing underflows; the log-likelihood is the sum of the logs of the scale factors.

    Baum–Welch as EM

    If the state path \(q\) were known, the log-likelihood would split into separate sums:

    \[\log p(o,q\mid\lambda)=\log\pi_{q_1}+\sum_{t<T}\log A_{q_tq_{t+1}}+\sum_t\log b_{q_t}(o_t).\]

    EM replaces the unknown path by its posterior under the current parameters \(\lambda'\). Taking the expectation gives

    \[Q(\lambda\mid\lambda')=\sum_i\gamma_1(i)\log\pi_i+\sum_{i,j}\Bigl(\sum_{t<T}\xi_t(i,j)\Bigr)\log A_{ij}+\sum_{i,t}\gamma_t(i)\log b_i(o_t).\]

    Each term is maximised separately. For row \(i\) of \(A\), maximising \(\sum_jc_{ij}\log A_{ij}\) subject to \(\sum_jA_{ij}=1\) with a Lagrange multiplier gives \(c_{ij}/A_{ij}=\nu\), so \(A_{ij}\propto c_{ij}\). The same argument applies to \(\pi\) and to a discrete \(B\), and for Gaussian emissions the weighted normal log-likelihood is maximised by the weighted mean and variance:

    \[\pi_i=\gamma_1(i),\quad A_{ij}=\frac{\sum_{t<T}\xi_t(i,j)}{\sum_{t<T}\gamma_t(i)},\quad B_{ik}=\frac{\sum_{t:o_t=k}\gamma_t(i)}{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)},\quad \mu_i=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)o_t}{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)},\quad \sigma_i^2=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)(o_t-\mu_i)^2}{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)}.\]

    These are expected counts divided by expected totals: "how often was the chain in \(i\) and moved to \(j\), out of how often it was in \(i\)".

    *The likelihood never decreases.* Write \(p(q\mid o,\lambda')\) as \(w(q)\). For any \(\lambda\),

    \[\log L(\lambda)=\log\sum_q w(q)\frac{p(o,q\mid\lambda)}{w(q)}\ \ge\ \sum_qw(q)\log\frac{p(o,q\mid\lambda)}{w(q)} =Q(\lambda\mid\lambda')+H(w),\]

    by Jensen's inequality, with equality at \(\lambda=\lambda'\) because then the ratio is the constant \(L(\lambda')\). The M-step chooses \(\lambda\) with \(Q(\lambda\mid\lambda')\ge Q(\lambda'\mid\lambda')\), so \(\log L(\lambda)\ge Q(\lambda\mid\lambda')+H(w)\ge Q(\lambda'\mid\lambda')+H(w)=\log L(\lambda')\). The iteration climbs to a stationary point, which need not be the global maximum.

    The occasionally dishonest casino

    A dealer rolls a fair die, and occasionally switches to a loaded one that shows six half the time and each other face with probability 0.1. The fair die is kept with probability 0.95 per roll and the loaded one with probability 0.90. The block samples 3,000 rolls with sample_hmm() and gives Baum–Welch only the rolls, starting from a nearly uniform model. It then decodes the dice with Viterbi and repeats the fit from 30 random starting models.

    From the near-uniform start the likelihood rose at every iteration, from \(-5312.9\) to \(-5244.2\), and the fitted parameters are close to the truth: the dice are kept with probability 0.951 and 0.911 (true 0.95 and 0.90), and the loaded die shows six with probability 0.464 (true 0.5). The fitted model's log-likelihood is 5.5 higher than the true model's. That is not an error: the maximum-likelihood estimate fits this particular sample, including its chance deviations, better than the parameters that generated it. With 3,000 rolls, of which 937 used the loaded die, the loaded die's face probabilities are estimated from the equivalent of about 900 observations, so errors of a few hundredths are expected.

    Decoding with the fitted model labels 80.5% of rolls with the correct die, almost as many as the true model manages (81.2%). The ceiling is set by the problem, not the fit: a single roll of 3 says little about which die was used, and short stretches with the loaded die are indistinguishable from luck.

    The random restarts show the local optima. Of 30 random starting models, 23 finished within 0.5 of the best log-likelihood, but others stalled for a long time near \(-5280\) and some never left it. That plateau is a poorer stationary point of the likelihood, which EM can approach and then leave only very slowly. In practice Baum–Welch is run from several starts and the best result is kept.

    Old Faithful's rhythm

    dm::datasets::faithful() holds 272 consecutive eruptions of the Old Faithful geyser: the eruption duration in minutes and the wait before it. Durations are bimodal, short (about 2 minutes) or long (about 4.5), and the order matters: a short eruption is almost never followed by another short one. An independent two-component Gaussian mixture captures the bimodality but not the order; a two-state Gaussian HMM captures both. The block fits the mixture (an HMM whose transition rows are forced equal), and HMMs with two and three states, with baum_welch_gaussian(), and compares them by BIC \(=-2\log L+k\log T\) for \(k\) free parameters.

    The order of the eruptions carries information the mixture ignores. Only 6 of the 97 short eruptions are followed by another short one, and the lag plot shows an almost empty bottom-left quadrant. The two-state HMM finds the same means and spreads as the mixture (about 2.0 and 4.3 minutes) but raises the log-likelihood from \(-276.4\) to \(-243.6\) at the cost of two parameters, lowering BIC from 580.8 to 526.4. Its transition matrix says a short eruption is followed by a long one with probability 0.938, while after a long eruption the next is short or long about equally often (0.521 and 0.479). Its stationary distribution puts 35.7% of eruptions in the short state, exactly the share below 3 minutes. The states are rarely in doubt: only 3 of 272 eruptions have a posterior between 0.05 and 0.95.

    BIC prefers three states still more (505.1). The third state splits the long eruptions into a "medium-long" group around 4.0 minutes and a "very long" group around 4.5 minutes with a smaller spread. The transitions show why this helps: a very long eruption is never followed by another very long one (probability 0.000), and a short eruption is usually followed by a very long one (0.815). A plausible physical reading is that a short eruption leaves the reservoir partly charged, so the next one is long, while a very long one empties it, so the next is shorter. An independent mixture cannot express any of this.

    Summary

    • Baum–Welch is EM for hidden Markov models: forward–backward gives the expected state and transition counts, and the M-step sets each parameter to an expected count over an expected total. Jensen's inequality makes every iteration raise the likelihood.
    • On 3,000 simulated casino rolls it recovered the switching probabilities to within 0.011 and the loaded die's six-probability as 0.464 (true 0.5), and decoded 80.5% of rolls correctly, close to the 81.2% possible with the true model. Random starts can stall at poorer stationary points, so several starts are needed.
    • On Old Faithful's eruption durations a two-state Gaussian HMM improves the log-likelihood of an independent mixture by 32.8 and finds that a short eruption is followed by a long one with probability 0.94; three states, splitting the long eruptions, are better still by BIC.
