    Linear Regression: Penguin Body Mass

    Abstract

    We often want to explain a numeric outcome from a few measurements and groupings: how heavy is a penguin, given its bill, its flipper and its species? A good answer gives each effect with an uncertainty, tests whether a group of terms matters, and predicts new cases with an honest interval. The linear model fitted by least squares does all of this with closed-form formulas. We derive the estimator as a projection, the t and F tests, the two kinds of interval and the influence diagnostics, and check every formula by hand on five points. A simulation confirms that the intervals cover 95% under the model's assumptions and shows how they fail when the error variance grows with \(x\). On the Palmer penguins, bill length, bill depth, flipper length and species explain 85% of the variance in body mass. The data show Simpson's paradox: across all penguins a deeper bill goes with a lighter bird, but within every species it goes with a heavier one. The residuals also reveal the most important missing variable, sex, whose effect differs between species.

    The problem

    We observe \(n\) cases, each with a response \(y_i\) and predictors. A formula such as body_mass_g ~ bill_length_mm + bill_depth_mm + flipper_length_mm + species turns the predictors into the rows \(x_i^\top\) of an \(n\times p\) design matrix \(X\): a column of ones for the intercept, one column per numeric predictor, and one indicator column per non-baseline level of each factor. The linear model is

    \[y=X\beta+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_n),\]

    with unknown coefficients \(\beta\in\mathbb R^p\) and error variance \(\sigma^2\). We want to estimate \(\beta\) and \(\sigma\), say how precise the estimates are, test hypotheses such as "species does not matter once the measurements are known", predict the response of new cases, and find the cases the fit depends on most. We assume \(X\) has full column rank \(p<n\).

    The method

    Least squares is a projection

    The least-squares estimate minimises the residual sum of squares \(\mathrm{RSS}(\beta)=\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2\). Setting the gradient \(-2X^\top(y-X\beta)\) to zero gives the normal equations and their solution:

    \[X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y,\qquad \hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.\]

    The fitted values are \(\hat y=X\hat\beta=Hy\) with the hat matrix \(H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\). \(H\) is symmetric and \(H^2=H\), so it is the orthogonal projection onto the column space of \(X\): \(\hat y\) is the closest point to \(y\) that the model can produce, and the residuals \(e=y-\hat y=(I-H)y\) are orthogonal to every column of \(X\). With an intercept column this means the residuals sum to zero.

    The diagonal entries \(h_{ii}\) are the leverages. Because \(H\) is a projection of rank \(p\),

    \[0\le h_{ii}\le1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n h_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}H=\operatorname{tr}\big((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X\big)=p.\]

    The average leverage is \(p/n\). Since \(\hat y_i=\sum_jh_{ij}y_j\), \(h_{ii}\) is the weight of \(y_i\) in its own fitted value: a case with \(h_{ii}\) near 1 pulls the fit through itself. For simple regression \(h_{ii}=1/n+(x_i-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}\) with \(S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\), so leverage measures how far \(x_i\) is from the centre of the data.

    The sampling distribution

    Substituting \(y=X\beta+\varepsilon\) gives \(\hat\beta=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon\), a linear function of a normal vector, so

    \[\hat\beta\sim N\big(\beta,\ \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\big).\]

    The estimate is unbiased whatever the error distribution, provided the errors have mean zero. The residuals are \(e=(I-H)\varepsilon\), and \(I-H\) is a projection of rank \(n-p\), so \(\mathrm{RSS}=\varepsilon^\top(I-H)\varepsilon\sim\sigma^2\chi^2_{n-p}\). This gives the unbiased variance estimate \(\hat\sigma^2=\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)\) (sigma() returns \(\hat\sigma\)). Because \(He\) and \((I-H)\varepsilon\) are uncorrelated normal vectors, \(\hat\beta\) and \(\hat\sigma^2\) are independent. The standard error of \(\hat\beta_j\) is \(\mathrm{SE}_j=\hat\sigma\sqrt{[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{jj}}\), and

    \[t_j=\frac{\hat\beta_j-\beta_j}{\mathrm{SE}_j}\sim t_{n-p},\]

    a standard normal divided by an independent \(\sqrt{\chi^2_{n-p}/(n-p)}\). Setting \(\beta_j=0\) gives the t test in every regression table, and inverting it gives the confidence interval \(\hat\beta_j\pm t_{n-p,0.975}\,\mathrm{SE}_j\).

    F tests for groups of terms

    A factor with \(k\) levels contributes \(k-1\) columns, so "species does not matter" is a hypothesis about several coefficients at once. Let a reduced model drop \(q\) columns of the full model. Its residual sum of squares can only be larger, and under the reduced model

    \[F=\frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_0-\mathrm{RSS}_1)/q}{\mathrm{RSS}_1/(n-p)}\sim F_{q,\,n-p},\]

    because the numerator and denominator are independent \(\chi^2\) variables divided by their degrees of freedom (the two RSS differences live in orthogonal subspaces). stats::anova(reduced, full) computes this test. Dropping every column but the intercept gives the overall F statistic of the summary, and \(R^2=1-\mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\), with \(\mathrm{TSS}=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2\), is the fraction of variance the model explains. LM::anova() adds the terms one at a time in formula order and reports each drop in RSS. These sequential sums of squares add up to \(\mathrm{TSS}-\mathrm{RSS}\), but each depends on the terms before it.

    Confidence and prediction intervals

    At a new point \(x_0\) the estimated mean \(\hat y_0=x_0^\top\hat\beta\) has variance \(\sigma^2q_0\), with \(q_0=x_0^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x_0\). A new observation \(y_0=x_0^\top\beta+\varepsilon_0\) adds its own independent error. The two intervals are

    \[\text{mean: }\ \hat y_0\pm t_{n-p,0.975}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{q_0},\qquad \text{new observation: }\ \hat y_0\pm t_{n-p,0.975}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+q_0}.\]

    As \(n\) grows, \(q_0\to0\) and the confidence interval shrinks to a point, but the prediction interval never gets narrower than about \(\pm1.96\sigma\). Both are narrowest at the centre of the data, where \(q_0=1/n\) for simple regression. LM::predict() returns either interval.

    Diagnostics

    The residuals do not all have the same variance: \(\operatorname{Var}(e_i)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})\), smaller for high-leverage cases because the fit is pulled towards them. The standardized residual \(r_i=e_i/(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1-h_{ii}})\) corrects this. The studentized residual \(t_i\) also replaces \(\hat\sigma\) by \(\hat\sigma_{(i)}\), the estimate from the fit without case \(i\). It follows \(t_{n-p-1}\) exactly and can be computed without refitting: \(t_i=r_i\sqrt{(n-p-1)/(n-p-r_i^2)}\).

    Cook's distance measures how far \(\hat\beta\) moves when case \(i\) is deleted, in the metric of its own covariance:

    \[D_i=\frac{(\hat\beta_{(i)}-\hat\beta)^\top X^\top X(\hat\beta_{(i)}-\hat\beta)}{p\,\hat\sigma^2}=\frac{r_i^2}{p}\,\frac{h_{ii}}{1-h_{ii}}.\]

    So influence needs both a large residual and high leverage. A case with \(D_i\) near 1 moves \(\hat\beta\) to the edge of its 50% confidence region. Plotting \(r_i\) against \(h_{ii}\) with the curves \(D=0.5\) and \(D=1\) (which are \(r=\pm\sqrt{Dp(1-h)/h}\)) shows both at once.

    Comparing models that are not nested

    The F test needs nested models. To compare others, the information criteria trade fit against size. The maximised log-likelihood of a linear model is \(\ell=-\tfrac n2\big(\log(2\pi\,\mathrm{RSS}/n)+1\big)\), and with \(k=p+1\) parameters (including \(\sigma\)), \(\mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell+2k\) and \(\mathrm{BIC}=-2\ell+k\log n\). Lower is better.

    Computation

    stats::LM builds \(X\) from the formula and the data frame and drops rows with a missing value in any column it uses, as R's na.omit does. It solves the least-squares problem through a Cholesky factorisation of \(X^\top X\) (\(O(np^2+p^3)\)) and computes the leverages from the stored \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) in \(O(np^2)\). Every number below has been checked against R's lm, anova, confint, predict, hatvalues and cooks.distance.

    Simple examples

    Five points by hand

    Take \(x=(1,2,3,4,5)\) and \(y=(1,3,2,5,4)\). Then \(\bar x=\bar y=3\), \(S_{xx}=10\) and \(S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-3)(y_i-3)=4+0+0+2+2=8\), so the slope is \(S_{xy}/S_{xx}=0.8\) and the intercept \(3-0.8\cdot3=0.6\). The fitted values are \(1.4,2.2,3.0,3.8,4.6\), the residuals \(-0.4,0.8,-1.0,1.2,-0.6\), and \(\mathrm{RSS}=3.6\). With \(n-p=3\), \(\hat\sigma^2=1.2\), \(\mathrm{SE}(\text{slope})=\sqrt{1.2/10}=0.346\) and \(t=0.8/0.346=2.31\). The leverages are \(1/5+(x_i-3)^2/10=0.6,0.3,0.2,0.3,0.6\), which sum to \(p=2\). At \(x_0=3\), \(q_0=1/5\) and \(t_{3,0.975}=3.182\), so the prediction interval is \(3\pm3.182\sqrt{1.2\cdot1.2}=3\pm3.819\).

    Checking the theory

    The derivations promise four things under the model: \(\hat\beta\) is unbiased with standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\) for the slope, \(t\) follows \(t_{n-p}\), and both intervals cover 95% of the time. We simulate \(y_i=1+0.5x_i+\varepsilon_i\) at \(x_i=1,\dots,20\) (\(n=20\), \(S_{xx}=665\)), 4,000 times for each of three error laws with standard deviation about 2:

    • normal: \(\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,4)\), the model's assumption;
    • heavy-tailed: \(\varepsilon_i=(2/\sqrt3)\,T_i\) with \(T_i\sim t_3\), variance 4 but frequent outliers;
    • heteroscedastic: \(\varepsilon_i\sim N\big(0,(2x_i/10.5)^2\big)\), a spread growing from 0.19 to 3.8.

    For each fit we record the slope, its standard error and whether its 95% interval covers 0.5, and we draw a new observation at every \(x_i\) to check the 95% prediction intervals.

    normal0.49930.07970.07640.9460.9490.947 / 0.947
    heavy-tailed0.49960.07770.06990.9550.9350.932 / 0.933

    Theory: slope 0.5, sd 2 / sqrt(665) = 0.0776; coverage 0.95. Normal errors: 0.054 of t statistics beyond the t(18) 2.5% quantiles, 0.070 beyond +/-1.96.

    With normal errors every promise holds: the slope is unbiased, its spread matches \(\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\), the reported standard error is right on average, and both intervals cover 95%. The \(t\) statistics follow \(t_{18}\), not the normal: 5% of them fall outside \(\pm2.10\), but more than 5% fall outside \(\pm1.96\). With heavy-tailed errors the slope is still unbiased and its interval still covers about 95%, because \(\hat\beta\) is a weighted average of many errors. The prediction intervals cover only about 93.5% at every \(x\): they rest on the distribution of one new error, which no averaging makes normal, and \(\hat\sigma\) is usually too small, because the rare large errors that make the variance 4 are missing from most samples (the mean standard error is 0.070 against a true 0.078). The heteroscedastic errors break the prediction intervals: they use one \(\hat\sigma\) everywhere, so they are far too wide at small \(x\) (coverage 1.000 at \(x=1\)) and far too narrow at large \(x\) (0.779 at \(x=20\)), although the overall coverage, 0.930, looks almost acceptable. The slope's standard error is also wrong under heteroscedasticity, because \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\Sigma X(X^\top X)^{-1}\) no longer reduces to \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\). A scale-location plot, as in the next section, is how this shows up in real data.

    A dataset example: Palmer penguins

    The data

    dm::datasets::penguins() is the Palmer Archipelago data of Gorman, Williams and Fraser (2014), as in the palmerpenguins R package: 344 penguins of three species, with bill length and depth, flipper length, body mass, sex, island and year.     The full model explains 85% of the variance in body mass with a residual standard error of 316 g. Each extra millimetre of flipper adds about 20 g and each millimetre of bill depth about 140 g, holding the other variables and species fixed. A Gentoo penguin is about 935 g heavier than an Adelie penguin with the same measurements, and a Chinstrap about 513 g lighter. Species is significant given the measurements (the F test above), and the full model has the lowest AIC and BIC, by about 150 and 130 points. Bill length and depth add almost nothing to flipper length when species is left out: the three-measurement model has a higher AIC than flipper length alone. These coefficients match R's lm(body_mass_g ~ bill_length_mm + bill_depth_mm + flipper_length_mm + species, penguins) to every printed digit.

    Simpson's paradox in bill depth

    The bill-depth coefficient depends on whether species is in the model. Across all penguins deeper bills go with lighter birds, because the heavy Gentoos have shallow bills. Within each species the relation is positive.

    The slope through all penguins is negative and its interval excludes zero; every within-species slope is positive, with intervals well above zero. Neither is wrong: they answer different questions. The marginal slope compares penguins of different species, and species drives both mass and bill shape. The full model's coefficient, 140 g/mm, is a within-species comparison at fixed bill length and flipper length. The three-measurement model without species gets only 20 g/mm, with an interval that includes zero: flipper length separates Gentoos from the rest, but not well enough to remove the confounding. A regression coefficient is only interpretable relative to the other terms in the model.

    What the residuals miss: sex

    The model has no term for sex, and male penguins are larger. If sex matters beyond what the measurements capture, the residuals should differ between the sexes.

    speciesfemalesmalesmale - female mass (g)same, fitted (g)same, residuals (g)
    Adelie7373674.7423.5251.2
    Chinstrap3434411.8580.9-169.2
    Gentoo5861805.1543.8261.3

    On the 333 penguins with known sex, adding sex gives F = 66.40 on 1 and 326 df (p = 7.99e-15), a male coefficient of 389.9 g, sigma 314.8 -> 287.3 g and AIC 4783.7 -> 4723.9. Letting the sex effect differ by species gives F = 13.07 on 2 and 324 df (p = 3.47e-06), sigma 277.3 g and AIC 4702.1.

    In Adelie and Gentoo penguins the males are about 250 g heavier, relative to the females, than their measurements predict. Chinstraps go the other way: males are 412 g heavier than females, but their longer bills and flippers predict 581 g, so the model overestimates male Chinstraps. Adding a single sex term is highly significant and lowers the residual standard error by about 30 g, and letting its effect differ by species (species:sex) is significant again. The diagnostic plots did not reveal any of this: a residual plot against fitted values cannot show a variable that is not in the model. Plotting the residuals against candidate variables can.

    Predicting new penguins

    Finally, we predict the mass of a typical penguin of each species, with the species' mean measurements, using the model without sex (so that a bird of unknown sex can be predicted).

    The mean of such penguins is known to within about 50 to 75 g, but a single new penguin can be anywhere in a range of about \(\pm620\) g, because the prediction interval keeps the full residual variance. The Chinstrap interval for the mean is the widest: Chinstraps are the smallest group (68 birds), so its coefficient is the least precise.

    Conclusion

    Least squares projects the response onto the span of the design matrix, and from that geometry follow the estimator, its covariance, the \(t\) and \(F\) tests, the two kinds of interval, leverage and Cook's distance. Each formula was checked by hand on five points, and a sixth point at \((12,2)\) showed how one high-leverage case can flatten a line. The simulation confirmed the exact \(t_{n-p}\) distribution and the 95% coverage under normal errors, and showed which promises survive when the assumptions fail: the slope interval tolerates heavy tails, but the prediction intervals fail when the variance changes with \(x\), too wide where the variance is small and too narrow where it is large.

    On the Palmer penguins, three measurements and species explain 85% of the variance in body mass. The bill-depth coefficient reverses sign once species is included, an instance of Simpson's paradox, so coefficients must be read relative to the other terms. The standard diagnostics found no outliers, curvature or influential points, but a residual plot against a variable outside the model found the main omission, sex, worth a further 30 g of residual standard error, with an effect that differs by species.

    Limits: the inference assumes independent cases, a correct mean structure and constant variance. The heteroscedastic simulation shows that a single \(\hat\sigma\) misstates prediction uncertainty when the spread varies; weighted least squares (LmOptions::weights_column) or a transformation of the response are the usual remedies. The sequential ANOVA depends on the order of the terms. And the model describes associations in these colonies, not the effect of changing a penguin's bill.
