    Kernel Ridge Regression: Centring, Shrinkage and Validation

    Abstract

    A regression curve must predict a numeric response from inputs whose relationship may be nonlinear, while controlling how closely it follows noisy observations. Kernel ridge regression fits a penalised function through similarities between training rows. We derive the free-intercept estimator and check every quantity on two observations. A four-row counterexample separates fixed-metric leave-one-out from validation that refits preprocessing. A noisy sine wave tests spectral shrinkage against a known mean; Iris supplies a separate, untouched regression holdout. The examples distinguish training fit, tuning scores and evaluation error.

    The method

    Similarities define a function space

    Given inputs \(x_i\) and numeric responses \(y_i\), choose a positive-semidefinite kernel \(k\) and its reproducing-kernel Hilbert space \(\mathcal H\). Fit a scalar intercept \(b\) and a function \(f\) by

    \[\min_{b\in\mathbb R,\,f\in\mathcal H}\ \sum_{i=1}^n[y_i-b-f(x_i)]^2+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal H}^2, \qquad f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i k(x_i,x),\qquad K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j).\]

    The representer form follows by decomposing any function into its projection onto the span of training kernels and an orthogonal remainder. The remainder evaluates to zero at every training input, so removing it preserves the loss and reduces the penalty. For \(\lambda>0\), a minimum therefore lies in that finite span. The library uses a *sum* of losses: a mean-loss penalty \(\rho\) corresponds to \(\lambda=n\rho\). Changing that convention changes numerical penalty values.

    Supported kernels are linear \(x^Tz\), polynomial \((\gamma x^Tz+c)^d\), and RBF \(\exp[-\gamma\|x-z\|^2]\). Polynomial degree is a positive integer, offset is nonnegative, and nonlinear gamma is positive. By default each resolved Formula feature uses its training mean and population SD; a constant feature uses divisor one. Thus gamma measures distance in those standardised coordinates. RBF length scale \(\ell\) in the convention \(\exp[-\|x-z\|^2/(2\ell^2)]\) gives \(\gamma=1/(2\ell^2)\). A large gamma makes similarities local; it does not by itself specify the fitted complexity, which also depends on lambda and the data.

    A free intercept requires kernel centring

    Let \(C=I-\mathbf1\mathbf1^T/n\), \(K_c=CKC\), and \(y_c=Cy\). For positive lambda, the intercept normal equation and kernel equations give

    \[\begin{bmatrix}K+\lambda I&\mathbf1\\\mathbf1^T&0\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y\\0\end{bmatrix}, \quad\alpha=(K_c+\lambda I)^{-1}y_c, \quad b=\bar y-\bar k^T\alpha,\quad \bar k=K\mathbf1/n.\]

    Merely subtracting \(\bar y\) before solving an uncentred kernel generally fails these equations: the dual coefficients need not sum to zero. A query's centred kernel row is \(k_{*c,j}=k(x_*,x_j)-\bar k_j-\bar k_*+\bar K\), where \(\bar k_*\) averages its training similarities and \(\bar K\) averages the full training Gram. Prediction is \(\bar y+k_{*c}^T\alpha=b+k_*^T\alpha\). Every centring quantity comes from the fitted rows. The option fit_intercept=false uses raw kernels and raw responses with zero intercept. Formula +1/-1 markers are suppressed in the feature design; this option controls the intercept.

    Eigenvalues explain effective complexity

    Write \(K_c=U\operatorname{diag}(d_j)U^T\) on the centred subspace. Each response coordinate \(u_j^Ty_c\) is multiplied by \(d_j/(d_j+\lambda)\). Hence

    \[\widehat y=Sy,\quad S=\mathbf1\mathbf1^T/n+U\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j+\lambda}\right)U^T, \quad\mathrm{df}=\operatorname{tr}S=1+\sum_{d_j>0}\frac{d_j}{d_j+\lambda}, \quad\frac{\partial\mathrm{df}}{\partial\lambda}=-\sum_{d_j>0}\frac{d_j}{(d_j+\lambda)^2}\le0.\]

    For a fixed kernel, df tends to one as the penalty grows; without an intercept the constant term is absent. Larger lambda reduces the fitted RKHS norm and increases training residual sum of squares. This also follows by adding the optimality inequalities for two penalties. Neither fact proves monotone validation or truth error. A free-intercept prediction has weights summing to one, so constant responses are reproduced; weights can be negative and predictions can exceed the observed response range. These are penalised function fits, unlike positive local averages.

    At zero penalty, the implementation uses a spectral pseudoinverse with eigenvalues at or below \(64n\epsilon\max_{ij}|K_{ij}|\) treated as numerical zero. It chooses the minimum-RKHS-norm least-squares limit under that rank convention. Contradictory responses at duplicate inputs cannot be interpolated. Tiny positive penalties can still be ill-conditioned. The diagnostics expose rank, objective and equation residual; at zero penalty with incompatible labels, an equation residual need not vanish because the centred response can lie outside the kernel range.

    Three different errors and two validation procedures

    Training predictions use all fitting responses. fitted_values(), rmse() and r_squared() retain this API's historical meaning: *fixed-metric LOO* predictions and scores. With fixed positive lambda, define

    \[B=C(K_c+\lambda I)^{-1}C,\qquad \widehat y_i^{(-i)}=y_i-\frac{\alpha_i}{B_{ii}},\qquad \operatorname{LOO}_{\mathrm{metric}}=\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i^{(-i)})^2.\]

    Here the deleted fit refits the intercept and kernel centring but freezes the full-data predictor scaling, resolved feature schema, gamma and lambda. Without an intercept use \(C=I\). This is the usual residual-over-one-minus-leverage identity, computed through the inverse diagonal to avoid cancellation when leverage is near one. At lambda zero the code explicitly fits deleted Gram matrices; it does not replace a zero denominator with an arbitrary floor.

    kernel_ridge_cross_validate instead fits each fold's Formula preprocessing, predictor scaling, response offset and kernel centring using that fold's training rows. It requires explicit, fixed candidates, contiguous retained fold IDs and at least two fitting rows per fold. Its pooled MSE weights validation rows equally. Returned fold means/scales and row-aligned predictions make this distinction inspectable. Candidate ties choose the first strict minimum. An unavailable fold schema or invalid held-out prediction raises an error rather than dropping it. Neither a minimum tuning score nor the score used to select gamma estimates the selected procedure's generalisation error without an outer holdout or nested CV.

    Default automatic fitting searches 40 log-spaced lambdas from 0.0001 to 1000 and 12 gammas from 0.03 to 30 times the inverse upper-median positive squared pair distance in the full training metric. Constant metrics use base one. Explicit candidate vectors preserve caller order. This finite search minimises the fixed-metric LOO score; it is neither continuous optimisation nor fold-refitted CV.

    Dense work for G gammas and L positive penalties is \(O(G(n^2p+n^3+Ln^2))\), with \(O(n^2+np)\) fitted storage. Zero-penalty deletion refits can add \(O(Gn^4)\). A GP with the same fixed mean, kernel and noise-to-signal ratio lambda has the corresponding posterior mean. Free-intercept treatment must also agree; empirical response centring alone is a different mean convention. KRR supplies no posterior predictive variance.

    Small worked examples

    Two RBF anchors: all the numbers fit on a page

    Use \(x=(0,1)\), \(y=(0,2)\), no predictor standardisation, \(\gamma=\log2\), and \(\lambda=1/2\). Then \(K=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1/2\\1/2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), \(K_c=\tfrac14\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), and \(y_c=(-1,1)^T\). The centred contrast eigenvalue is 1/2, so its shrinkage is 1/2. Thus \(\alpha=(-1,1)\), \(b=1\), training predictions are \((1/2,3/2)\), \(S=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/4&3/4\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), and df is 1.5. Each deletion leaves a single observation whose free intercept reproduces that response: LOO predictions are \((2,0)\), RMSE is two, and LOO R-squared is minus three. Negative R-squared is legitimate. The midpoint prediction is one by symmetry. Training RSS is 1/2, squared RKHS norm is one, and objective is one.

    Linear kernel: ridge in feature coordinates, with signed extrapolation weights

    For a linear kernel the representer form gives \(f(x)=x^T\beta\) with \(\beta=X_c^T\alpha=(X_c^TX_c+\lambda I)^{-1}X_c^Ty_c\). The first equality and the centred kernel system prove the second by multiplying through \(X_c^T\); uniqueness follows for positive lambda. With the same two rows, \(X_c^TX_c=1/2\) and \(X_c^Ty_c=1\), so \(\beta=1\), \(b=1/2\). At query \(x=3\), prediction is 3.5, and its response weights are \((-3/4,7/4)\). They sum to one but are not probabilities. For constant responses seven, the prediction is seven at every query. The figure includes the extrapolation region explicitly rather than implying interpolation is all the model can do.

    Checking the theory on a known function

    A noisy sine wave: tuning targets observations, evaluation targets the known mean

    kernel_ridge_wave preserves the original experiment: 120 uniform inputs on [0,1], responses \(y=\sin(2\pi x)+\epsilon\), independent normal noise SD 0.15, and local seed 20240815. The C++ standard distributions can differ across standard libraries, so exact reproducibility refers to this build's generated rows. We retain all rows for fitting and use the default finite fixed-metric search. The 401-point evaluation grid has response equal to the generating mean, not a fresh noisy observation. Its RMSE measures curve recovery; it is not a holdout estimate of noisy-response prediction error. In particular the noise SD is not a lower bound on error against the noiseless mean.

    The selected gamma is about 15.6026 and lambda 0.170125, giving df 18.5290. Training RMSE is about 0.12535 and conditional LOO RMSE 0.14734. This corrected free-intercept fit is fairly local: its mean-curve error is about 0.06, despite a small tuning score. The displayed curve and independent reference calculation are more informative than claiming that cross-validation recovers the true function optimally. The tuning curve below holds gamma at its selected value; it is one slice of the full 480-candidate search.

    A dataset example: Iris petal regression

    Declare the holdout and tune within the remaining rows

    dm::datasets::iris() contains 150 flowers, 50 per species, with four numeric measurements in centimetres and a species label. Predict Petal.Length from Petal.Width; this is regression of measurements, not species classification. The pooled relation partly reflects differences between species. We leave out original rows 0, 5, 10, ... for final evaluation: 30 flowers, ten per species. The remaining 120 rows supply all tuning and preprocessing. This deterministic split is transparent, but does not quantify population uncertainty or robustness to a different sampling scheme.

    Inside those 120 rows, fold ID is retained-row index modulo four. Every validation fold has 30 rows (ten per species) and 90 fitting rows. Compare RBF gammas 0.1, one and ten, each at lambda 0.01, 0.1 and one, plus the linear kernel at those three penalties. Each of the 48 fold fits learns its own scaling. Gamma values are therefore comparable in training-standardised units, without importing validation-row SDs. We choose the first minimum pooled validation MSE, then refit that fixed candidate on all 120 fitting rows. The 30 holdout rows take no part in this choice. Fold means and SDs below belong to the winning candidate only.

    Refit the chosen model and evaluate the untouched rows

    The following complete program repeats the declared tuning protocol, refits its winner once, and scores all 30 held-out flowers. The baseline always predicts the *120-row training response mean*. R-squared uses variation about the holdout response mean, so its baseline differs from that reported baseline RMSE. The training and fixed-metric LOO diagnostics printed alongside it describe the refitted model; they are not substituted for held-out performance. On this split, RBF gamma 0.1 and lambda 0.01 win with pooled validation RMSE 0.402698 cm. The refitted df is 3.9336. Held-out RMSE is 0.366721 cm versus 1.816220 cm for the training-mean predictor, with held-out R-squared 0.959029. The holdout result happens to be better than the tuning score; this ordering is not guaranteed by the procedure. No confidence band is shown because this estimator does not supply predictive uncertainty. The fitted curve covers only the observed petal-width range.

    What the calculations establish

    The two-point examples recover the augmented normal equations, centred-kernel shrinkage, full smoother trace and deletion predictions exactly. Signed weights explain linear extrapolation while preserving constant responses. The four-row example demonstrates that exact fixed-metric LOO is a different question from refitting the whole preprocessing pipeline. The sine path checks monotone complexity and norm reduction without claiming monotone evaluation error. The Iris exercise reserves evaluation rows before fitting and compares against a training-only baseline. Its single deterministic split supports a limited predictive comparison, not population confidence intervals or a universal choice of kernel.

    Required-null fitting rows are omitted jointly; retained nonfinite values are rejected. Prediction preserves query-row order, returning NaN for invalid required query cells. score requires every evaluation response and prediction to be finite, and refuses empty or partially scored sets. Dense kernels limit scale; categorical expansions and a chosen distance metric can dominate the result. Gamma and lambda are model choices whose uncertainty is absent from the reported point predictions.
