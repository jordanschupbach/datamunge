    Generalized Linear Models: Working Responses and Iris Probabilities

    Abstract

    A binary species label needs a probability in the interval zero to one; an event count needs a nonnegative mean. Ordinary regression does not enforce those different response ranges or their different mean–variance relationships. Generalized linear models connect a conditional mean to linear predictors through a link and fit it by iteratively reweighted least squares. We calculate two tiny fits by hand, inspect every working response and weight, check how repeated observations change uncertainty, and show why numerical convergence cannot certify a finite logistic maximum under separation. The iris analysis compares logistic models on the same training flowers and evaluates probabilities on a separate test split. A final deliberately artificial count response shows why rounding a measurement does not establish a Poisson sampling model. The petal model correctly classifies 29 of 30 held-out flowers, with Brier loss 0.0254 and log loss 0.0852; the sepal model classifies 22 correctly. These are results of one small, prespecified split.

    The method

    A response family, a mean and a link

    For independent responses, a GLM specifies \(\mu_i=E(Y_i\mid x_i)\), a link \(g(\mu_i)=\eta_i=x_i^\top\beta\), and a variance function \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid x_i)=\phi V(\mu_i)/a_i\), where \(a_i>0\) is a prior weight. An intercept is included unless the formula removes it. The four fixed family/link pairs in dm::stats::GLM are:

    FamilyLink \(g(\mu)\)Variance function \(V(\mu)\)Response
    Gaussian\(\mu\)\(1\)finite continuous values
    Binomial\(\log[\mu/(1-\mu)]\)\(\mu(1-\mu)\)exactly 0 or 1
    Poisson\(\log\mu\)\(\mu\)nonnegative counts
    Gamma\(1/\mu\)\(\mu^2\)strictly positive values

    These are R's default links; Gamma's inverse link is canonical up to sign and scale. The implementation fixes \(\phi=1\) for binomial and Poisson, and estimates it for Gaussian and Gamma. Categorical species labels must be encoded explicitly for a binomial fit. Prior weights are not automatically survey weights or exposure offsets, and the current API does not fit arbitrary links, offsets or grouped binomial proportions.

    Deriving the update

    Write the log density in exponential-family form

    \[\ell(\beta,\phi)=\sum_i\frac{a_i\{y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)\}}{\phi} +c_i(y_i,\phi,a_i),\qquad \mu_i=b'(\theta_i),\quad V(\mu_i)=b''(\theta_i).\]

    The chain rule gives a score proportional to \(X^\top\operatorname{diag}\{a_i/[V_i g'_i]\}(y-\mu)\). The expected information is \(X^\top W X/\phi\), where \(w_i=a_i/[V_i(g'_i)^2]\). A Fisher-scoring step can therefore be written as a weighted least-squares problem:

    \[z_i=\eta_i+(y_i-\mu_i)g'(\mu_i),\qquad w_i=\frac{a_i}{V(\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)^2},\qquad \beta_{\rm new}=\arg\min_b\sum_i w_i(z_i-x_i^\top b)^2.\]

    For logistic regression, \(w_i=a_i\mu_i(1-\mu_i)\); for Poisson, \(w_i=a_i\mu_i\). Gaussian identity-link weights are simply \(a_i\), and \(z_i=y_i\), recovering weighted least squares immediately. The library solves each weighted system by Householder QR. Later steps that leave the finite family domain or increase deviance are halved; an invalid first step throws because no preceding feasible coefficient vector is available. This is a guarded local optimizer, not a guarantee that every valid dataset admits a finite maximum.

    working_data(k) reconstructs the inputs to update \(k+1\), starting at zero. iteration_history() records completed updates, including any step fractions. Starting means follow R's family conventions: binomial \((a_i y_i+1/2)/(a_i+1)\), Poisson \(y_i+0.1\), and Gaussian/Gamma \(y_i\). Formula-null training rows are omitted jointly, and kept_rows() retains their original zero-based indices. Nonfinite retained values and nonpositive or nonfinite prior weights are errors.

    Deviance, uncertainty and numerical stopping

    For Bernoulli data and counts respectively, unit deviance is

    \[d_B(y,\mu)=2\left[y\log\frac{y}{\mu}+(1-y)\log\frac{1-y}{1-\mu}\right], \qquad d_P(y,\mu)=2\left[y\log\frac{y}{\mu}-(y-\mu)\right], \qquad D=\sum_i a_i d(y_i,\mu_i).\]

    The Gaussian unit deviance is \((y-\mu)^2\); Gamma uses \(2[-\log(y/\mu)+(y-\mu)/\mu]\). Raw deviance residuals are \(\operatorname{sign}(y_i-\mu_i)\sqrt{a_i d_i}\), so their squared sum is \(D\), including for weighted fits. The intercept-only null mean is the prior-weighted response mean. With no intercept, the null linear predictor is zero instead.

    At the returned fit, covariance() uses \(\widehat\phi(X^\top W X)^{-1}\); \(\widehat\phi=1\) for binomial/Poisson, and Pearson chi-square divided by \(n-p\) otherwise. Coefficient intervals are Wald intervals, using a normal reference for fixed dispersion and a residual-df Student reference otherwise. For a new design row \(x_0\), mean-response confidence limits are constructed on the link scale:

    \[s_\eta^2=x_0^\top\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\widehat\beta)x_0, \qquad [L,U]=g^{-1}\{\eta_0\pm q_{1-\alpha/2}s_\eta\}, \qquad s_\mu=\left|(g^{-1})'(\eta_0)\right|s_\eta.\]

    For a decreasing inverse link the endpoints are reordered. A Gamma interval crossing zero on the link scale has an infinite upper response limit; a point prediction with nonpositive Gamma linear predictor is rejected. Logistic limits stay between zero and one. These are pointwise intervals for a conditional mean, not intervals containing a future response with the stated probability.

    converged() means only that \(|D_{k+1}-D_k|/(0.1+|D_{k+1}|)<\texttt{tol}\). The iteration limit returns a finite last iterate with that flag false, when numerical systems remain solvable. boundary() flags binomial probabilities within \(10^{-8}\) of zero or one. It is a warning heuristic, not a formal separation test. Training probabilities are bounded at machine epsilon to keep computations finite; that guard does not supply a finite MLE.

    Canonical-link likelihoods have negative-semidefinite Hessians on their domains. Full rank gives strict curvature at an interior finite solution, but existence still requires suitable data overlap. Complete logistic separation lets coefficient norms grow while the likelihood approaches its supremum. Deviance changes can become tiny along that path, so numerical convergence and existence of a maximum are different claims.

    The current QR implementation explicitly accumulates an \(n\times n\) orthogonal matrix. For \(K\) iterations and \(p<n\) columns, fitting uses \(O(Kn^2p+Kp^3)\) work and \(O(n^2+np+Kp)\) storage, including the coefficient trace. This implementation is intended for modest dense problems.

    Simple examples

    Eight binary responses in two groups

    At \(x=0\) observe \((0,0,0,1)\); at \(x=1\) observe \((0,1,1,1)\). The binomial score equations set each group's fitted probability to its sample proportion:

    \[\widehat\mu_0=\tfrac14,\quad \widehat\mu_1=\tfrac34,\quad \widehat\beta_0=-\log3,\quad \widehat\beta_1=2\log3,\quad X^\top W X=\begin{pmatrix}1.5&0.75\\0.75&0.75\end{pmatrix},\quad \operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}4/3&-4/3\\-4/3&8/3\end{pmatrix}.\]

    Initially, zero responses have \(\mu=1/4\), \(\eta=-\log3\) and \(z=-\log3-4/3\); one responses have the opposite \(\eta,z\). Every initial weight is \(3/16\). Averaging \(z\) within the groups makes the first intercept \(-[\log3+4/3]/2\) and slope \(\log3+4/3\). The table and figure expose that first update before giving the converged fit.

    Four counts and one intercept

    For \(y=(0,1,2,5)\), the Poisson intercept score is \(\sum_i(y_i-\mu)=0\), so \(\widehat\mu=2\), \(\widehat\beta=\log2\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta)=1/8\).     Checking the theory

    Repetition changes information, not the fitted probabilities

    Repeat the entire eight-row binary example \(r\) times. Its log likelihood and information multiply by \(r\), but its maximizer is unchanged. Consequently

    \[\widehat\beta_1(r)=2\log3,\qquad \operatorname{SE}\{\widehat\beta_1(r)\}=\sqrt{\frac8{3r}},\qquad D(r)=rD(1).\]

    This is an information calculation for independent replicated responses with the same observed pattern. Copying the same flowers would not create new independent evidence.

    A stopping flag does not rule out separation

    Now take \(x=(-2,-1,1,2)\), \(y=(0,0,1,1)\). For intercept zero and positive slope \(b\), every observed class is on the correct side, and

    \[\ell(b)=-2\log(1+e^{-b})-2\log(1+e^{-2b})\longrightarrow0 \quad\text{as }b\longrightarrow\infty.\]

    Every finite \(b\) leaves \(\ell(b)<0\), proving that no finite MLE attains the supremum. The fitted path below remains numerically finite, but the rising slope and collapsing deviance show the degeneracy. The boundary flag and any convergence flag must be read together; neither the epsilon guard nor extra iterations repairs the statistical problem. Penalisation or bias reduction would define a different estimator.

    Iris: two overlapping species

    Fit probabilities on the original measurements

    dm::datasets::iris() contains 150 flowers, 50 from each species, with four measurements in centimetres. We retain the 100 versicolor and virginica flowers, encode virginica as one, and fit y ~ Petal.Length + Petal.Width. The response is an observed binary label, not a count made from a measurement. Setosa is excluded because its clean separation would turn this into the preceding nonexistence example rather than a regular coefficient analysis. These probabilities describe the balanced two-species sample; they do not establish prevalence or calibrated probabilities in another population.

    The following full-data fit is descriptive. Its curve holds petal width at 1.75 cm; each flower's fitted probability in the diagnostic panel uses its own length and width. The confidence band conditions on the model and the fixed query width. The full-data fit's boundary flag records extreme probabilities for some flowers. Both species still overlap near the boundary; that heuristic does not diagnose separation, and the finite coefficients agree with an independent R fit. Width 1.75 cm combined with very short petals lies away from much of the observed joint predictor support, so the curve's ends also illustrate conditional extrapolation.

    For a binary observation, the residual is \(\sqrt{-2\log\widehat p}\) when \(y=1\), and \(-\sqrt{-2\log(1-\widehat p)}\) when \(y=0\). The two curved branches are therefore algebraically required, not by themselves evidence of misspecification or normally distributed errors. A small training deviance does not estimate test accuracy.

    One prespecified train/test comparison

    We split once using train_test_split(...,0.3,42,"Species"), giving 35 training and 15 test flowers per species. The three candidates are fixed in advance: intercept only, two sepals, and two petals. No response or fitted model sees the test labels during training. Their AIC values are comparable because they use the same binary responses, rows and Bernoulli likelihood. We do not compare these to a Gaussian density AIC on a zero/one vector: a probability mass and a density use different reference measures.

    Test probabilities are assessed by accuracy at the fixed threshold 0.5 and two proper probability losses:

    \[\operatorname{Brier}=\frac1m\sum_i(\widehat p_i-y_i)^2,\qquad \operatorname{logloss}=-\frac1m\sum_i\{y_i\log\widehat p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\widehat p_i)\}.\]

    The petal model's 29 correct classifications exceed the sepal model's 22 on this split; its Brier loss 0.0254 and log loss 0.0852 are also lower. The training AIC ranking agrees here, but the test set was not used to choose formulas or thresholds.

    The score helper omits joint formula-null rows and reports their retained count. Its scores are unweighted, including if the model was trained with prior weights. These 30 test flowers give one small, reproducible assessment, not a precise estimate of future performance or a tuning set for selecting thresholds.

    A deliberate Poisson mismatch

    To retain the old example's count demonstration, define count = nearbyint(Sepal.Length) on all 150 flowers. These numbers are rounded centimetres, not counts of independently occurring events over a fixed exposure. A log-link fit can produce positive means, but that range property does not establish the Poisson variance assumption.

    For a correctly specified fixed-dispersion count model, Pearson chi-square divided by residual degrees of freedom is often used as a rough dispersion diagnostic. A very small ratio here reflects the restricted spread created by rounding lengths. Discrete bands in a residual plot are expected for a response with few distinct values; they do not by themselves establish adequacy. We show the ratio without estimating a free Poisson dispersion or using rounded measurements as biological count evidence.

    Conclusion

    The tiny group and count fits expose exactly what IRLS solves, while replication checks its information scaling. The separated sample demonstrates why a tiny deviance change cannot certify a finite logistic MLE. On iris, comparing models within a common Bernoulli likelihood and evaluating a separate test split gives interpretable probability scores. Mean-response confidence limits, training fit, numerical convergence and held-out prediction answer different questions. Neither a successful optimizer nor positive fitted means can validate an assumed response distribution; the rounded-length example makes that limit visible.
