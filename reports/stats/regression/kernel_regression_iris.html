    Kernel Regression: Local Weights, Bandwidth and Coverage

    Abstract

    A response may vary smoothly with predictors without following one global line or a chosen parametric curve. We want predictions that borrow information locally while showing when nearby observations are scarce. Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression forms nonnegative weighted response averages, with bandwidth setting their locality. Three-point calculations expose the weights, boundary bias and the distinction between ordinary and leave-one-out fits. Duplicate predictors prevent interpolation of every response as bandwidth vanishes. A compact-window example shows why bandwidths must not be ranked after silently dropping unsupported validation rows. We then check a known function and analyse iris petal length from petal width, separating full-data smoothing and conditional bandwidth tuning from an independent 120/30 train/test split. Coverage, local effective sample size and extrapolation behaviour accompany error summaries.

    The method

    A local constant minimizes a weighted least-squares problem

    For training predictors \(x_i\in\mathbb R^p\), responses \(y_i\) and query \(x\), choose a positive bandwidth \(h\) and nonnegative radial kernel. In the metric used by this implementation, \(u_i=\|z(x)-z(x_i)\|_2/h\), where \(z\) is the frozen resolved formula design, optionally standardized. Differentiating the local constant objective gives

    \[\hat m_h(x)=\arg\min_c\sum_i K(u_i)(y_i-c)^2 =\frac{\sum_iK(u_i)y_i}{\sum_iK(u_i)} =\sum_iw_i(x)y_i,\qquad w_i(x)=\frac{K(u_i)}{\sum_jK(u_j)}.\]

    If the denominator is positive, weights sum to one. Constants are reproduced exactly, and predictions lie between the smallest and largest observed response. This prevents ordinary local-constant regression from extending a linear trend beyond the observed response range. It does not reproduce every affine function: at a boundary, most available predictors lie to one side of the query. A Taylor expansion gives the first-order term \(m'(x)\sum_iw_i(x)(x_i-x)\), which need not vanish. Under regular one-dimensional sampling, symmetry gives interior bias of order \(h^2\); one-sided boundary weighting can leave bias of order \(h\). Local linear regression addresses that term, but it is a different estimator from the one fitted here.

    Kernel shape, units and scaling

    The four kernels use the Euclidean radius \(u\ge0\):

    \[K_G(u)=e^{-u^2/2},\quad K_E(u)=\tfrac34(1-u^2)\mathbf1_{u<1},\quad K_U(u)=\mathbf1_{u\le1},\quad K_T(u)=(1-u)\mathbf1_{u<1}.\]

    Multiplying a kernel by any positive normalizing constant does not change the average. Compact support is a ball in the resolved predictor space, not a product box across coordinates. Gaussian product kernels with a common bandwidth give the same weights as this radial Gaussian; compact product kernels generally do not.

    With standardization enabled, the fit uses training-column means and population SDs: \(z_{ij}=(X_{ij}-\bar X_j)/\sqrt{n^{-1}\sum_i(X_{ij}-\bar X_j)^2}\). Exact constant columns use scale one. The bandwidth is then dimensionless. With scaling disabled it is in raw design units; a single scalar is scientifically meaningful only with a defensible metric across columns. Formula transformations and dummy coding also change that metric. No intercept column is needed for local averaging.

    Training nulls in required predictors or the response are omitted jointly; retained values must be finite. kept_row_indices() preserves original zero-based row alignment. Prediction keeps every query row: missing/nonfinite predictors or empty compact windows give NaN and an explicit status in prediction_details(). Unknown categorical levels raise an error. A Gaussian has mathematical support everywhere, but support is not data proximity or a guarantee of useful extrapolation. Relative log weights preserve its normalized local mean even when all original exponentials would underflow.

    The smoother matrix and conditional complexity

    For a fixed design and fixed \(h\), ordinary predictions at training points satisfy \(\hat y=Sy\), where \(S_{ij}=w_j(x_i)\). Its rows sum to one, but S generally is neither symmetric nor an orthogonal projection. smoothing_matrix() exposes it. Diagonal entries measure own-response influence and \(\operatorname{tr}(S)\) is the conditional linear-smoother degrees of freedom. Bandwidth selection depends on responses, so this trace does not include the complexity cost of searching for h.

    For independent homoskedastic errors with variance \(\sigma^2\), fixed query weights give

    \[\operatorname{Var}(\hat m_h(x)\mid X)=\sigma^2\sum_iw_i(x)^2 =\frac{\sigma^2}{n_{\rm eff}(x)},\qquad n_{\rm eff}(x)=\frac1{\sum_iw_i(x)^2}.\]

    This is a concentration diagnostic, not a confidence interval: it does not estimate noise variance or account for smoothing bias, heteroskedasticity or tuning. Small local neighbourhoods and multiple predictors can make effective sample sizes small; under a regular p-dimensional design the number of local points is roughly proportional to \(nh^p\), expressing the dimensionality penalty.

    Deleting a response is different from fitting it

    At fixed preprocessing and bandwidth, removing only observation i yields

    \[\hat y_i^{(-i)}=\frac{\hat y_i-S_{ii}y_i}{1-S_{ii}},\qquad y_i-\hat y_i^{(-i)}=\frac{y_i-\hat y_i}{1-S_{ii}}.\]

    This identity requires positive remaining weight. Numerically, \(S_{ii}\) can round to one at tiny bandwidths even though a Gaussian deletion still has a well-defined relative weighted mean. The implementation therefore computes deletions directly, excluding the row before normalizing. fitted_values() retains its historical deletion meaning; full_fitted_values() explicitly exposes ordinary full-sample fits.

    With distinct predictor locations and positive self-weight, ordinary predictions tend toward interpolation as \(h\to0\). With duplicates they instead approach each duplicate location's response mean; deleting one flower retains the other flowers at that width. As \(h\to\infty\), full fits tend toward the global response mean and deletion fits toward the corresponding mean excluding each observation. A small apparent error is a description of the smoother, not a test-set performance estimate.

    Bandwidth selection must compare the same validation rows

    Negative bandwidth requests a search. The default grid has 50 log-spaced values from \(0.01\sqrt p\) to \(2\sqrt p\) in the chosen metric units; an explicit positive bandwidth_candidates vector overrides that grid. The first minimum in caller order wins. The grid is finite, not a global optimization guarantee, and boundary choices deserve inspection.

    For each candidate, bandwidth_diagnostics() reports deletion coverage, conditional MSE on finite predictions and a selection objective. The objective is the mean square error only when all n deletion predictions are finite; otherwise it is infinity. A wholly ineligible grid raises an error. This prevents a narrow compact kernel from winning by refusing its difficult rows. Fixed-bandwidth models may still have partial coverage; rmse() and r_squared() then describe the covered subset, and model_summary() shows its size. No coverage or zero response variation gives an undefined R-squared, not one.

    The full-training design, transforms and scaling stay fixed during these deletions. This is conditional response-deletion CV, not a refit of the whole preprocessing pipeline inside each fold. Its minimum is also optimistically selected on those same responses. The held-out iris analysis below fits all preprocessing and selects h using training rows only, then evaluates the untouched test rows. Its training-mean baseline uses no test responses to make predictions; evaluation R-squared uses test-response variation only as its stated denominator.

    Fixed-bandwidth training costs \(O(n^2p)\), a B-candidate search \(O(Bn^2p)\), and m new queries \(O(mnp)\). The model stores \(O(np+n+B)\) values. Explicit weight/smoother matrices add \(O(mn)\) or \(O(n^2)\) output storage; they are diagnostics rather than a required dense-matrix implementation of every prediction.

    Simple examples

    Three points reveal the exact weights

    Use \(x=(-1,0,1)\), \(y=(0,1,0)\), raw units and Gaussian \(h=1\). Let \(a=e^{-1/2}\), \(b=e^{-2}\). The ordinary smoother matrix is

    \[S=\begin{pmatrix} 1/(1+a+b)&a/(1+a+b)&b/(1+a+b)\\ a/(1+2a)&1/(1+2a)&a/(1+2a)\\ b/(1+a+b)&a/(1+a+b)&1/(1+a+b) \end{pmatrix}.\]

    Thus \(\hat y_1=1/(1+2a)\) at the central point, whereas its deleted prediction is zero because both remaining responses are zero. At an endpoint the ordinary fit is \(a/(1+a+b)\), while deletion gives \(a/(a+b)\). The full matrix is row-stochastic, but its differently normalized rows demonstrate why it is not generally symmetric.

    Boundary bias and limited extrapolation

    Now take \(x=y=(0,1,2)\), Gaussian \(h=1\), without standardization. The true line passes through every observation, but the ordinary fit at zero is \((a+2b)/(1+a+b)>0\). At one, symmetric weights reproduce one. Far to the right, relative Gaussian weights concentrate on the largest predictor and the mean tends to two; far left it tends to zero. This is a stable prediction rule with poor linear extrapolation, not evidence that the response is known precisely outside the data.

    Duplicates and incomplete validation coverage

    Use \(x=(0,0,10)\), \(y=(0,2,100)\) and a raw-unit uniform window. At \(h=0.1\), ordinary predictions at x=0 average the two responses to one; they cannot interpolate both. Deleting one duplicate leaves the other, giving predictions two and zero. Deleting the isolated x=10 row leaves no support. Conditional deletion MSE is only four on two rows, so silently omitting the third row would make this candidate look excellent.

    At \(h=11\), every deletion has both remaining observations available. Predictions are 51, 50 and 1, giving full-coverage MSE \((51^2+48^2+99^2)/3=4902\). The objective correctly treats the incomplete h=0.1 candidate as ineligible, rather than pretending that four and 4902 measure the same validation experiment.

    Checking the theory on a known function

    We fix 31 equally spaced predictor locations in [0,1], the target \(m(x)=\sin(2\pi x)\) and deterministic contamination \(0.2\cos(20\pi x)\). This is a transparent finite-design stress check, not a random-noise Monte Carlo experiment or an estimate of statistical bias and variance. Training responses contain the oscillation, while the dense reference grid uses the known smooth target. Every candidate uses the same raw-coordinate bandwidth, training design and complete Gaussian deletion coverage.

    We compare apparent RMSE, conditional deletion RMSE against contaminated training responses, and dense-grid RMSE against the true function. They are three different targets. The selected bandwidth minimizes the declared deletion criterion, not the withheld function error; their rankings need not agree. The same fixed data also show the deterioration from excessive smoothing and the attraction of apparent interpolation at tiny h.

    Iris petal measurements

    Data and a full-data smoothing illustration

    dm::datasets::iris() has 150 complete flowers, 50 per species, four measurements in centimetres and a species label. We use the numeric formula Petal.Length ~ Petal.Width. Species helps describe the sample and stratify the later split; it is not a predictor. Petal width takes repeated recorded values, so the distinct-location interpolation argument does not apply to every response separately.

    The next fit uses all 150 rows to illustrate the curve, training-only standardization, bandwidth path and local influence. Its conditional deletion RMSE is a training selection diagnostic. Because h is chosen using these same deletion losses, the minimum is not an independent estimate of the selected model's test performance. The bandwidth plot uses log10 h and only finite full-coverage objectives.

    Train first, then evaluate untouched flowers

    We prespecify a 120/30 split using train_test_split(iris,.2,42,"Species"), leaving ten test flowers per species. The Gaussian width-only model is the primary procedure. Three other kernels on that same predictor, and a Gaussian width-plus-sepal-length model, are declared sensitivity comparisons. Each learns its means/scales and searches its own bandwidth grid on the training frame only. Test outcomes do not choose h, predictors or kernel. Comparing their test scores is descriptive on one small shared split, not a stable ranking of methods across populations.

    The baseline predicts the training response mean for every test flower. score() reports coverage, RMSE, MAE and R-squared, with errors conditional on available predictions. We retain coverage in every comparison because full training-deletion coverage does not ensure a compact kernel covers every future query. The historical species-stratified sample is not a random treatment experiment or a general population prevalence sample.

    Local concentration and extrapolation

    Finally inspect the full-data Gaussian model over a wider width range. Smooth-looking curves can hide sparse support. nearest_distance is the distance to the closest training point in the frozen standardized design; effective_n measures weight concentration. Neither supplies an uncertainty interval or validates predictions for biologically unrepresented flowers. A compact uniform model is included only to expose its explicit no-support status on the same selected query rows, with its own training-selected h.

    Conclusion

    Kernel regression is a local-constant weighted average, not a guarantee of interpolation, linear extrapolation or calibrated uncertainty. The small examples make its row-normalized weights, leverage, duplicate-location behaviour and deletion coverage inspectable. Bandwidth selection must use the same validation targets for every candidate. A known function separates apparent, deletion and reference errors; iris then separates training selection from a genuinely held-out evaluation. Coverage and local concentration remain part of the result even when an aggregate RMSE looks attractive.
