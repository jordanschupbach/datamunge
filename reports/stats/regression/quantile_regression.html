    Quantile Regression: Modelling the Whole Conditional Distribution

    Abstract

    Least squares estimates how the mean of a response changes with the predictors. Quantile regression estimates how any chosen quantile changes: the 10th percentile, the median, the 90th. This report derives the estimator from the check loss, writes it as a linear program and derives its dual. It then solves the program with an exact simplex, which the library now uses by default, and with ADMM. Hand examples show the counting rule behind a sample quantile, and the ten candidate lines through five points. They also show that the optimal line is a step function of \(\tau\), and that it is not always unique. On a cloud whose spread fans out with \(x\), the fitted slopes rise from 1.37 at \(\tau=0.1\) to 2.79 at \(\tau=0.9\), against true values of 1.36 and 2.64, while least squares reports only the middle. One extreme response moves the least-squares slope from 1.50 to 131 and leaves the median slope at exactly 1.50. A point of high leverage breaks both. The theory checks out to rounding: exactly \(n\tau-1\) points lie below each fit, and the primal and dual objectives agree. The simplex needs 5 to 8 pivots for up to 20 000 rows. ADMM stops at its 10 000-iteration cap from 1000 rows up, with losses \(2\times10^{-9}\) to \(4\times10^{-8}\) above the optimum. In 500 simulated samples the IID standard error is 16–17% too small and its 95% intervals cover 86–89% of the time; the NID sandwich and the bootstrap cover 94–96%. On the New York ozone data a degree Fahrenheit raises the 10th percentile of ozone by 1.4 ppb, the median by 2.1 and the 75th percentile by 2.5. The library also gained the dual solution and IID, NID and bootstrap standard errors, plus a quantile-process helper. A thin QR replaced a factorisation whose memory grew as \(n^2\). Across 120 fits with ties, the simplex reached scikit-learn's linear-programming optimum to \(10^{-15}\).

    The problem

    Least squares fits the conditional mean \(\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]\). Often the question is about another part of the distribution. A drug may help the sickest patients more than the typical patient. Peak ozone, not average ozone, breaks the health standard. The spread of the response may grow with the predictor, so that one line through the middle hides how the tails move. Quantile regression (Koenker and Bassett, 1978) fits the conditional quantile

    \[Q_Y(\tau\mid x)=\inf\{y:\Pr(Y\le y\mid X=x)\ge\tau\},\qquad 0<\tau<1,\]

    as a linear function \(x^\top\beta(\tau)\), one \(\tau\) at a time. The median is \(\tau=\tfrac12\). Fitting a grid of values of \(\tau\) describes the whole conditional distribution without assuming normal errors or a constant variance. This report derives the estimator and the linear program behind it, solves that program two ways, derives and checks the standard errors, and applies the method to the New York ozone data.

    The method

    The check loss and the sample quantile

    The mean of a sample minimises the sum of squared deviations. The \(\tau\)-quantile minimises a sum of asymmetric absolute deviations,

    \[\hat q_\tau=\arg\min_q\sum_{i=1}^n\rho_\tau(y_i-q),\qquad \rho_\tau(r)=r\bigl(\tau-\mathbf 1\{r<0\}\bigr)=\begin{cases}\tau r,&r\ge0,\\(\tau-1)r,&r<0.\end{cases}\]

    A point above the fit costs \(\tau\) per unit and a point below costs \(1-\tau\). The objective is convex and piecewise linear in \(q\), with a corner at every \(y_i\). Moving \(q\) up by a small amount raises the cost of the points at or below \(q\) and lowers that of the points above it, so the right and left derivatives are

    \[D^+(q)=(1-\tau)N_\le(q)-\tau\bigl(n-N_\le(q)\bigr)=N_\le(q)-n\tau,\qquad D^-(q)=N_<(q)-n\tau,\]

    where \(N_\le(q)\) and \(N_<(q)\) count the observations at most \(q\) and below \(q\). A minimiser needs \(D^-(q)\le0\le D^+(q)\), that is

    \[N_<(q)\le n\tau\le N_\le(q).\]

    At most a fraction \(\tau\) of the sample lies below \(q\) and at least a fraction \(\tau\) lies at or below it: \(q\) is a sample \(\tau\)-quantile. When \(n\tau\) is not an integer the minimiser is the unique order statistic \(y_{(\lceil n\tau\rceil)}\). When it is an integer, every \(q\) between \(y_{(n\tau)}\) and \(y_{(n\tau+1)}\) is a minimiser. With weights \(w_i\) the counts become sums of weights.

    Regression quantiles

    Replacing the constant \(q\) by \(x_i^\top\beta\) gives the regression quantile

    \[\hat\beta(\tau)=\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb R^p}\sum_{i=1}^nw_i\rho_\tau\bigl(y_i-x_i^\top\beta\bigr).\]

    The population version justifies it. If \(Q_Y(\tau\mid x)=x^\top\beta(\tau)\), the derivative of \(\mathbb E\,\rho_\tau(Y-x^\top b)\) in \(b\) is \(\mathbb E\bigl[x\bigl(\Pr(Y<x^\top b\mid x)-\tau\bigr)\bigr]\), which vanishes at \(b=\beta(\tau)\), and the objective is convex. Nothing is assumed about the shape of the errors or about how their spread depends on \(x\). Two properties follow from the definition. The fit is equivariant: replacing \(y\) by \(ay+X\gamma\) with \(a>0\) gives \(a\hat\beta(\tau)+\gamma\). And conditional quantiles commute with increasing transformations, \(Q_{h(Y)}(\tau\mid x)=h(Q_Y(\tau\mid x))\), which conditional means do not.

    A linear program and its dual

    Split each residual into its positive and negative parts, \(y_i-x_i^\top\beta=u_i-v_i\) with \(u_i,v_i\ge0\). The fit is the linear program

    \[\min_{\beta,u,v}\;\sum_iw_i\bigl(\tau u_i+(1-\tau)v_i\bigr)\quad\text{subject to}\quad X\beta+u-v=y,\;u\ge0,\;v\ge0.\]

    With a multiplier \(w_ia_i\) for row \(i\) of the constraint, the Lagrangian is \(\sum_iw_i\bigl[(\tau-a_i)u_i+(1-\tau+a_i)v_i+a_iy_i\bigr]-\beta^\top X^\top Wa\). It is bounded below in \(\beta\) only if \(X^\top Wa=0\), and in \(u\) and \(v\) only if \(\tau-1\le a_i\le\tau\). The dual is therefore

    \[\max_a\;\sum_iw_ia_iy_i\quad\text{subject to}\quad X^\top Wa=0,\quad\tau-1\le a_i\le\tau .\]

    Complementary slackness says \(a_i=\tau\) for a point above the fit and \(a_i=\tau-1\) for a point below it; only points on the fit take values in between. At the optimum the two objectives are equal. The shifted values \(a_i+1-\tau\in[0,1]\) are Koenker's regression rank scores.

    A linear program attains its optimum at a vertex. Here a vertex is a set \(h\) of \(p\) rows with an invertible submatrix \(X_h\), and \(\hat\beta=X_h^{-1}y_h\): the fitted plane passes exactly through \(p\) of the points. The counting rule for the sample quantile carries over. Write \(a_i=\tau-b_i\), with \(b_i=1\) below the fit, \(b_i=0\) above it and \(b_i\in[0,1]\) on it. With an intercept, the first row of \(X^\top a=0\) (unit weights) is \(\sum_ia_i=n\tau-N_--\sum_{\text{on}}b_i=0\), so

    \[N_-\le n\tau\le N_-+N_0 ,\]

    where \(N_-\) and \(N_0\) count the points below and on the fit. With \(N_0=p\), the share of points below the fitted plane is within \(p/n\) of \(\tau\).

    An exact simplex

    The library's default solver walks from vertex to vertex. At a vertex \(h\) with residuals \(r=y-X\hat\beta\), let \(d_j=X_h^{-1}e_j\). Moving to \(\hat\beta+td_j\) keeps every basis row except \(h_j\) on the fit, and moves the fit at row \(h_j\) by \(t\). Along that edge the loss is

    \[f_j(t)=\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau(r_i-tc_i),\qquad c_i=x_i^\top d_j ,\]

    convex and piecewise linear, with a corner at each \(t_i=r_i/c_i\). As \(t\to-\infty\) its slope is \(-\sum_iw_i\lvert c_i\rvert\kappa_i\), with \(\kappa_i=\tau\) if \(c_i>0\) and \(1-\tau\) otherwise, and crossing corner \(t_i\) raises the slope by \(w_i\lvert c_i\rvert\). The minimiser is the first corner, in sorted order, at which the slope turns non-negative: a weighted quantile of the \(t_i\), found by one sort. The solver evaluates every edge, takes the one that lowers the loss most, swaps the row at its minimising corner into the basis, and repeats.

    It stops when no edge lowers the loss, and that is a proof of optimality. Using \(X^\top Wa=0\), the right and left derivatives of \(f_j\) at \(t=0\) are \(w_{h_j}(a_{h_j}+1-\tau)\) and \(w_{h_j}(a_{h_j}-\tau)\). Neither edge direction descends exactly when \(\tau-1\le a_{h_j}\le\tau\), the dual feasibility of the vertex. The basis duals solve \(X_h^\top W_ha_h=-\sum_{i\notin h}w_ia_ix_i\), with \(a_i\) fixed by the sign of \(r_i\) off the basis.

    Two details make this robust. The start is the \(p\) linearly independent rows nearest the least-squares fit, so that few pivots are needed. Ties in the data, such as rounded responses or repeated rows, put more than \(p\) points on a fit. At such a degenerate vertex an edge can have zero length, and the descent can stall short of the optimum. The solver therefore walks on responses perturbed by a fixed pseudo-random amount of relative size \(10^{-9}\), which removes the ties. At the final basis it recomputes \(\hat\beta\) from the unperturbed responses and certifies it: the basis duals must lie in \([\tau-1,\tau]\), and every point clearly off the fit must have the sign its dual value claims. One pivot costs \(O(pn\log n)\).

    ADMM

    The alternating direction method of multipliers (Boyd et al., 2011) is the alternative. It splits off the residual, \(\min\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau(r_i)\) subject to \(y-X\beta-r=0\), and with a penalty \(\varrho>0\) and scaled dual \(u\) iterates

    \[\beta\leftarrow\arg\min_\beta\lVert y-r+u-X\beta\rVert_2^2,\qquad r_i\leftarrow\operatorname{prox}_i(y_i-x_i^\top\beta+u_i),\qquad u\leftarrow u+y-X\beta-r .\]

    The first step is least squares with a fixed design, done with one thin QR factorisation. The second minimises \(w_i\rho_\tau(r)+\tfrac\varrho2(r-v)^2\), whose solution is an asymmetric soft threshold: \(r=v-w_i\tau/\varrho\) if \(v>w_i\tau/\varrho\), \(r=v+w_i(1-\tau)/\varrho\) if \(v<-w_i(1-\tau)/\varrho\), and \(r=0\) in between. At a fixed point, \(\varrho u_i\) is a subgradient of \(w_i\rho_\tau\) at \(r_i\), so \(\varrho u_i/w_i\) estimates the dual \(a_i\). Each iteration costs \(O(np)\), but convergence on this non-smooth problem is slow, and the answer is only as accurate as the stopping tolerances.

    Standard errors

    Under regularity conditions (Koenker, 2005, section 4.2),

    \[\sqrt n\bigl(\hat\beta(\tau)-\beta(\tau)\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}N\bigl(0,\;\tau(1-\tau)H^{-1}JH^{-1}\bigr),\qquad J=\mathbb E[xx^\top],\quad H=\mathbb E\bigl[f_x(x^\top\beta(\tau))\,xx^\top\bigr],\]

    where \(f_x\) is the conditional density of \(Y\) given \(x\). A sketch: the estimator nearly solves \(n^{-1/2}\sum_ix_i(\tau-\mathbf 1\{y_i<x_i^\top\hat\beta\})=0\). The summands at the true \(\beta\) have mean zero and covariance \(\tau(1-\tau)J\), and a small change \(\delta\) in \(\beta\) shifts their mean by \(-H\delta\), since \(\Pr(Y<x^\top(\beta+\delta)\mid x)\approx\tau+f_x\,x^\top\delta\). Solving for \(\delta\) gives the sandwich. The density at the quantile is unknown and is estimated in one of three ways.

    • IID. If the errors are independent of \(x\), \(f_x\) is one number \(f\), and the covariance reduces to \(\tau(1-\tau)s^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) with the sparsity \(s=1/f(F^{-1}(\tau))\). The sparsity is the slope of the quantile function of the residuals, estimated over \(\tau\pm h\).
    • NID (Hendricks and Koenker, 1992). The slope of the conditional quantile function in \(\tau\) is \(1/f_x\), so \(\hat f_i=2h/x_i^\top\bigl(\hat\beta(\tau+h)-\hat\beta(\tau-h)\bigr)\), clamped at zero, and the covariance is \(\tau(1-\tau)\hat H^{-1}\hat J\hat H^{-1}\) with \(\hat H=\sum_i\hat f_ix_ix_i^\top\) and \(\hat J=\sum_ix_ix_i^\top\). This allows the spread to depend on \(x\).
    • Bootstrap. Resample the \(n\) rows with replacement, refit, and take the standard deviation of the refitted coefficients.

    The bandwidth is Hall and Sheather's (1988) \(h=n^{-1/3}z_{0.975}^{2/3}\bigl(1.5\,\phi(z_\tau)^2/(2z_\tau^2+1)\bigr)^{1/3}\) with \(z_\tau=\Phi^{-1}(\tau)\), the default of R's quantreg. Intervals are estimate \(\pm\,t_{n-p,0.975}\) standard errors.

    Goodness of fit

    Squared-error \(R^2\) belongs to the mean. Koenker and Machado (1999) compare the loss with that of the intercept-only fit at the same \(\tau\):

    \[R^1(\tau)=1-\frac{\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau\bigl(y_i-x_i^\top\hat\beta(\tau)\bigr)}{\sum_iw_i\rho_\tau(y_i-\hat q_\tau)} .\]

    It lies in \([0,1]\) and measures the improvement at that quantile, not a share of variance.

    In the library

    stats::QuantileRegression(data, "y ~ x1 + x2", {.quantile = tau}) resolves the formula like stats::LM and fits by the simplex; {.method = QuantileRegressionMethod::ADMM} selects ADMM. basis() gives the interpolated rows and dual_solution() the dual \(\hat a\). coefficient_table(method, level) gives IID, NID or bootstrap standard errors with t tests and intervals. stats::quantile_process(data, formula, taus) fits a grid of \(\tau\), and check_loss, hall_sheather_bandwidth and bofinger_bandwidth are free functions.

    Each line's loss is linear in \(\tau\). With residuals \(r_i\), \(\sum_i\rho_\tau(r_i)=A+B\tau\) with \(A=\sum_{r_i<0}\lvert r_i\rvert\) and \(B=\sum_ir_i\). For the line through points 1 and 5, \(y=0.25+0.75x\), the residuals are \(0,1.25,-0.5,1.75,0\), so \(A=0.5\), \(B=2.5\), and the loss is 1.125 at \(\tau=0.25\) and 1.75 at \(\tau=0.5\). The regression quantile at each \(\tau\) is the lowest of the ten lines \(A+B\tau\). The table shows that the line through 1 and 5 is lowest at both levels, so the two fits are the same line. The lower envelope has three pieces. For \(\tau<0.2\) the best loss is \(5\tau\), from the line through 1 and 3, which never has a point below it. At \(\tau=0.2\) it meets \(0.5+2.5\tau\), the line through 1 and 5. At \(\tau=0.6\) three lines tie at loss 2, and above it the line through 2 and 4 is lowest. The simplex, run at the middle of each range, returns the same lines. The regression quantile is therefore a step function of \(\tau\), constant between the levels at which the optimal basis changes.

    It is not always unique. The lines through 1 and 3 and through 3 and 5 have the same \(A=0\) and \(B=5\), so they tie at every \(\tau<0.2\). In fact every line through the point \((3,2)\) with a slope between 0.5 and 1 keeps the other four points on or above it, and its loss is then \(\tau\) times the sum of its residuals, which is 5 for all such lines. The whole range of slopes is optimal, and the simplex reports one end of it.

    The dual values at \(\tau=0.25\) follow the rules derived above. Points 2 and 4 lie above the fit, so \(\hat a=\tau=0.25\), and point 3 lies below it, so \(\hat a=\tau-1=-0.75\). The two points on the fit share the rest, \(0.125\) each, inside \([-0.75,0.25]\). By hand, \(\sum\hat a_i=0\), \(\sum\hat a_ix_i=0.125+0.5-2.25+1+0.625=0\), and \(\sum\hat a_iy_i=0.125+0.75-1.5+1.25+0.5=1.125\), equal to the loss. No pivot was needed: the least-squares line \(y=0.6+0.8x\) is nearest to points 1 and 5 (residuals \(-0.4\) and \(-0.6\)), and that start was already optimal. In the left panel the constant fits behave as the counting rule says. With \(n=5\), \(n\tau\) is 1.25, 2.5 and 3.75, so the minimisers are the 2nd, 3rd and 4th order statistics, 2, 3 and 4.

For larger \(n\) it stops at the iteration cap with a loss \(2\times10^{-9}\) to \(4\times10^{-8}\) above the optimum, and slopes that differ from the exact ones in the fifth decimal. A pivot sorts \(n\) breakpoints for each of the \(p\) edges, so 8 pivots cost about as much as a few hundred passes over the data. ADMM's 10 000 iterations are 10 000 such passes. The figure shows why ADMM is slow here. The gap after 1000 iterations is \(9.6\times10^{-6}\) for \(\varrho=0.1\), \(2.5\times10^{-6}\) for \(\varrho=1\), and \(3.1\times10^{-4}\) for \(\varrho=10\). The small penalty makes fast progress and then oscillates, and the large one moves slowly. After 10 000 iterations all three are within \(2\times10^{-8}\) to \(5\times10^{-8}\) of the optimum, and none is exact. For this reason the library's default is now the simplex. ADMM remains available, for example as a starting point for penalised variants. Its default penalty \(\varrho=1\) was the best of the three after 1000 iterations.

    Outside this report, the simplex was compared with scikit-learn's QuantileRegressor, which solves the same linear program with HiGHS. On 40 random data sets with \(p=1\) to 5 predictors, heavy-tailed errors and rounded values that create ties, at three levels each, the two losses agreed to a relative \(8\times10^{-16}\) in all 120 fits. The coefficients agreed to \(10^{-8}\) except on one data set with rounded predictors and integer \(n\tau\), where the optimum is a whole face and the two solvers report different points of it. Before the tie-breaking perturbation was added, the simplex stalled at degenerate vertices in 8 of those 120 fits. The certificate flagged all 8 as not optimal.

    New York ozone

    R's airquality data has daily ozone concentrations (parts per billion, at Roosevelt Island) and weather for New York from May to September 1973. Of the 153 days, 116 have an ozone reading and 111 have ozone, temperature, wind and solar radiation. Ozone forms in sunlight and heat, and its spread grows with temperature.

At \(\tau=0.9\) the NID interval is the wider one. Both data columns are integers, so many residuals tie, and the density estimates behind NID become unstable. At this sample size neither interval should be read to two digits. Both methods do agree that the slope at \(\tau=0.1\) is lower than at \(\tau=0.75\). Their intervals barely overlap: 2.27 against 2.05 for NID, and 2.33 against 2.13 for the bootstrap. The \(R^1\) values, 0.16 to 0.41, measure improvement over a constant quantile and are not comparable with the least-squares \(R^2\) of 0.488. The fitted lines also go below zero at low temperatures, which is impossible for ozone. Worse, the 0.1 and 0.25 lines cross at 65.25 °F, inside the data: below that temperature the fitted 25th percentile lies under the fitted 10th. Each \(\tau\) is fitted separately, and nothing in the method prevents this. A linear quantile model is only a good description where it has many points.

    Conclusion

    Quantile regression replaces squared error by the check loss and so estimates a conditional quantile instead of the mean. The resulting fit is a linear program. Its solutions interpolate \(p\) points, leave \(n\tau\) points below them up to \(p\), and change only at isolated values of \(\tau\). Its dual gives a certificate of optimality, and its dual values are the regression rank scores. On data whose spread depends on the predictors, the fits at different \(\tau\) recover the different slopes of the true quantiles, which least squares averages away. The median is resistant to wild responses, but like every fit that passes through data points, not to a single point of high leverage.

    Two practical lessons came from the checks. An exact simplex started near the least-squares fit needs only a handful of pivots and is certified optimal. ADMM, the previous default, stopped at its iteration cap from 1000 rows up, and the library now uses the simplex. Ties need care: they made the plain simplex stall, and a fixed tiny perturbation with a final check on the original data fixed that. For inference, the IID standard error assumes a density that does not depend on \(x\), and it undercovered badly when that failed. NID and the bootstrap held their nominal 95%. On the ozone data they disagreed at \(n=116\) with integer-valued data, a reminder that tail quantiles need many observations. The method's main limitation is that each \(\tau\) is fitted separately. Nothing stops two fitted quantile lines from crossing, and on the ozone data the 10th- and 25th-percentile lines crossed inside the range of the data.
