    Linear Mixed Models: Partial Pooling for Grouped Data

    Abstract

    Grouped data, such as pupils in schools or repeated measurements on the same child, break the independence that ordinary regression assumes. Pooling every row into one least-squares fit gets the standard errors wrong, and fitting each group separately gives noisy estimates for small groups. A linear mixed model treats the group effects as random draws with an estimated variance, which decides how far each group's estimate is pulled towards the population: partial pooling. We derive the model's marginal covariance, generalised least squares, the profiled ML and REML criteria that datamunge's stats::LMM minimises, and the best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs). On a balanced one-way design of twelve numbers, the library reproduces the ANOVA closed forms for both ML and REML to six decimals, and the BLUPs equal the group means shrunk by the reliability \(n\tau^2/(n\tau^2+\sigma^2)\), exactly, for unequal group sizes too. Simulations confirm the theory: REML removes ML's downward bias in the group variance; the estimates that land on zero are exactly the data sets the \(F\) statistic predicts; OLS reports a standard error 2.4 times too small for a group-level covariate (95% intervals cover 59% of the time); BLUPs beat group means for every group size; and the likelihood-ratio test for a zero variance is far more conservative than \(\chi^2_1\) or even the 50:50 boundary mixture suggest. On children's orthodontic growth the fit agrees with R's nlme to seven digits; boys grow 0.30 mm/year faster than girls (\(p=0.024\)), and OLS misstates every standard error, by up to a factor of 1.7.

    The problem

    Many data sets come in groups: pupils in schools, repeated measurements on the same patient, plots in the same field. Observations in one group share influences that the covariates do not record, so they are more alike than observations from different groups. Two simple analyses both go wrong. Ordinary least squares on all the rows (complete pooling) treats the observations as independent. Its estimates are still unbiased, but its standard errors are too small for anything that varies between groups, because \(n\) correlated rows carry less information than \(n\) independent ones. Fitting each group separately (no pooling) respects the grouping, but a group with two observations gets an estimate as noisy as two observations allow, and nothing can be said about a group that was not sampled.

    A linear mixed model treats the group effects as draws from a population with a variance that is estimated from the data. The variance decides how far each group's estimate is pulled towards the overall mean: a lot when groups barely differ or the group is small, hardly at all when groups differ a lot or the group is large. This is partial pooling.

    The method

    The model

    Observations \(y_{gi}\), \(i=1,\dots,n_g\), in groups \(g=1,\dots,G\), with \(n=\sum_gn_g\). Each row has fixed-effect covariates \(x_{gi}\in\mathbb R^p\) and random-effect covariates \(z_{gi}\in\mathbb R^q\) (for a random intercept \(z_{gi}=1\); for a random intercept and slope in age, \(z_{gi}=(1,\text{age}_{gi})\)):

    \[y_g=X_g\beta+Z_gb_g+\varepsilon_g,\qquad b_g\sim N(0,\Sigma),\qquad \varepsilon_g\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_{n_g}),\]

    independently across groups. \(\beta\) is the population (fixed) effect, the same for everyone; \(b_g\) is group \(g\)'s deviation from it. In the formula language this is y ~ x + (1 + age | subject): the terms left of | are the columns of \(Z\), and the variable on the right defines the groups.

    Integrating out \(b_g\) gives the marginal model. The rows of one group are jointly normal with

    \[y_g\sim N\big(X_g\beta,\ V_g\big),\qquad V_g=\sigma^2I+Z_g\Sigma Z_g^\top .\]

    For a random intercept alone, \(\Sigma=\tau^2\) and \(V_g=\sigma^2I+\tau^2\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\): every pair of rows in a group has correlation \(\rho=\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)\), the intraclass correlation, and rows in different groups are independent.

    Generalised least squares, given the variances

    If \(V\) (block diagonal, blocks \(V_g\)) were known, the best linear unbiased estimator of \(\beta\) would be generalised least squares,

    \[\hat\beta=(X^\top V^{-1}X)^{-1}X^\top V^{-1}y,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)=(X^\top V^{-1}X)^{-1}.\]

    OLS is the special case \(V=\sigma^2I\). With correlated rows, OLS is still unbiased but no longer efficient, and its usual variance formula is wrong. For a covariate \(w\) that is constant within groups, with \(m\) rows per group and a random intercept, the OLS formula understates the variance of \(\hat\beta_w\) by the design effect \(1+(m-1)\rho\): the \(m\) rows of one group carry roughly one group's worth of information about \(w\).

    Estimating the variances: profiled maximum likelihood

    Write \(\Sigma=\sigma^2\Lambda\Lambda^\top\) with \(\Lambda\) lower triangular, and collect its \(q(q+1)/2\) entries into \(\theta\) (row by row). \(\theta\) is the random-effect covariance relative to the residual variance; for a random intercept, \(\theta=\tau/\sigma\). Then \(V_g=\sigma^2M_g\) with \(M_g=I+Z_g\Lambda\Lambda^\top Z_g^\top\), which depends on \(\theta\) only. The log-likelihood of the marginal model is

    \[-2\ell(\beta,\sigma^2,\theta)=n\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\sum_g\log|M_g|+\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_g(y_g-X_g\beta)^\top M_g^{-1}(y_g-X_g\beta).\]

    For fixed \(\theta\), the minimiser over \(\beta\) is GLS with \(M\) in place of \(V\) (the factor \(\sigma^2\) cancels), and the minimiser over \(\sigma^2\) is \(\hat\sigma^2_\theta=r^\top M^{-1}r/n\) with \(r=y-X\hat\beta_\theta\). Substituting both leaves a function of \(\theta\) alone, the profiled deviance

    \[d_{\mathrm{ML}}(\theta)=n\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2_\theta)+n+\sum_g\log|M_g(\theta)|,\]

    which has only \(q(q+1)/2\) arguments: one for a random intercept, three for an intercept and slope. LMM::deviance_at(theta) evaluates it.

    REML

    The ML estimate of \(\sigma^2\) divides by \(n\), ignoring that \(p\) degrees of freedom were spent on \(\hat\beta\), just as \(\frac1n\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\) underestimates a variance. The variance components inherit the same bias, and with few groups it is large. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) maximises the likelihood of \(n-p\) error contrasts \(K^\top y\) with \(K^\top X=0\), which do not depend on \(\beta\). Up to a constant, its profiled criterion is

    \[d_{\mathrm{REML}}(\theta)=(n-p)\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2_\theta)+(n-p)+\sum_g\log|M_g|+\log|X^\top M^{-1}X|, \qquad \hat\sigma^2_\theta=\frac{r^\top M^{-1}r}{n-p}.\]

    The extra term \(\log|X^\top M^{-1}X|\) is the penalty for estimating \(\beta\). REML is the default (LMMOptions::reml, as in lme4 and nlme). Two REML fits can be compared by their criteria only when they have the same fixed effects; to compare fixed effects, fit by ML.

    The balanced one-way model in closed form

    With a random intercept only, \(G\) groups of equal size \(m\) and no covariates, everything is explicit in terms of the one-way ANOVA mean squares

    \[\text{MSB}=\frac{m\sum_g(\bar y_g-\bar y)^2}{G-1},\qquad \text{MSW}=\frac{\sum_g\sum_i(y_{gi}-\bar y_g)^2}{G(m-1)} .\]

    Since \(E[\text{MSW}]=\sigma^2\) and \(E[\text{MSB}]=\sigma^2+m\tau^2\), the estimates are (Searle, Casella and McCulloch, 1992, chapter 3)

    \[\hat\beta_0=\bar y,\qquad \hat\sigma^2=\text{MSW},\qquad \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}}=\frac{\text{MSB}-\text{MSW}}{m},\qquad \hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=\frac{\tfrac{G-1}{G}\text{MSB}-\text{MSW}}{m},\]

    when these are positive, and \(\hat\tau^2=0\) otherwise. The REML estimate is the unbiased ANOVA estimator. ML shrinks MSB by \((G-1)/G\), which is a large bias with few groups. The standard error of the intercept is \(\sqrt{\text{MSB}/(mG)}\) under REML: the variance of a mean of \(G\) independent group means, each with variance \(\tau^2+\sigma^2/m\).

    Under \(\tau^2=0\), \(F=\text{MSB}/\text{MSW}\) has an \(F_{G-1,\,G(m-1)}\) distribution, and in general \(F/(1+m\tau^2/\sigma^2)\) does. So the probability that the estimate lands on the boundary is known exactly:

    \[P(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}}=0)=F_{G-1,G(m-1)}\Big(\frac{1}{1+m\tau^2/\sigma^2}\Big),\qquad P(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=0)=F_{G-1,G(m-1)}\Big(\frac{G/(G-1)}{1+m\tau^2/\sigma^2}\Big).\]

    Predicting the group effects: BLUPs

    The random effects are not parameters, but given the data they have a conditional distribution. Its mean, with the estimates plugged in, is the best linear unbiased predictor (Robinson, 1991)

    \[\hat b_g=\Sigma Z_g^\top V_g^{-1}(y_g-X_g\hat\beta)=\Lambda\Lambda^\top Z_g^\top M_g^{-1}r_g .\]

    For a random intercept, \(\mathbf 1^\top V_g^{-1}=\mathbf 1^\top/(\sigma^2+n_g\tau^2)\), so

    \[\hat b_g=\frac{n_g\tau^2}{n_g\tau^2+\sigma^2}\,\big(\bar y_g-\bar x_g^\top\hat\beta\big).\]

    The group's own mean residual is multiplied by a shrinkage factor between 0 and 1: the reliability of a mean of \(n_g\) observations. A large or very distinct group keeps nearly all of its own estimate; a small group in a population of similar groups is pulled to the overall line. With known variances, this weighted average of no pooling (factor 1) and complete pooling (factor 0) has the smallest mean squared prediction error of any linear predictor. With the variances estimated from the data it is an empirical Bayes estimator, in the same family as the James–Stein estimator.

    Computation

    stats::LMM parses the formula, builds \(X\) (dummy columns for factors, products for interactions) and \(Z\), and minimises the profiled criterion over \(\theta\). A global search (differential evolution, seeded, de_population_size × de_max_generations) covers a box with the diagonal entries of \(\Lambda\) in \([0,b]\) and the others in \([-b,b]\), where \(b\) is theta_bound (default 5; raise it if a group SD may exceed five residual SDs). Nelder–Mead then polishes the best point, and the signs of \(\Lambda\)'s columns are fixed so that its diagonal is non-negative. Each evaluation needs one Cholesky factorisation of each \(n_g\times n_g\) block \(M_g\), so the cost is linear in the number of groups. At the optimum, \(\hat\beta\), its covariance \(\hat\sigma^2(X^\top M^{-1}X)^{-1}\), Wald \(z\)-statistics and the BLUPs are computed once. coefficient_frame() and random_effects_frame() return them as data frames.

    Simple examples

    A balanced one-way design by hand

    Four groups of three:

    groupvaluesmean

    The grand mean is 6. The deviations of the group means are \((-2,-1,1,2)\), so \(\text{MSB}=3\cdot10/3=10\). The within-group sums of squares are \(6,6,2,6\), so \(\text{MSW}=20/8=2.5\) and \(F=4\). REML gives \(\hat\sigma^2=2.5\), \(\hat\tau^2=(10-2.5)/3=2.5\), so \(\hat\theta=\hat\tau/\hat\sigma=1\) and \(\rho=0.5\). The shrinkage factor is \(3\cdot2.5/(3\cdot2.5+2.5)=0.75\), so the BLUPs are \(0.75\cdot(-2,-1,1,2)=(-1.5,-0.75,0.75,1.5)\), and the intercept has standard error \(\sqrt{10/12}=0.913\). ML gives \(\hat\tau^2=(\tfrac34\cdot10-2.5)/3=\tfrac53\), \(\hat\theta=\sqrt{2/3}=0.816\), shrinkage factor \(5/7.5=\tfrac23\) and BLUPs \((-\tfrac43,-\tfrac23,\tfrac23,\tfrac43)\).

    Unequal group sizes: shrinkage depends on n

    Checking the theory

    Bias of ML and REML, and estimates on the boundary

    Three hundred data sets from the balanced one-way model with \(G=8\) groups of \(m=5\), \(\tau=\sigma=1\), are each fitted by REML and by ML. Without the constraint \(\tau^2\ge0\), the REML estimator would be unbiased and the ML estimator would have mean \(\big(\tfrac{G-1}{G}(\sigma^2+m\tau^2)-\sigma^2\big)/m=0.85\). The exact probabilities of a zero estimate come from the \(F\) distribution as above.

    methodmean tau^2 hatunconstrained theorymean sigma^2 hatzero estimatesF below cut-offexpected, exact
    REML1.0351.001.007663.0
    ML0.8810.851.007994.4

    ML underestimates \(\tau^2\) as predicted: the mean of 0.881 is close to the unconstrained value 0.85, pushed up a little by the estimates truncated at zero. REML's mean of 1.035 is close to the true 1; truncation at zero lifts it slightly above. Both estimate \(\sigma^2\) without bias, because \(\hat\sigma^2=\text{MSW}\) for both. The estimates that are exactly zero are the data sets whose \(F\) statistic is below the cut-off (1 for REML, \(8/7\) for ML), one for one, so the optimiser finds the boundary whenever it should. There happen to be twice as many as expected (6 against 3.0, 9 against 4.4). The probability of 6 or more when 3 are expected is about 8%.

    Standard errors: mixed model against OLS

    Data with \(G=12\) groups of \(m=6\), \(\tau=2\), \(\sigma=1\) (so \(\rho=0.8\)), and two covariates with true slopes 0.5: \(w\) is constant within each group (a school's funding), \(x\) varies within groups (a pupil's prior score). Over 1000 data sets, the table compares the spread of the estimates with the average reported standard error, and the coverage of nominal 95% intervals. The design-effect argument predicts that OLS's standard error for \(w\) is too small by a factor \(\sqrt{1+(m-1)\rho}=\sqrt5=2.24\).

    Both methods estimate both slopes without bias, but they differ in how well they know it.

    For \(w\), the group-level covariate, the two estimators have the same spread (0.667 and 0.668): with \(w\) constant within groups, each estimator effectively regresses group means on \(w\). But OLS reports a standard error of 0.274, 0.41 of the actual spread, close to the \(1/\sqrt5=0.45\) the design effect predicts, and its intervals cover 0.5 only 59% of the time. The mixed model's standard error is nearly right (0.642), and its coverage, 0.914, is close to the 0.922 that a normal interval achieves when the true reference distribution is \(t\) with \(G-2=10\) degrees of freedom: with 12 groups there is little information about \(\tau^2\), which Wald intervals ignore.

    For \(x\), which varies within groups, the mixed model is nearly twice as precise as OLS (0.129 against 0.244): it compares observations within the same group, where the group effect cancels, while OLS treats the group effects (variance 4) as noise. Both report their own spread correctly, and both cover at 95.5%.

    In a separate check, the 400 data sets of an earlier run of this simulation were also fitted with R's nlme::lme. The slopes, standard errors and \(\theta\) agreed to \(10^{-9}\), \(10^{-9}\) and \(5\times10^{-7}\) on every one.

    BLUPs against group means, and the likelihood-ratio test on the boundary

    Two more checks, both shown in the figure below.

    Prediction error. Twenty-four groups, three each of sizes 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20 and 30, with \(\tau=1\), \(\sigma=2\), are simulated 300 times. For each group the true effect \(b_g\) is compared with the BLUP and with the no-pooling estimate \(\bar y_g-\hat\beta_0\). With known variances and \(\beta_0\), the mean squared errors would be \(\sigma^2/n\) for the group mean and \(\tau^2\sigma^2/(n\tau^2+\sigma^2)\) for the BLUP, always smaller.

    Testing \(\tau^2=0\). The likelihood-ratio statistic \(\Lambda=d_{\mathrm{ML}}(0)-d_{\mathrm{ML}}(\hat\theta)\) is zero whenever \(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=0\). Because \(\tau^2=0\) is on the boundary of the parameter space, \(\Lambda\) does not have the usual \(\chi^2_1\) null distribution. Asymptotically it is the 50:50 mixture of a point mass at 0 and \(\chi^2_1\) (Self and Liang, 1987), but in finite samples the point mass is larger (Crainiceanu and Ruppert, 2004): for a balanced design it is exactly \(P(\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=0)=F_{G-1,G(m-1)}(G/(G-1))\). One thousand null data sets with \(G=10\), \(m=5\) are fitted by ML with a smaller search (population 15, 30 generations), which is ample for one parameter.

    Prediction error. The BLUP's error is below the group mean's at every size, and much below for small groups: 0.83 against 4.0 for a single observation, where the group mean is four times worse than ignoring the data and predicting zero (the grand mean, error \(\tau^2=1\)). Both errors are a little above the known-parameter curves, mainly because \(\hat\beta_0\) is estimated (its own mean squared error is 0.066, which enters both).

    Testing \(\tau^2=0\). With no group effect, 65.4% of the statistics are exactly zero, against 50% for the asymptotic mixture and 62.3% from the exact \(F\) probability; the 65.3% of data sets with \(F\) below the cut-off agree with the fits to within one data set. The positive part is also smaller than \(\chi^2_1\): at the usual 5% critical value 3.84, only 0.6% of the statistics exceed it, against 2.5% for the 50:50 mixture and 1.9% for the exact point mass with a \(\chi^2_1\) tail. So the naive \(\chi^2_1\) test has a size of about 0.6% instead of 5%, and even the mixture, the usual recommendation, is conservative with ten groups. Crainiceanu and Ruppert (2004) give the exact finite-sample null distribution; a parametric bootstrap from the null fit is the general alternative.

    Children's orthodontic growth

    The orthodont data set (Potthoff and Roy, 1964; Pinheiro and Bates, 2000) has the distance in millimetres from the pituitary to the pterygomaxillary fissure, measured on 27 children (16 boys, 11 girls) at ages 8, 10, 12 and 14. Each child has their own growth curve: the question is how the average curve differs between boys and girls, and how much children vary around it. Age is centred at 11, so that the intercept is the distance at the middle of the study rather than at birth.

    The random intercept and slope model matches nlme's fit of the same model (the regression test RandomSlopeMatchesNlmeOnOrthodont checks it to \(10^{-7}\)). The population lines are \(22.65+0.480(\text{age}-11)\) mm for girls and \(24.97+0.784(\text{age}-11)\) mm for boys. At age 11 the boys are 2.32 mm larger (SE 0.76), and they grow 0.30 mm/year faster (SE 0.135, \(p=0.024\)).

    Centring changes the parametrisation, not the model. With age centred at 11, the intercept SD is 1.83 mm and the intercept and slope are almost uncorrelated (0.21). With raw age the intercept is the extrapolated distance at birth, which varies more (SD 2.41) and correlates \(-0.67\) with the slope, since a steeper line extrapolates to a lower start. The slope SD, the residual SD and the restricted log-likelihood are identical: \(\Lambda\) has only been re-expressed in another basis.

    Is a random slope needed? The children's growth rates vary with SD 0.18 mm/year, but the REML likelihood-ratio statistic against the intercept-only model is 1.18. Adding a random slope puts its variance on the boundary and adds a free covariance, so the reference distribution is the 50:50 mixture of \(\chi^2_1\) and \(\chi^2_2\) (Stram and Lee, 1994): \(p=0.42\). The thin lines in the figure are indeed close to parallel. Comparing the two REML fits is valid because their fixed effects are the same.

    OLS gets the estimates right and the uncertainty wrong. Every child is measured at the same four ages, so GLS and OLS give identical fixed effects. Their standard errors differ in both directions. For the between-child contrasts (the intercept and the sex difference), OLS treats 108 rows as independent when there are only 27 children, and its standard errors are too small by a factor of 1.7. For the within-child effects (age and the age-by-sex interaction), OLS leaves the children's level differences in the residual, and its standard errors are 1.5 times too large.

    Conclusion

    A linear mixed model accounts for grouping by giving the group effects a distribution and estimating its covariance from the data. Profiling out \(\beta\) and \(\sigma^2\) reduces the fit to a minimisation over the relative covariance factor \(\theta\), one number for a random intercept and three for an intercept and slope. REML corrects ML's downward bias in the variances, which is large with few groups. Both were checked here against the closed forms of the balanced one-way design, and the random-slope model against nlme on the Orthodont data, to machine or optimiser precision. The BLUPs shrink each group's own estimate by its reliability \(n\tau^2/(n\tau^2+\sigma^2)\), and in simulation they beat both no pooling and complete pooling for every group size. The simulations also show where the method's standard inference is weak. Wald intervals for group-level covariates are a little too narrow with a dozen groups, and likelihood-ratio tests of a variance component are conservative even against the boundary mixture. Ignoring the grouping is worse: OLS's intervals for a group-level covariate covered 59% of the time, and on the Orthodont data its standard errors were off by a factor of 1.5 to 1.7 in both directions.
