    Gradient-Free Bayesian Sampling

    Abstract

    We compare three samplers that need only a log density: random-walk Metropolis, Metropolis-within-Gibbs and importance sampling, all from datamunge's bayes module. The test case regresses fuel economy on weight for the 32 cars in dm::datasets::mtcars(), with Normal errors and with heavier-tailed Laplace errors. With three parameters the posterior can be computed exactly by quadrature on a grid, and every sampler is checked against that answer. We derive detailed balance for Metropolis–Hastings and show that the joint-density ratio of a coordinate update is the full-conditional ratio. We also derive two closed forms: an exact Gibbs sampler on a correlation-\(\rho\) Gaussian keeps only a fraction \((1-\rho^2)/(1+\rho^2)\) of its draws as effective, and Gaussian importance sampling with a proposal \(k\) times too wide keeps \((\sqrt{2k^2-1}/k^2)^d\). The intercept and slope have posterior correlation \(-0.957\), and an isotropic random walk on them gives only 19 effective draws out of 20,000 (\(\hat R=1.25\)). Learning the proposal covariance during warmup raises this to about 800, and centring the predictor lets Metropolis-within-Gibbs reach about 3,900. Importance sampling reproduces the exact log evidence of both models to within 0.03. Its Bayes factor of 2.19 in favour of Normal errors matches the grid's to three decimals.

    Sampling with only a log density

    Hamiltonian Monte Carlo needs the gradient of the log posterior. Many models do not offer one. The likelihood may come from a simulator or other black box, or it may have kinks, like the absolute-error likelihood used below. The three methods in this report need only the ability to evaluate \(\log\pi(\theta)\), the log posterior up to an additive constant:

    • *random-walk Metropolis* (bayes::RandomWalkMetropolis) proposes a jump in all coordinates at once;
    • *Metropolis-within-Gibbs* (bayes::GibbsSampler) updates one coordinate at a time;
    • *importance sampling* (bayes::ImportanceSampling) reweights independent draws from a Gaussian approximation, and also estimates the evidence \(p(y)\) needed to compare models.

    Each takes an optim::ArbitraryFunction. In C++, bayes::LogDensity wraps any lambda from std::vector<double> to double as one. The model here has three parameters, so the posterior can also be computed exactly by quadrature on a grid (bayes::grid_posterior). That gives every sampler a known answer to be checked against.

    Fuel economy and weight

    dm::datasets::mtcars() lists 32 cars from the 1974 Motor Trend road tests. We regress fuel economy (miles per US gallon) on weight (thousands of pounds), \(\text{mpg}_i=a+b\,\text{wt}_i+e_i\), with two error laws:

    \[\text{Normal: } e_i\sim N(0,s^2),\qquad\text{Laplace: } p(e_i)=\frac1{2s}e^{-|e_i|/s}.\]

    The Normal model's maximum-likelihood fit is least squares. The Laplace model's is least absolute deviations, a median regression that gives large residuals less influence. Its log likelihood \(-\sum_i|e_i|/s\) has a kink wherever a residual is zero. Both models share weakly informative priors \(a\sim N(20,20^2)\), \(b\sim N(0,10^2)\), \(s\sim\text{half-Cauchy}(0,5)\). We sample \((a,b,\log s)\) and include the log-Jacobian \(\log s\). Every density keeps its normalising constant, so the integral of the unnormalised posterior is the evidence \(p(y)\).

    The grid has \(120^3\) cells over \(a\in[25,50]\), \(b\in[-9,-2]\), \(\log s\in[\log1.2,\log6]\). The "edge mass" column is the posterior probability in the outermost layer of cells. It is tiny, so the box contains essentially the whole posterior.

    Each extra 1,000 lb costs about 5.3 mpg under the Normal model (posterior sd 0.58) and 5.0 mpg under the Laplace model (sd 0.65). The largest residuals are three cars that do better than their weight predicts: the Fiat 128 (+7.0), the Toyota Corolla (+6.5) and the heavy Chrysler Imperial (+5.9). The Laplace likelihood penalises these residuals linearly instead of quadratically, and its line is a little flatter. The evidence nevertheless prefers Normal errors, by a Bayes factor of \(e^{0.782}=2.19\). That is weak evidence: these residuals are not extreme enough for a heavy-tailed error law to pay for itself.

    The column that matters most for sampling is the correlation of \(a\) and \(b\), \(-0.957\) and \(-0.971\). The data fix the height of the line near the mean weight, 3.2 thousand lb, much more tightly than its intercept at weight zero, which lies far outside the data. Every pair \((a,b)\) that fits well therefore lies along a narrow diagonal ridge.

    Metropolis–Hastings

    Detailed balance

    A Markov chain with transition density \(K(\theta\to\theta')\) leaves \(\pi\) invariant if \(\int\pi(\theta)K(\theta\to\theta')\,d\theta=\pi(\theta')\). A sufficient condition is detailed balance, \(\pi(\theta)K(\theta\to\theta')=\pi(\theta')K(\theta'\to\theta)\), because integrating both sides over \(\theta\) gives \(\pi(\theta')\int K(\theta'\to\theta)\,d\theta=\pi(\theta')\). Metropolis–Hastings proposes \(\theta'\sim q(\theta'\mid\theta)\) and accepts it with probability

    \[\alpha(\theta,\theta')=\min\Bigl(1,\frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\Bigr).\]

    For \(\theta'\ne\theta\) the flow from \(\theta\) to \(\theta'\) is then \(\pi(\theta)q(\theta'\mid\theta)\alpha(\theta,\theta')=\min\bigl(\pi(\theta)q(\theta'\mid\theta),\,\pi(\theta')q(\theta\mid\theta')\bigr)\). This is symmetric in \(\theta\) and \(\theta'\), which is detailed balance. The normalising constant of \(\pi\) cancels in the ratio, so only the unnormalised log density is needed. Random-walk Metropolis uses \(\theta'=\theta+\varepsilon Lz\) with \(z\sim N(0,I)\). This proposal is symmetric, so the \(q\) terms cancel too and \(\alpha=\min(1,\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta))\).

    Scaling the proposal

    Small steps are almost always accepted but move little. Large steps are almost always rejected. For a target made of \(d\) independent identical components, Roberts, Gelman and Gilks (1997) showed that as \(d\to\infty\) the efficiency is maximised by \(\varepsilon\approx2.38/\sqrt d\) in units of the target's standard deviation. The acceptance rate is then 0.234. Warmup tunes \(\varepsilon\) towards that rate by the same dual averaging that HMC uses.

    The theory assumes the proposal has the target's shape. An isotropic proposal (\(L=I\)) on a posterior whose scales differ must use a step suited to the narrowest direction, and crawls along the widest. With adapt_covariance set, RandomWalkMetropolis estimates the posterior covariance \(\hat\Sigma\) halfway through warmup and proposes with \(LL^\top=\hat\Sigma\). This is the adaptive Metropolis of Haario, Saksman and Tamminen (2001). It is equivalent to running an isotropic walk on the whitened parameters \(L^{-1}\theta\).

    Metropolis-within-Gibbs and correlation

    A Gibbs sampler updates one coordinate at a time from its full conditional \(\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})\). Each such update leaves \(\pi\) invariant, since it resamples \(\theta_j\) from its exact conditional law, so a full sweep does too. When the conditional cannot be sampled directly, a scalar Metropolis step on \(\theta_j\) can stand in for it, because for that step

    \[\frac{\pi(\theta_j',\theta_{-j})}{\pi(\theta_j,\theta_{-j})}=\frac{\pi(\theta_j'\mid\theta_{-j})\,\pi(\theta_{-j})}{\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})\,\pi(\theta_{-j})}=\frac{\pi(\theta_j'\mid\theta_{-j})}{\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})}.\]

    The joint-density ratio is the full-conditional ratio, so the step satisfies detailed balance for the conditional. GibbsSampler adapts each coordinate's step towards 0.44, the optimal acceptance rate of a one-dimensional random walk.

    Coordinate updates are slowed by correlation. Take a standard bivariate Normal with correlation \(\rho\) and exact conditional draws: \(\theta_1'=\rho\theta_2+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,z_1\), then \(\theta_2'=\rho\theta_1'+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,z_2\). Substituting the first into the second gives

    \[\theta_2'=\rho^2\theta_2+\rho\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,z_1+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,z_2,\]

    an AR(1) process with coefficient \(\rho^2\). Its autocorrelations are \(\rho^{2t}\), so by §1's formula the effective fraction of draws is

    \[\frac{N_{\text{eff}}}{N}=\frac1{1+2\sum_{t\ge1}\rho^{2t}}=\frac{1-\rho^2}{1+\rho^2}.\]

    At \(\rho=0.95\) that is 0.05: twenty sweeps per independent draw, even with exact conditionals. In regression the intercept and slope are strongly correlated whenever the predictor's mean is far from zero relative to its spread. Centring the predictor, \(\text{mpg}=\alpha+b(\text{wt}-\overline{\text{wt}})\) with \(\alpha=a+b\,\overline{\text{wt}}\), is a linear reparameterisation of the same posterior that removes this correlation.

    Exact posterior correlation: corr(a, b) = -0.957, corr(alpha, b) = 0.003. Exact-Gibbs efficiency (1 - rho2) / (1 + rho2): 0.043 and 1.000.

    Every configuration targets the same posterior, but they differ in efficiency by a factor of about 80. On the uncentred parameters the isotropic walk must use steps small enough for the ridge's narrow width. It then needs about a thousand log-density evaluations per effective draw, and in 5,000 iterations per chain it has not explored the ridge. \(\hat R=1.25\) flags this, and its posterior mean of \(b\) is 0.11 away from the exact \(-5.280\). Metropolis-within-Gibbs moves only parallel to the axes, so it climbs the diagonal ridge in a staircase. Its effective fraction of 0.011 is about a quarter of the 0.043 that the formula of §3.3 gives for exact conditional draws at \(\rho=-0.957\). The shortfall comes from replacing each exact draw with a Metropolis step.

    Both fixes work, and they are the same idea applied in two places. The adapted covariance whitens the proposal, which raises the effective sample size about 40-fold to about 800. Centring the predictor whitens the parameters themselves: the posterior correlation of \(\alpha\) and \(b\) is 0.003, which lifts every sampler. With centring, Metropolis-within-Gibbs reaches about 3,900 effective draws from 20,000, and adapted random-walk Metropolis needs the fewest log-density evaluations, about 14 per effective draw. Isotropic random-walk Metropolis still lags after centring, because the three posterior scales still differ (\(\log s\) has a posterior sd of about 0.13, against about 0.58 for \(b\)). The five other configurations put the posterior mean of \(b\) within 0.035 of the exact value.

    Importance sampling

    The estimator

    Importance sampling draws \(\theta_n\sim g\) independently from a proposal \(g\) and weights each draw by \(w_n=\tilde\pi(\theta_n)/g(\theta_n)\), where \(\tilde\pi=p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\) is the unnormalised posterior. Because

    \[E_g\Bigl[\frac{\tilde\pi(\theta)}{g(\theta)}\Bigr]=\int\tilde\pi(\theta)\,d\theta=p(y),\]

    the mean weight \(\hat Z=\frac1N\sum_nw_n\) is an unbiased estimate of the evidence, as long as \(g>0\) wherever \(\tilde\pi>0\). Posterior expectations use the self-normalised estimator \(\sum_n\bar w_nh(\theta_n)\), with \(\bar w_n=w_n/\sum_mw_m\). It is a ratio of two unbiased estimates, so it has a small bias, but it is consistent. Kong (1992) showed that its variance is roughly \(\operatorname{var}_\pi(h)\,(1+\operatorname{var}_g(w/\bar w))/N\), which suggests the effective sample size

    \[N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+\operatorname{var}_g(w/E w)}\approx\frac1{\sum_n\bar w_n^2}.\]

    All the difficulty is in the variance of the weights, which depends on how well \(g\) matches the posterior.

    How wide should the proposal be?

    Take a Gaussian target \(N(0,1)\) and proposal \(N(0,k^2)\). Then \(w\propto e^{-x^2(1-1/k^2)/2}\) and

    \[E_g\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{p}{g}\Bigr)^2\Bigr]=\int\frac{p(x)^2}{g(x)}\,dx=\frac{k}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-x^2\left(1-\frac1{2k^2}\right)}dx=\frac{k^2}{\sqrt{2k^2-1}},\]

    which is finite only when \(k^2>1/2\). In \(d\) independent dimensions the factors multiply:

    \[\frac{N_{\text{eff}}}{N}\to\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{2k^2-1}}{k^2}\Bigr)^d .\]

    The two kinds of error are not symmetric. A proposal that is too wide wastes draws, and the efficiency falls polynomially, to \(0.83^d\) at \(k=1.5\). A proposal narrower than \(1/\sqrt2\) of the target has weights of infinite variance. The estimate then converges erratically and its reported ESS cannot be trusted. bayes::gaussian_importance_efficiency(k, d) evaluates the formula. Below, the proposal is the mean and covariance of a pilot run of adapted random-walk Metropolis (bayes::draws_mean, bayes::draws_covariance), scaled by \(k^2\), and we estimate both models' evidence.

    For proposals at least as wide as the posterior, the efficiency follows the Gaussian formula closely: 0.547 and 0.520 at \(k=1.5\) against 0.575, and 0.090 and 0.087 at \(k=3\) against 0.096. Narrow proposals do much worse than the formula, with 0.19 and 0.25 at \(k=0.8\) against 0.565. This fits a posterior with heavier tails than a Gaussian. With the error scale \(s\) unknown, the posterior of \((a,b)\) is closer to a Student-t, and \(\log s\) is skewed. A narrow Gaussian proposal undersamples these tails, and the few draws that land there get large weights. Below \(k=1/\sqrt2\) the weight variance is infinite in theory. The reported ESS of about 0.02 at \(k=0.6\) then measures nothing reliable, even though the evidence estimate happens to be close. So a proposal should err on the wide side.

    The evidence itself is easier. At every scale in the table the estimate is within 0.03 of the grid value. At \(k=1.5\) the log Bayes factor of 0.783 matches the grid's 0.782. Importance sampling gives the evidence directly, while MCMC draws alone do not.

    Summary

    • Metropolis–Hastings satisfies detailed balance because the probability flow between two states is the minimum of the two directed flows. The normalising constant cancels, so a log density is all it needs. A coordinate-wise Metropolis step targets the full conditional because the joint-density ratio equals the conditional ratio.
    • Gradient-free samplers are limited by the posterior's shape. The intercept–slope correlation of \(-0.957\) left isotropic random-walk Metropolis with 19 effective draws out of 20,000 and \(\hat R=1.25\). Exact Gibbs could do no better than \((1-\rho^2)/(1+\rho^2)=0.043\) of its draws. Adapting the proposal covariance (RWMOptions::adapt_covariance) or centring the predictor removed most of the problem. Together they brought the cost to about 14 log-density evaluations per effective draw.
    • Importance sampling with a Gaussian proposal \(k\) times wider than the posterior keeps \((\sqrt{2k^2-1}/k^2)^d\) of its draws as effective (bayes::gaussian_importance_efficiency). Too narrow is far worse than too wide, and this real posterior's heavier tails made the narrow side worse still.
    • Exact quadrature (bayes::grid_posterior) is cheap for three parameters, and every sampler was checked against it. Importance sampling reproduced the evidence and the Bayes factor of 2.19 in favour of Normal errors over Laplace errors. That is weak evidence: the cars that beat their predicted fuel economy are not outlying enough to need heavy-tailed errors.
