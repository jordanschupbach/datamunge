    Gaussian Process Regression: Conditioning, Noise and Uncertainty

    Abstract

    Given noisy measurements of a nonlinear relationship, we want both a predicted mean and an account of the uncertainty in an unobserved curve value or a future measurement. Those are different targets: observation noise remains even when the underlying curve is well determined. Gaussian process regression conditions a joint Gaussian model using a covariance kernel. We derive the posterior and profiled marginal likelihood, solve two-point examples by hand, and distinguish shared latent values from independent noise at repeated query locations. A generated extrapolation sweep checks reversion to a finite prior variance. On iris, a fit of petal length against petal width illustrates hyperparameter selection, conditional deletion diagnostics, a separate train/test assessment and the limits of interpreting narrow plug-in intervals as universal confidence guarantees. The held-out RMSE is 0.403 cm; 95% new-observation intervals contain 42 of 45 test flowers, while the latent-function intervals contain 14. Far outside the observed widths, the model returns to a prior mean of 3.758 cm with finite latent standard deviation 1.423 cm.

    The method

    A function prior and two prediction targets

    A Gaussian process specifies jointly Gaussian function values at every finite collection of inputs. Write \(f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)\) and observe \(y_i=f(x_i)+\epsilon_i\), where independent observation errors have variance \(\sigma_n^2\). The squared-exponential or RBF kernel is

    \[k(x,x')=s\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-x'\|^2}{2\ell^2}\right)=sR(x,x').\]

    Here \(\ell\) controls correlation distance and \(s\) is a variance, not a standard deviation. An isotropic length scale uses the same distance scale in every expanded predictor coordinate. Scaling the inputs therefore changes the model. The default standardises each formula-expanded predictor using its training population mean and standard deviation. A formula intercept is suppressed; the prior mean is instead the empirical response mean. Transformations and categorical expansions affect distance through their resulting design columns, not through the original column names alone.

    The response is always represented as \(z=(y-\bar y)/s_y\), with population standard deviation \(s_y\); a scale below \(10^{-12}\) is replaced by one. In the following matrix formulas, \(s\) is in standardized-response variance units and \(\eta=\sigma_n^2/s\). A dimensionless numerical ridge \(j\) gives

    \[C=R+(\eta+j)I,\qquad K=sC=LL^\top,\qquad \alpha=K^{-1}z.\]

    The default ridge is \(j=10^{-8}\). It is included consistently in likelihood selection and the final factorisation. It is not added as noise on a new query target. The exact hand calculations below set \(j=0\) on positive-definite examples. With zero noise and zero ridge, duplicate training inputs make the covariance singular.

    For query inputs, let \(K_*=sR_{X,*}\) and \(K_{**}=sR_{*,*}\). Conditioning the joint Gaussian, equivalently completing its quadratic form, gives

    \[\mu_* = \bar y\mathbf1+s_yK_*^\top\alpha,\qquad \Sigma_f=s_y^2(K_{**}-K_*^\top K^{-1}K_*),\qquad \Sigma_{\mathrm{new}\ y}=\Sigma_f+s_y^2s\eta I.\]

    The mean uses two triangular solves after Cholesky; covariance reduction can use \(V=L^{-1}K_*\) and \(V^\top V\). No explicit inverse is needed for prediction. The Schur complement is positive semidefinite in exact arithmetic. Rounding-sized negative variances are clipped; materially invalid values cause an error. These equations follow Rasmussen and Williams, chapter 2.

    predict_detail() describes the latent function. predict_observation_detail() describes a new noisy observation. prediction_table() puts both side by side, and predict_covariance() preserves dependence between several queries. A central pointwise interval is mean plus or minus a Gaussian quantile times the relevant posterior standard deviation. It is not a simultaneous confidence band, nor an interval that integrates uncertainty in fitted hyperparameters and preprocessing.

    Profiling the amplitude and searching a bounded grid

    For fixed \(\ell,\eta,j\), put \(q=z^\top C^{-1}z\). The standardized log density is

    \[\mathcal L(s)=-\frac12\left[n\log(2\pi)+n\log s+\log|C|+\frac qs\right],\qquad \hat s=\max\left(10^{-12},\frac qn\right).\]

    Differentiation gives \(-n/(2s)+q/(2s^2)=0\). At the unconstrained optimum \(q/\hat s=n\), but that simplification is invalid when the variance floor is active. In particular, a constant response has \(z=0\): its covariance scale is driven to the imposed floor, not to a well-identified nonzero prior variance.

    This is conditional maximum likelihood with empirical centring and scaling held fixed. It integrates out the Gaussian function values, then plugs in covariance parameters; it does not integrate over those parameters. In that sense it is an empirical-Bayes fit rather than a fully integrated Bayesian posterior. The normalized-scale accessor signal_variance() retains the original API convention. Original response variances multiply by \(s_y^2\); model_summary() names these explicitly. Original-scale log density subtracts \(n\log s_y\), treating the estimated scale as fixed.

    By default the library searches 20 geometrically spaced lengths from \(0.1\sqrt p\) to \(3\sqrt p\), and 15 noise ratios from \(10^{-4}\) to 1. A positive length or nonnegative noise ratio fixes that quantity; negative finite values request selection. Every candidate profiles \(s\). The first largest finite log density wins. This is a bounded grid search, not a continuous or global optimisation guarantee. The profile at each length maximizes over the searched noise ratios; hyperparameter_search() exposes the full grid, including failed candidates. See Rasmussen and Williams, chapter 5.

    At fixed \(\ell,\eta,j\), the posterior mean uses \(R_*^\top C^{-1}z\): the amplitude cancels. Changing noise ratio and profiling the amplitude can change both smoothing and uncertainty, so a comparison across refitted models is not a fixed-prior variance monotonicity argument.

    Conditional deletion is a diagnostic, not a refitted pipeline

    Let \(A=K^{-1}\). Block inversion of the Gaussian training covariance gives

    \[\mu_{-i}=\bar y+s_y\left(z_i-\frac{\alpha_i}{A_{ii}}\right),\qquad v_{-i,\mathrm{new}\ y}=s_y^2\left(\frac1{A_{ii}}-sj\right).\]

    When \(j=0\), the variance is the familiar inverse-diagonal expression. The subtraction for nonzero ridge excludes numerical jitter on the new held-out target while retaining regularisation on the observations used to predict it. training_details() reports these means, residuals, observation standard deviations and standardized residuals.

    The historical fitted_values(), rmse() and r_squared() accessors use these conditional deletion means. They hold the response mean, feature scales, amplitude and selected kernel parameters from the full sample fixed. They therefore do not equal a sequence of refitted models with each row removed, and do not provide an unbiased assessment of the complete tuning pipeline. Full-data posterior means are exposed separately as posterior_fitted_values(). A train/test split below keeps test responses out of preprocessing and tuning. Its empirical interval coverage assesses the new-observation target.

    Computation and limits

    An exact dense fit stores \(O(n^2+np)\) numbers. A grid of \(G\) candidates costs \(O(G(n^3+n^2p))\) work: each uses kernel construction and Cholesky. Conditional deletion uses solves for the inverse diagonal after selection. At \(M\) queries, marginal predictions cost \(O(M(n^2+np))\); the joint covariance additionally costs \(O(M^2(n+p))\) and stores \(M^2\) entries. This is a small-to-moderate-data implementation, not a sparse GP approximation.

    Far from all training inputs, an RBF cross-covariance vanishes. The mean returns to \(\bar y\), latent variance approaches \(s_y^2s\), and observation variance approaches \(s_y^2s(1+\eta)\). Uncertainty saturates at that finite, fitted prior level; it does not grow without bound or guarantee protection against an inappropriate prior mean or kernel.

    Simple worked examples

    Two distinct inputs: shrinkage and a two-by-two covariance

    Take \(x=(0,1)\), \(y=(-1,1)\), disable predictor standardisation and choose \(\ell=1/\sqrt{2\log2}\), \(\eta=1/2\), \(j=0\). The response already has mean zero and population standard deviation one. Then

    \[R=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2\\1/2&1\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}3/2&1/2\\1/2&3/2\end{pmatrix},\quad C^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&-1/4\\-1/4&3/4\end{pmatrix},\quad q=2,\quad \hat s=1.\]

    Here \(\alpha=(-1,1)\), so the latent posterior means at the observations are \((-1/2,1/2)\), rather than interpolating the noisy responses. Direct multiplication gives

    \[\Sigma_f=\frac1{16}\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\1&5\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Sigma_{\mathrm{new}\ y}=\frac1{16}\begin{pmatrix}13&1\\1&13\end{pmatrix}.\]

    At the midpoint, the posterior mean is zero and latent variance is \(1-1/\sqrt2\). It is slightly smaller than the variance at either observed input: posterior uncertainty depends on the entire covariance geometry, not just nearest-point distance. The conditional deletion means are \((1/3,-1/3)\), giving RMSE \(4/3\).

    Repeated locations: identical latent values, distinct future errors

    Now put both observations at \(x=0\), keeping responses \((-1,1)\), length one, noise ratio one half and zero ridge. The correlation matrix is all ones; its contrast direction has variance supplied entirely by the noise ratio. Profiling gives \(s=2\), so physical noise variance is one. The posterior mean at zero is zero and its latent variance is \(2-8/5=2/5\).

    For two new queries at this same location, the latent values refer to the same random function value. Their covariance matrix is rank one. The observation errors are instead independent, so

    \[\Sigma_f=\begin{pmatrix}2/5&2/5\\2/5&2/5\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Sigma_{\mathrm{new}\ y}=\begin{pmatrix}7/5&2/5\\2/5&7/5\end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(f_1-f_2)=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(y_1-y_2)=2.\]

    Treating pointwise standard deviations as if they implied independent function values would miss this distinction. Zero noise and zero ridge cannot fit these conflicting, coincident observations: the training covariance is singular, and the library rejects it.

    Checking the theory

    Finite prior limits and the exact noise increment

    Shift the first example's responses to \((9,11)\). Its empirical mean is now ten, while response scale, profiled signal variance and noise variance remain one, one and one half. Translation changes the posterior mean by ten and leaves the covariance unchanged. Over a grid extending well beyond the two training inputs, check

    \[0\leq v_f(x)\leq1,\qquad v_{\mathrm{new}\ y}(x)-v_f(x)=1/2,\qquad \mu(x)\longrightarrow10,\quad v_f(x)\longrightarrow1.\]

    The upper bound follows because the covariance reduction is nonnegative. The limits follow from every RBF cross-covariance tending to zero. They are not a claim of monotone variance in distance from the nearest observation. The future-observation variance approaches 1.5, not one.

    A dataset example: iris petal length from petal width

    Data and units

    dm::datasets::iris() has 150 rows, four numeric measurements in centimetres and a species label. Each species contributes 50 flowers. We retain the original example's formula Petal.Length ~ Petal.Width. Species describes the mixture and stratifies the later split; it is not supplied as a predictor. A one-dimensional Gaussian-noise GP therefore approximates a pooled relationship, not separate species-specific mechanisms.

    Hold out flowers before preprocessing and tuning

    A seeded split holds out 30 percent within each species: 105 training flowers and 45 test flowers. The GP sees only training rows during formula resolution, scaling, amplitude profiling and the entire hyperparameter search. score(test) assesses these external predictions without fitting on test responses. It reports RMSE, empirical coverage of each interval target, mean observation-interval width and Gaussian negative log predictive density, which rewards accurate distributions rather than wide intervals alone.

One small, balanced holdout is an assessment of this split, not a population-wide coverage guarantee or a comparison of all kernels.

    Extrapolation and the constant prior mean

    Return to the full-data model and query widths 0.2, 1.3 and 2.5 cm within the training range, then 10 cm far outside it. The output includes both interval targets. Far from observed widths, the constant empirical prior pulls the mean towards the overall petal length mean, even though that need not describe a flower with a much wider petal. The bounded prior variance should not be read as an automatic guarantee against such model misspecification. RBF smoothness, Gaussian homogeneous noise and plug-in parameter values are all substantive assumptions.

    Conclusion

    Two-point calculations identify what the matrix algebra means: conditioning shrinks the posterior mean away from noisy observations, while independent future noise adds to the covariance diagonal. Repeated-location queries show why a collection of marginal standard deviations cannot replace a joint covariance matrix. The extrapolation sweep checks both the exact noise increment and the finite prior limit.

    On iris, likelihood selection supplies a reproducible, model-dependent covariance fit. Conditional deletion is useful for diagnosis, while the held-out split assesses a model whose preprocessing and tuning did not see test responses. Latent and observation intervals answer different questions; neither includes hyperparameter or mean-estimation uncertainty. More data, different kernels, heteroscedastic models or a different prior mean could change the predictions and their calibration. A GP provides an explicit conditional probability model, whose assumptions still need to match the prediction problem.
