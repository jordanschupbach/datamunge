    U-statistics: Unbiased Estimation, Overlap and Two Kinds of Uncertainty

    Abstract

    Suppose a population quantity is the expected value of a function of several independent observations, such as half the squared difference of two measurements. We want to estimate that quantity from one sample, use all its observations fairly, and quantify the uncertainty without pretending that overlapping pairs are independent. A U-statistic averages a symmetric kernel over distinct-index subsets. We derive its unbiasedness, its first-order projection and the extra Monte Carlo variability introduced by sampling only some subsets. Three-point calculations connect the kernel terms to sample variance and delete-one diagnostics. An exact four-point sampling law shows why a degenerate kernel need not have a normal limit, or a useful first-order standard error. A two-point population sweep checks both variance rates. On pooled iris sepal lengths the complete variance is 0.685694 square centimetres and Gini mean difference is 0.946192 centimetres. A repeated-seed check separates computational error from sampling uncertainty: conditional RMSE falls from 0.288 with 10 draws to 0.0169 with 3000.

    The method

    Symmetric kernels and the estimand

    Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent observations from a common distribution \(F\). A symmetric degree-\(m\) kernel \(h\) is unchanged by permuting its \(m\) arguments. Its population target and complete U-statistic are

    \[\theta=E[h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)],\qquad U_n=\binom nm^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_1<\cdots<i_m\leq n}h(X_{i_1},\ldots,X_{i_m}).\]

    Every term has expectation \(\theta\), so integrability gives \(E[U_n]=\theta\). With finite kernel variance there is also a simple variance argument for using all subsets. Start with the unbiased estimator \(h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)\), randomly permute all \(n\) indices, then average over the permutations conditional on the observed sample. The conditional average is \(U_n\); the law of total variance shows that this symmetrisation cannot increase variance. Broader minimum-variance claims require specifying the distribution family and the class of unbiased competitors; unbiasedness alone does not establish universal efficiency.

    The observations may have equal values; it is their indices that must be distinct within a tuple. The library accepts a callback of type SymmetricKernel. The caller must supply a deterministic, symmetric function of the configured degree. Symmetry is not tested. The finite-input checks reject NaNs and infinities rather than silently dropping observations.

    Three familiar choices are the mean kernel \(h(x)=x\), the variance kernel \(h(x,y)=(x-y)^2/2\), and Gini's mean-difference kernel \(h(x,y)=|x-y|\). The second targets population variance because independent copies satisfy \(E[(X-Y)^2]=2\operatorname{Var}(X)\). The identity

    \[\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2=n\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U_n\left[\frac{(x-y)^2}{2}\right]=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}\]

    makes the usual unbiased sample variance a degree-two U-statistic. Averaging over all ordered pairs including \(i=j\), a V-statistic, instead gives the variance with divisor \(n\). Gini's mean difference has the original measurement units; variance has squared units. Gini grows linearly with a single extreme observation, variance quadratically. Neither has bounded outlier influence, so calling Gini automatically robust would be misleading.

    Overlap, Hoeffding projections and the non-degenerate limit

    There are \(\binom nm\) kernel terms, but they share observations. Dividing the variance of one term by that count would generally miss their covariance. For degree two define

    \[h_1(x)=E[h(x,X')]-\theta,\qquad h_2(x,y)=h(x,y)-\theta-h_1(x)-h_1(y).\]

    Then \(E[h_1(X)]=0\) and \(E[h_2(X,X')\mid X]=0\). Substitution gives

    \[U_n-\theta=\frac2n\sum_i h_1(X_i)+\binom n2^{-1}\sum_{i<j}h_2(X_i,X_j), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(U_n)=\frac{4\zeta_1}{n}+\frac{2\zeta_2}{n(n-1)},\]

    where \(\zeta_1=E[h_1(X)^2]\) and \(\zeta_2=E[h_2(X,X')^2]\). The variance formula follows because the two orders are orthogonal, and distinct canonical pair terms are uncorrelated even when they share one index: condition on that index and use the zero conditional expectation. For general degree, the canonical projection variances \(\zeta_s\) obey

    \[\operatorname{Var}(U_n)=\sum_{s=1}^m\frac{\binom ms^2}{\binom ns}\zeta_s.\]

    These are canonical projection variances. They must not be substituted unchanged into formulas that instead use covariances of kernels with a given number of shared arguments. For fixed \(m\), finite second moment and \(\zeta_1>0\), the first term dominates; the ordinary i.i.d. central limit theorem and a negligible remainder imply

    \[\sqrt n(U_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,m^2\zeta_1).\]

    If \(\zeta_1=0\), this argument gives no nontrivial normal approximation. The rate can change from \(n^{-1/2}\) to \(n^{-1}\), and the limit can be non-normal. A small computed standard error does not diagnose which regime applies.

    What the library's finite-sample diagnostics estimate

    For a complete average let \(g_i\) be the mean of the \(\binom{n-1}{m-1}\) terms containing observation \(i\). Every term is counted \(m\) times, so \(n^{-1}\sum_i g_i=U_n\). The existing result fields use

    \[\widehat\zeta_1=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(g_i-U_n)^2, \qquad\widehat V_{\rm projection}=\frac{m^2}{n}\widehat\zeta_1.\]

    This is a leading-order estimate, not an exact finite-sample variance. analyze() also computes the complete-fit delete-one values without reevaluating the kernel. Splitting the complete sum into terms that contain \(i\) and terms that do not gives

    \[U_{(-i)}=\frac{nU_n-mg_i}{n-m},\qquad \widehat V_{\rm JK}=\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_i(U_{(-i)}-U_n)^2 =\left(\frac{n-1}{n-m}\right)^2\widehat V_{\rm projection}.\]

    The pseudo-value is \(P_i=nU_n-(n-1)U_{(-i)}\). Its average equals \(U_n\); centred pseudo-values show which observations change the estimator most. The jackknife is still a sampling-variance estimate, with its own non-degeneracy requirements. It does not rescue a degenerate kernel. If \(n=m\), delete-one values do not exist and are reported as NaN, even though the legacy projection estimate is zero. projections exposes all these intermediate quantities.

    Incomplete averages: sampling variability plus computational variability

    With max_terms=B>0, the library draws \(B\) uniform \(m\)-subsets. Indices are distinct within a draw, but the same subset can be drawn again. Conditional on the fixed data the draws are independent. Let \(H\) be one uniformly selected subset's kernel value, and let \(v_X=\operatorname{Var}(H\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n)\). Then

    \[E[U_{n,B}\mid X]=U_n,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(U_{n,B}\mid X)=\frac{v_X}{B}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(U_{n,B})=\operatorname{Var}(U_n)+\frac{E[v_X]}{B}.\]

    Equivalently, \(\operatorname{Var}(U_{n,B})=(1-1/B)\operatorname{Var}(U_n)+ \operatorname{Var}(h(X_1,\ldots,X_m))/B\). The conditional Monte Carlo standard error is estimated from the sampled kernel values, using their sample variance divided by \(B\). It is unavailable from one draw. It does not describe how the underlying data sample varies. No finite-population correction applies: subset draws are with replacement, even when \(B\) exceeds the number of distinct subsets.

    The legacy incomplete projection fields use noisy inclusion averages, sometimes with uncovered observations. They are not a calibrated total standard error. analyze() reports coverage, conditional Monte Carlo variance and unavailable jackknife values explicitly. incomplete_path() checks computational RMSE against a complete reference; it is intended for samples where that reference is tractable.

    For a fixed non-degenerate kernel, typically \(B\gg n\) is needed to make computational variance negligible relative to the \(1/n\) sampling variance. For a degree-two degenerate kernel the corresponding requirement can be \(B\gg n^2\). Merely letting \(B\) grow does not give the complete estimator's limiting law. Subbagging and some random-forest constructions can be analysed as growing-degree U-statistics, but their rates, learner randomness and regularity assumptions require separate results; this report makes no automatic infinite-order normality claim.

    Enumeration takes \(\binom nm\) kernel calls; an incomplete average takes exactly \(B\). Bookkeeping costs \(O(m)\) per call and \(O(n+m)\) working memory. Complete enumeration has no automatic budget cap; an unrepresentable term count is rejected before evaluation. term_details() additionally stores \(O(Bm)\) or \(O(\binom nm m)\) output, so use it for small examples. Seeded draws reproduce within the same standard-library implementation.

    A four-point sampling law with a genuinely degenerate kernel

    Let \(X\) be equally likely to be \(-1\) or \(+1\), and take \(h(x,y)=xy\). Then \(\theta=(EX)^2=0\) and \(h_1(x)=xEX-\theta=0\) for both population values. If \(K\) of \(n\) observations equal \(+1\), then \(S=2K-n\) and

    \[U_n=\frac{S^2-n}{n(n-1)},\qquad K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,1/2), \qquad E[U_n]=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(U_n)=\frac2{n(n-1)}.\]

    For \(n=4\), \(K=0,1,2,3,4\) has probabilities \((1,4,6,4,1)/16\), and the corresponding U-values are \((1,0,-1/3,0,1)\). A negative unbiased estimate of the non-negative target \((EX)^2\) is possible. In the balanced sample \((-1,-1,1,1)\), all inclusion means equal \(-1/3\), so both the projection and jackknife standard errors are zero, despite the population sampling variance being \(1/6\).

    Indeed \(S/\sqrt n\) tends to a standard normal \(Z\), while \(\sum X_i^2=n\). It follows that \(nU_n\) tends to \(Z^2-1\), a centred chi-square law, rather than a normal distribution. For incomplete sampling in this example, \(\operatorname{Var}(h)=1\), so its unconditional variance is exactly \((1-1/B)2/[n(n-1)]+1/B\). This makes the \(B\gg n^2\) requirement concrete.

    Checking the theory

    Exact sampling moments in two population regimes

    For the same product kernel under a general population mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), \(h_1(x)=\mu(x-\mu)\) and \(h_2(x,y)=(x-\mu)(y-\mu)\). Thus

    \[\theta=\mu^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(U_n)=\frac{4\mu^2\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{2\sigma^4}{n(n-1)}.\]

    On \(\{-1,+1\}\), probability \(p=3/4\) for \(+1\) gives \(\mu=1/2,\sigma^2=3/4\), so \(n\operatorname{Var}(U_n)\to3/4\). At \(p=1/2\) the first projection vanishes and \(n^2\operatorname{Var}(U_n)\to2\). two_point_law() computes these laws from binomial and hypergeometric weights: a tuple's number of high values conditional on the sample count is hypergeometric. It evaluates only \(m+1\) kernel compositions and does not enumerate \(2^n\) ordered samples.

    Conclusion

    A symmetric kernel makes the estimand explicit; distinct-index averaging produces an unbiased estimate without requiring independent kernel terms. The inclusion means reveal why those terms are dependent and how delete-one diagnostics can be obtained from a single complete enumeration. The three-point calculation also shows how large the finite-sample jackknife correction can be.

    The exact product-kernel law supplies a limit to first-order reasoning: a balanced sample can report zero projection and jackknife uncertainty even though repeated sampling has positive variance and a non-normal limit. Incomplete averaging introduces a second source of variability with a different experimental interpretation. On the fixed iris sample it follows the conditional \(B^{-1/2}\) law; it cannot be substituted for uncertainty about the population or used to justify unrestricted growing-degree normal approximations.
