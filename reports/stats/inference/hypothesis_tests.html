    Hypothesis Tests: Null Models, Effects and Calibration

    Abstract

    Observed differences can arise from sampling variation, systematic effects or a poor sampling model. Given data and a specified null model, a hypothesis test measures how extreme a chosen statistic would be under that model; it does not assign a probability to the null being true. We derive and check classical mean, rank, correlation, variance, shape and count tests using small examples before examining iris measurements. Exact enumeration exposes the conservatism of discrete tests and the limitations of a normal approximation. A binomial counterexample shows why an exact two-sided p-value and an equal-tailed confidence interval need not give matching decisions. The iris analysis reports effect sizes, intervals and a declared four-test Holm family, distinguishes pooled from within-species association, and avoids treating a fitted-normal KS p-value as valid evidence. Small representable p-values are retained and printed in scientific notation.

    The method

    Specify the sampling model and question first

    For a statistic \(T\) and a prespecified ordering of extreme outcomes, a p-value is a null probability such as \(p=P_0\{T\ge T_{\rm obs}\}\). Under a valid null model it satisfies \(P_0(p\le\alpha)\le\alpha\). Equality is typical for continuous exact references; discreteness can make the inequality strict. This calibration is a repeated- sampling property, not \(P(H_0\mid\text{data})\), the probability of replication, or the size of the effect. Failure to reject does not prove equality or equivalence.

    Choose the outcome, groups, direction, sampling unit and multiplicity family before inspecting p-values. Independent flowers, paired repeated measurements and conditional count tables are different designs. A paired test uses within-pair differences, and pairing cannot be manufactured by sorting two independent samples. Selection after seeing outcomes requires additional inference; changing tests until one rejects invalidates a single-test error interpretation.

    The common HypothesisTestResult owns a method, statistic, parameters, p-value, estimates and optional confidence bounds. Field meanings are test-specific: a Wilcoxon statistic is a rank sum/U, not a z score; a Spearman statistic here is its t approximation, not rho. hypothesis_test_table() gathers results, leaving absent intervals null. Vector APIs reject nonfinite values rather than silently dropping them. Any complete-case selection must preserve paired-row alignment and be described by the caller.

    Mean and variance references

    For a one-sample mean, normal independent observations give

    \[\bar x=\frac1n\sum_i x_i,\quad s^2=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1},\quad T=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1},\quad C_{1-\alpha}=\bar x\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.\]

    The normal mean and residual sum of squares are independent; their standardized normal and chi-square ratio gives the Student reference. A paired t-test applies the same result to \(d_i=x_i-y_i\). The differences need the distributional assumption, not each marginal measurement separately. Under the same normal model, the two-sided mean interval excludes \(\mu_0\) exactly when its matching two-sided t-test rejects.

    For independent groups, Welch estimates the variance of the mean difference and its df:

    \[v_x=s_x^2/n_x,\quad v_y=s_y^2/n_y,\qquad T_W=\frac{\bar x-\bar y}{\sqrt{v_x+v_y}},\quad \nu=\frac{(v_x+v_y)^2}{v_x^2/(n_x-1)+v_y^2/(n_y-1)}.\]

    This is an approximate t reference even for unequal-variance normal populations. Pooled Student instead estimates a common variance and uses \(n_x+n_y-2\) df; it requires that common-variance model. An F-test is not a required gatekeeper before choosing Welch. For variance comparison itself, independent normal samples give \(s_x^2/s_y^2\sim F_{n_x-1,n_y-1}\); its confidence interval targets the variance ratio. That reference is especially sensitive to nonnormality.

    Classical one-way ANOVA uses a common within-group variance:

    \[SS_B=\sum_g n_g(\bar x_g-\bar x)^2,\quad SS_W=\sum_{g,i}(x_{gi}-\bar x_g)^2,\quad F=\frac{SS_B/(k-1)}{SS_W/(N-k)}\sim F_{k-1,N-k}.\]

    The orthogonal between/within projections yield independent scaled chi-squares under that null. one_way_anova() is this equal-variance procedure, not Welch ANOVA. The returned F-test does not identify which group differs or estimate a causal effect.

    Ranks answer a different question

    Pool two samples and give tied observations their average one-based ranks. The rank-sum statistic for the first sample is

    \[U=\sum_{i\in x}R_i-\frac{n_x(n_x+1)}2,\quad E_0(U)=\frac{n_xn_y}2,\quad V_0(U)=\frac{n_xn_y}{12}\left[N+1-\frac{\sum_j(t_j^3-t_j)}{N(N-1)}\right].\]

    The implementation subtracts a half-unit continuity correction towards the null centre for a two-sided z score and uses the normal reference at every sample size. It does not switch to exact enumeration when samples are small. \(U/(n_xn_y)\) estimates \(P(X>Y)+\tfrac12P(X=Y)\). The usual null is identical independent distributions; a common-shape location-shift model permits a location interpretation. Equal medians alone is not sufficient, and a rank test is not a mean test under weaker assumptions.

    The signed-rank test ranks nonzero absolute differences and sums ranks with positive signs. Its null requires a symmetric difference distribution about zero; it is not a distribution-free test of an arbitrary median. Kruskal–Wallis instead uses group rank sums \(R_g\), with tie factor \(C=1-\sum_j(t_j^3-t_j)/(N^3-N)\):

    \[H=\frac{12\sum_g R_g^2/n_g}{N(N+1)}-3(N+1),\qquad H_{\rm corrected}=H/C\ \dot\sim\ \chi^2_{k-1}.\]

    The identical-distribution null supplies exchangeability. The statistic is sensitive to rank-location differences; it is not an omnibus detector of every possible distributional change. Tiny groups can give a poor chi-square approximation.

    Association and shape

    Pearson measures linear association and Spearman measures correlation of average ranks. For independent bivariate-normal pairs under zero Pearson correlation, \(T=r\sqrt{(n-2)/(1-r^2)}\sim t_{n-2}\). Pearson's Fisher-z confidence interval is approximate. Spearman here reuses the t reference for rho; it is not an exact permutation test. Neither zero Pearson nor zero Spearman correlation generally implies independence. Mixing groups can produce a large pooled association even when each group's association is substantially smaller.

    Shapiro–Francia uses the squared correlation of ordered data with Blom normal scores, \(W'=\operatorname{corr}(x_{(i)},\Phi^{-1}((i-3/8)/(n+1/4)))^2\), with Royston's approximate p-value for \(5\le n\le5000\). It tests a composite normal family. A large value is not proof of normality; rounding and limited sample size matter.

    KS uses \(D=\sup_x|F_n(x)-F_0(x)|\). For a one-sample test its continuous reference CDF must be fully specified independently of the sample. Fitting the reference mean and SD from that same sample changes the null distribution: the ordinary KS p-value then has no advertised calibration. This API uses an asymptotic reference with a finite-n correction, not exact finite-sample KS or a Lilliefors correction. Ties from measurement rounding also affect its continuous-null calibration. In iris we show a benchmark D without interpreting its nominal p-value.

    Counts, exact probabilities and confidence intervals

    For independent Bernoulli trials, \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p_0)\) under the null. One-sided exact p-values include the observed count. Two-sided binomial_test() sums PMFs no larger than the observed PMF, with relative ordering tolerance \(10^{-7}\):

    \[p_{\rm exact}(x)=\sum_{k:\,f_0(k)\le f_0(x)} f_0(k),\qquad f_0(k)=\binom nk p_0^k(1-p_0)^{n-k}.\]

    Its Clopper–Pearson interval inverts two equal-tailed one-sided binomial bounds. Coverage is at least nominal under the Bernoulli model, but it does not in general invert this probability-ordered two-sided test. The score proportion test instead uses the null SE, optionally a capped half-count correction, and a normal approximation. Its returned Wilson interval is uncorrected even when the test is corrected, another reason to check the stated interval/test definitions rather than assuming duality.

    For a 2x2 table \((a,b;c,d)\), Fisher conditions on fixed row and column margins. With \(N=a+b+c+d\), \(r=a+b\) and \(c_1=a+c\), its top-left count has probability \(P(A=k)=\binom{c_1}k\binom{N-c_1}{r-k}/\binom Nr\). Two-sided ordering again uses conditional probabilities, not twice an arbitrary tail. The reported sample odds ratio is \(ad/(bc)\), not R's conditional maximum-likelihood estimate. A zero observed cell is not automatically a structural impossibility in the population.

    Pearson categorical independence uses \(X^2=\sum(O-E)^2/E\), with \(E_{ij}=O_{i+}O_{+j}/N\) and a chi-square reference. The approximation needs sufficiently populated expected cells. Yates correction changes the 2x2 statistic; Fisher changes the reference experiment. Choose these methods for their model and calibration, not whichever produces the preferred p-value.

    Multiple comparisons, numerics and computation

    For a declared family of \(m\) p-values sorted as \(p_{(1)}\le\cdots\le p_{(m)}\), Holm reports \(p^{\rm adj}_{(i)}=\min\{1,\max_{j\le i}(m-j+1)p_{(j)}\}\). The first true-null rejection would require a true-null p-value at most \(\alpha/m_0\), so a union bound over the \(m_0\) true nulls gives strong family-wise control without independence. Four separate 95% intervals remain marginal intervals after Holm adjustment; they do not become simultaneous 95% intervals merely by sharing a table.

    Mean/count sufficient-statistic tests cost linear time in observations; rank/shape/KS sorting costs \(O(n\log n)\). Exact binomial and 2x2 Fisher p-values enumerate their finite support, costing \(O(n)\) or the number of feasible tables. The complete binomial rejection table costs \(O(n^2)\); its size/power calculation is deterministic, not Monte Carlo. Normal tails use erfc, Student tails use symmetry, F tails use a complementary beta identity and chi-square tails use the upper incomplete gamma. This retains small representable p-values that would be lost by subtracting a rounded-to-one CDF.

    Checking calibration and power

    Exact enumeration provides an independent repeated-sampling question: how often does this fixed rule reject? For rejection set \(\mathcal R\), compute

    \[\operatorname{size}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal R}f(x;n,p_0),\qquad \operatorname{power}(p_1)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal R}f(x;n,p_1).\]

    We fix \(p_0=0.1\), \(p_1=0.3\), \(\alpha=0.05\) and a two-sided alternative, and enumerate every count for each trial number. Exact, uncorrected score and corrected score rules are all declared in advance. The red uncorrected-score curve shows why a normal approximation is unreliable with sparse expected successes. The exact size stays at or below 5%, but power and size have discrete jumps; a continuous smooth-power intuition can be misleading. These calculations have no Monte Carlo uncertainty.

    Iris measurements

    Dataset, location effects and a declared family

    dm::datasets::iris() contains 150 flowers, 50 of each species, with four complete measurements in centimetres and a species label. We first summarize the data, then compare setosa and versicolor petal length with Welch and rank-sum tests, and all three species with classical ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis. The F-test compares setosa/virginica petal-length variance. These are distinct questions, not five replications of one test. ANOVA's common-variance assumption is questionable for these petal lengths; its result is presented as a model calculation rather than made robust by another test agreeing.

    Separately, we declare the four Welch comparisons of versicolor minus virginica on all four measurements as one multiplicity family and apply Holm. The plot and table give raw-unit mean differences and marginal 95% intervals. The historically collected, species-stratified data are not randomized treatments; these calculations are a sampling- model illustration, not evidence for a causal species effect or a new confirmatory study.

    Shape diagnostics with a stated calibration limitation

    Setosa sepal lengths are recorded to one decimal place. Their normal QQ plot and Shapiro–Francia statistic describe shape, with the latter's continuous-normal p-value only an approximation for rounded data. Failure to reject is not proof that normality holds, and it is not a licence to ignore the design or variance assumptions of other tests.

    For KS we use the illustrative fixed benchmark \(N(5,0.4^2)\), in centimetres. We display its D statistic only: rounding already violates a continuous-null model, and this historically selected dataset is not a prospective test of that benchmark. In particular, replacing the benchmark by the sample's fitted mean and SD would not turn the ordinary KS p-value into a valid composite-normality test.

    Conclusion

    A test result is interpretable only with its null, sampling design, ordering and reference calibration. The worked examples separate mean, rank and conditional-count questions; finite enumeration reveals conservative exact rules and sparse-count approximation errors. Confidence intervals add effect units and uncertainty but must be matched to their actual construction. Iris illustrates species-stratified effects, a declared multiplicity family, and the difference between pooled association and within-species relationships. Agreement among several p-values neither validates their assumptions nor supplies causal evidence.
