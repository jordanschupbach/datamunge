    Birkhoff Interpolation: Values, Derivatives and Uniqueness

    Abstract

    Given scattered values and derivatives of a function, find a polynomial that satisfies all the conditions. Missing derivative orders are allowed, but the conditions may be redundant, inconsistent or nearly dependent. We derive the linear system, recover the original cubic from values and curvatures, and exhibit both infinitely many solutions and a family that becomes singular. Datamunge exposes the constraint matrix and derivative evaluator, and its scaled solver reports numerical failure separately from a successful fit.

    The method

    For N constraints, seek \(p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}c_kx^k\), of degree *at most* \(N-1\). A condition \(p^{(d_i)}(x_i)=v_i\) becomes row i of

    \[A c=v,\qquad A_{ik}=\begin{cases} \dfrac{k!}{(k-d_i)!}x_i^{k-d_i},&k\ge d_i,\\0,&k<d_i. \end{cases}\]

    Derivative orders need not be consecutive at a node. This is the lacunary feature of Birkhoff interpolation: unlike Hermite data, a value and a second derivative need not come with a first derivative.

    In exact arithmetic, the fixed-node problem is poised when A is nonsingular: every right-hand side then has one unique coefficient vector. A singular A may have no solution or infinitely many for a particular v. A numerical solver also has a third practical limit: coefficients may be extremely sensitive to small changes even when the exact matrix is nonsingular.

    Simple example: recover the original cubic

    The conditions are \(p(-1)=-2\), \(p''(-1)=-18\), \(p(1)=10\), and \(p''(1)=30\). Since \(p''(x)=2c_2+6c_3x\), adding and subtracting the curvature equations gives \(c_2=3\), \(c_3=4\). The value equations then give \(c_0=1\), \(c_1=2\). Thus \(p(x)=1+2x+3x^2+4x^3\).

    A nonpoised problem has more than one possible outcome

    For a quadratic, prescribe \(p(-1)=p(1)=0\) and \(p'(0)=0\). Every \(p_\lambda(x)=\lambda(x^2-1)\) satisfies these conditions, so there is no unique interpolant. Changing only the derivative target to 1 makes the same matrix inconsistent: the endpoint conditions force \(c_1=0\), whereas \(p'(0)=1\) demands \(c_1=1\). The API returns an empty vector in both cases; an empty result does not distinguish these two mathematical situations.

    The original report's second rejected scheme, \(p'(0)=p''(-1)=p''(1)=0\), is also nonpoised for degree at most two: the constant coefficient is unconstrained, and the curvature rows are identical. Rejection is appropriate even though the zero polynomial itself satisfies the conditions.

    Checking the theory: approach to singularity

    Move the derivative node to t and prescribe \(p'(t)=1\), retaining the zero endpoint values. The matrix and its determinant are

    \[A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&1\\1&1&1\\0&1&2t\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det A(t)=4t,\qquad p_t(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{2t}\quad(t\ne0).\]

    The coefficient size grows as \(1/(2|t|)\), while a perturbation delta in the derivative target changes \(c_2\) by \(\delta/(2t)\). Thus the geometry itself amplifies measurement error as t approaches zero. A numerically returned answer cannot make this sensitivity disappear.

    Scaling and numerical limits

    The solver equilibrates each matrix row and column before pivoted elimination. This removes the old absolute pivot cutoff's dependence on a single coefficient's units. For example, the valid line through \((0,1)\) and \((10^{-16},2)\) has coefficients \((1,10^{16})\): column scaling resolves it even though the unscaled slope column is tiny.

    Scaling does not guarantee a well-conditioned monomial basis. Pivots at most \(64N\epsilon\) on the equilibrated matrix are treated as numerically singular; this is an implementation threshold rather than an exact rank theorem. The routine uses \(O(N^3)\) work and \(O(N^2)\) storage. Negative orders and nonfinite conditions are rejected, and nonrepresentable arithmetic raises an overflow error. Empty coefficients evaluate as the zero polynomial, so callers must check for an empty fit before interpreting it as an interpolant.

    Conclusion

    The original cubic is recovered from values and second derivatives, with the matrix and fitted derivatives visible. The quadratic controls distinguish nonuniqueness, inconsistency, exact poisedness and numerical resolution. Scaling fixes the tiny-coordinate affine control, while the approaching-singularity example retains its unavoidable sensitivity. Generated polynomial controls are sufficient here; an observed dataset would not establish these exact properties.
