    De Boor's Algorithm: From a Control Polygon to a B-Spline Point

    Abstract

    Given a knot sequence and a polygon of control points, we want to evaluate a smooth parametric curve without expanding its pieces into powers or repeatedly computing basis functions that contribute nothing. De Boor's algorithm reduces a small active control net through knot-weighted linear interpolation. We derive that reduction from the B-spline basis recursion, calculate two quadratic examples by hand, and expose every intermediate point and weight through datamunge's DeBoorSpline. Repeated knots show the difference between a corner and a jump. Generated Bezier families check the curve against a closed form and verify the quadratic interpolation count. A control-point perturbation demonstrates exact local support, and the original six-control cubic provides a larger construction. Both endpoints are evaluated directly, including on parameter domains much smaller than an arbitrary fixed epsilon.

    The method

    A parametric curve, rather than a fit through observations

    Choose degree \(p\geq0\), \(N\geq p+1\) planar controls \(P_i\), and \(N+p+1\) nondecreasing knots \(u_i\). The curve is

    \[C(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}P_i B_{i,p}(t),\qquad t\in[u_p,u_N].\]

    The parameter \(t\) is not generally the curve's horizontal coordinate or its arc length. Interior controls guide the curve; they are not interpolation targets. Fitting coefficients from noisy measurements is a separate problem, covered by the P-spline report. No empirical dataset is needed here: prescribed control nets let us check the evaluator against exact answers.

    On a nonempty knot span \([u_k,u_{k+1})\), only the \(p+1\) basis functions with indices \(k-p,\ldots,k\) can contribute. Normalized B-splines are nonnegative and sum to one on the base interval. This representation therefore gives both local control and a convex-hull constraint.

    Deriving the interpolation triangle

    The degree-zero basis indicates a knot interval. Higher degrees obey the recursion

    \[B_{i,q}(t)=\frac{t-u_i}{u_{i+q}-u_i}B_{i,q-1}(t) +\frac{u_{i+q+1}-t}{u_{i+q+1}-u_{i+1}}B_{i+1,q-1}(t).\]

    Substitute this into a sum \(\sum_i Q_i B_{i,q}(t)\) and collect the coefficient of each \(B_{i,q-1}\). It becomes \((1-\alpha_i)Q_{i-1}+\alpha_i Q_i\), with \(\alpha_i=(t-u_i)/(u_{i+q}-u_i)\). Repeating this degree reduction leaves one nonzero degree-zero basis value, equal to one on the chosen span. Thus the final coefficient is the curve point.

    In local work-vector indices, initialise \(d_j^{(0)}=P_{k-p+j}\) for \(j=0,\ldots,p\). At round \(r=1,\ldots,p\), put \(i=k-p+j\) and compute

    \[\alpha_j^{(r)}=\frac{t-u_i}{u_{i+p-r+1}-u_i},\qquad d_j^{(r)}=(1-\alpha_j^{(r)})d_{j-1}^{(r-1)}+\alpha_j^{(r)}d_j^{(r-1)}, \quad j=r,\ldots,p.\]

    The answer is \(d_p^{(p)}\). Updating \(j\) from \(p\) down to \(r\) lets the same work vector hold successive rounds: the left parent still belongs to the old round when it is read. An ascending update would consume a modified parent and usually give a different answer. Trace tables retain the actual descending update order. The method follows de Boor's original work.

    Each active denominator spans the selected nonempty interval, so it is positive even when other knots coincide. On that span all weights lie in \([0,1]\). The curve therefore stays inside the convex hull of the active controls and reproduces a constant control net. For an affine map \(F(P)=AP+b\), interpolation commutes with \(F\): transforming the controls transforms the curve by exactly the same map in exact arithmetic.

    Endpoints, repeated knots and what stability means

    The library selects the right-hand span at an interior knot. At the final domain endpoint it evaluates the last span's left limit directly. Repeating each boundary knot \(p+1\) times makes the endpoints equal to the first and last controls. Unclamped splines need not interpolate either control. There is no extrapolation in DeBoorSpline.

    An interior knot of multiplicity \(m\leq p\) guarantees at least \(C^{p-m}\) continuity; special controls can make it smoother. Multiplicity \(p+1\) allows a jump. A curve with such knots should not be drawn as one polyline across all sampled values. plot_curve() draws separate polynomial spans, including their individual left-limit endpoints, so no line is invented across a discontinuity.

    For fixed knots and parameter, write \(w_i=B_{i,p}(t)\). If each control is perturbed by at most \(\varepsilon\) in a chosen norm, then

    \[\|\Delta C(t)\|=\left\|\sum_iw_i\Delta P_i\right\| \leq\sum_iw_i\|\Delta P_i\|\leq\varepsilon.\]

    This is an absolute conditioning statement with respect to controls. It does not promise small relative error when a coordinate nearly cancels to zero, or insensitivity to perturbing very close knots. Floating-point evaluation also rounds the knot ratios and interpolations. The implementation uses bounded blends and scales a knot ratio if its full interval width would overflow; it cannot recover distinctions already lost when knots are rounded to the same floating-point number.

    Work and library interfaces

    There are \(p+(p-1)+\cdots+1=p(p+1)/2\) point interpolations per evaluation. A validated DeBoorSpline locates the span by binary search, then evaluates in \(O(\log N+p^2)\) work with \(O(p)\) scratch. Construction validates and owns the inputs in \(O(N+p)\). A full trace stores \((p+1)(p+2)/2\) initial/intermediate points and takes \(O(p^2)\) space. The compatibility function de_boor() constructs a validated object for every call, so repeated work should use DeBoorSpline.

    Inputs must be finite, knots must have the stated count and order, multiplicity must not exceed \(p+1\), and both domain-end spans must be nonempty. Out-of-domain parameters and sample counts below two are rejected. sample() includes both exact endpoints and is uniform in parameter, not arc length. No fixed epsilon is subtracted from the right endpoint; on a domain such as \([0,10^{-15}]\), subtracting \(10^{-12}\) would move the last sample outside the domain altogether.

    Simple worked examples

    A quadratic Bezier: all weights equal one quarter

    Use degree two, controls \((0,0),(2,4),(4,0)\), and knots \((0,0,0,1,1,1)\). There is one polynomial piece, a quadratic Bezier curve. At \(t=1/4\), the first round produces

    \[d_1^{(1)}=(1/2,1),\qquad d_2^{(1)}=(5/2,3),\qquad C(1/4)=\tfrac34(1/2,1)+\tfrac14(5/2,3)=(1,3/2).\]

    All three interpolation weights are one quarter, so de Boor reduces to de Casteljau. The trace includes the three original controls, the two first-round points and the final point. Initial rows have undefined weights, shown as NaN rather than as artificial zeros.

    Repeated knots: a corner and a discontinuity

    For the quadratic with controls \((0,0),(1,2),(2,0),(3,2),(4,0)\) and knots \((0,0,0,1,1,2,2,2)\), the double interior knot at one allows a corner. Both pieces meet at \(P_2=(2,0)\), but their one-sided parameter derivatives are \(2(P_2-P_1)=(2,-4)\) and \(2(P_3-P_2)=(2,4)\).

    A degree-one spline with controls \((0,0),(1,1),(2,3),(3,2)\) and knots \((0,0,1,1,2,2)\) has full multiplicity at one. Its two straight pieces need not meet:

    \[C(1^-)=(1,1),\qquad C(1) = C(1^+)=(2,3).\]

    nextafter(1,0) and nextafter(1,2) select the adjacent representable parameters. The displayed rounding makes both look like one; their distinct span indices reveal the side convention. The curve helper leaves the jump visibly disconnected.

    Checking the theory

    Closed-form Bezier families and exact operation counts

    For degree \(p\geq1\), take \(P_j=(j/p,(j/p)^2)\), with zero and one each repeated \(p+1\) times. The weights are binomial probabilities for \(J\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(p,t)\). Using \(E[J]=pt\) and \(E[J^2]=p^2t^2+pt(1-t)\) gives the exact curve

    \[C_p(t)=\left(t,t^2+\frac{t(1-t)}p\right),\qquad \#\text{point interpolations}=\frac{p(p+1)}2.\]

    The curve is not exactly the parabola through the controls; for finite degree the second coordinate lies above it in the interior. We check degrees one through twelve at 101 parameters, and count actual recorded updates after the initial controls. For every degree we also scale the knot domain to [0, 1e-15], sample three points, and check the last sample against the exact endpoint control. The count measures the recurrence, not validation, span-search time or wall-clock speed.

    Use ten cubic controls on knots \((0,0,0,0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,7,7,7)\), then move only \(P_4\) upwards by two. Linearity gives a stronger prediction than a visual comparison:

    \[\Delta C(t)=B_{4,3}(t)(0,2),\qquad \Delta C(t)=0\quad\text{outside }[u_4,u_8]=[1,5].\]

    A larger construction: the original clamped cubic

    Return to the original six controls \((0,0),(1,4),(3,5),(5,1),(7,4),(8,0)\), with knots \((0,0,0,0,1,2,3,3,3,3)\). Simple interior knots give a \(C^2\) curve. At \(t=1.5\), span \(k=4\) uses controls one through four. Its first round has weights \((3/4,1/2,1/4)\) in increasing local-index order, its second has \((3/4,1/4)\), and its final weight is \(1/2\). The result is

    \[C(1.5)=(4,49/16)=(4,3.0625),\qquad C(0)=(0,0),\qquad C(3)=(8,0).\]

    The curve generally passes near interior controls rather than through them. The table also shows why uniformly spaced parameter values need not produce uniformly spaced points in the plane.     Conclusion

    The basis recursion explains both the algorithm and its geometric guarantees. A fixed span needs only a small control net, descending updates preserve the previous round, and convex weights give local control, affine equivariance and an absolute perturbation bound. The hand calculations distinguish Bezier weights from nonuniform knot weights. Repeated knots make the side convention and continuity guarantees essential.

    The generated families verify exact polynomial expectations and the \(p(p+1)/2\) interpolation count. The moved-control experiment verifies a whole support interval, not just one visually similar curve. Endpoint evaluation now uses the requested parameter directly. Uniform sampling remains a rendering choice, and stable evaluation does not solve fitting, knot selection or arc-length sampling.
