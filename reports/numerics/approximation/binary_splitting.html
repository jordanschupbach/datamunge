    Binary Splitting: Exact Fractions and a Finite Word-Size Limit

    Abstract

    Given a finite rational series, compute its exact fraction before converting it to floating point. Repeatedly merging balanced subranges organises the integer products and avoids rounding each term separately. We derive the recurrence for the exponential series, inspect a four-term product tree, and distinguish the omitted series tail from floating-point conversion error. Datamunge's implementation uses unsigned 64-bit integers: it supports at most 21 terms, returns an unreduced fraction, and rejects larger requests before arithmetic.

    The method

    The target is \(S_N=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}1/k!\). Set \(q_0=1\), and \(q_k=k\) for \(k>0\). Define each half-open subtree by

    \[Q(a,b)=\prod_{j=a}^{b-1}q_j,\qquad P(a,b)=\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}\prod_{j=k+1}^{b-1}q_j.\]

    The ratio is a *normalised* sum of reciprocal prefix products starting at a. It is not generally \(\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}1/k!\). For example, the leaf \([3,4)\) is \(1/3\), while the original factorial term is \(1/6\). Only the root starting at zero has the desired original-series interpretation: \(Q(0,N)=(N-1)!\) and \(P(0,N)/Q(0,N)=S_N\).

    At a leaf, \(P=1\) and \(Q=q_a\). Split a longer range at its midpoint m. Every suffix starting in the left half contains the entire right-half product, while suffixes starting in the right half do not. Therefore

    \[P(a,b)=P(a,m)Q(m,b)+P(m,b),\qquad Q(a,b)=Q(a,m)Q(m,b).\]

    Induction from the leaves proves the invariant. This merge is not ordinary addition of two unrelated fractions: that would have another denominator factor in the second numerator term. The normalisation above is why this recurrence works.

    Checking the theory: truncation and conversion are different errors

    For positive N, the omitted tail starts at \(1/N!\). Every subsequent ratio is at most \(1/(N+1)\), with a strictly smaller ratio after the first. A geometric majorant gives

    \[\frac1{N!}<e-S_N<\frac{N+1}{N\,N!}.\]

    This is an exact mathematical bound on the rational result, not on its rounded double conversion. The next program preserves the original convergence study through 18 terms and extends it to the supported limit. The printed bound is evaluated in floating point for display. double reference difference compares two rounded values; zero means equality to std::exp(1.0) at that precision, and cannot certify equality to the irrational number e.

    Tree size and the implementation's scope

    A full binary tree with N leaves has \(N-1\) merges, so recording stores \(2N-1\) nodes. Midpoint splitting gives maximum leaf depth \(\lceil\log_2 N\rceil\). Each merge uses two fixed-word multiplications and an addition. The implementation therefore performs \(O(N)\) word work, uses \(O(\log(N+1))\) recursion-stack space, and optionally records \(O(N)\) nodes. The exact fractions need not be reduced at intermediate stages.

    General multiprecision binary splitting can organise much larger products and benefit from fast integer multiplication. This API does not implement that arithmetic and is not a route to millions of digits. A balanced tree alone also does not establish a speed advantage over a sequential uint64 recurrence for these tiny supported counts. No timing or asymptotic digit-performance claim is inferred from the node table.

    Conclusion

    The exact root fraction reproduces the original examples, while its recorded subtrees explain the normalisation needed by the merge formula. The rational tail keeps shrinking after the double conversion reaches its precision limit. The report distinguishes an unreduced fraction, its rounded conversion and the irrational target, and makes the 21-term word-size limit explicit. These exact series controls need no observed dataset.
