    Dense Linear Algebra: Factorizations, Conditioning and Least Squares

    Abstract

    Almost every statistical fit ends in a dense linear algebra problem, and the way that problem is solved decides how many correct digits survive. This report surveys the dense toolkit in dm::linalg: the DenseMatrix type, the LU, Cholesky and Householder QR factorizations, and a new one-sided Jacobi singular value decomposition. Each method is derived, and their costs and error bounds are stated. The running example is least squares on dm::datasets::mtcars(): fuel consumption regressed on the other ten measurements, then on a polynomial in weight whose condition number grows from \(10^1\) to \(10^{15}\). The central result is that the normal equations square the condition number, \(\kappa(X^\top X)=\kappa(X)^2\). We prove this and then watch it happen. At degree 8 the normal equations keep about one correct digit, while QR keeps nine, and both errors match the predictions \(u\kappa^2\) and \(u\kappa\) once the harmless part of \(\kappa\), column scaling, is removed. The SVD then handles an exactly collinear design that defeats the other two methods. A timing study confirms the cubic cost of every factorization. Building the report fixed five library bugs. stats::LM's standard errors came from \(X^\top X\) and were 88% wrong at \(\kappa=10^9\). The Jacobi eigensolver's absolute stopping test returned wrong eigenvalues for small-scale matrices. QR's rank test was not scale invariant. Formulas such as mpg ~ . - model ignored the - model. And RPlot::text() colours were overridden by the SVG's stylesheet.

    The data

    Strong correlations make the columns of \(X\) nearly linearly dependent, and that is what the condition number measures. The rest of the report is about solving \(\min_\beta\lVert X\beta-y\rVert_2\) for such an \(X\).

    Dense matrices and their norms

    A dm::linalg::DenseMatrix<T> stores its \(m\times n\) elements row-major in one contiguous vector, so element \((i,j)\) sits at offset \(in+j\). The product \(C=AB\) is computed with an \(i\)-\(k\)-\(j\) loop. The innermost loop then runs along a row of \(B\) and a row of \(C\), both contiguous in memory, so a product of an \(m\times k\) and a \(k\times n\) matrix costs \(2mkn\) floating-point operations (flops) at close to the machine's streaming speed. The class has the three induced norms \(\lVert A\rVert_1\) (largest column sum), \(\lVert A\rVert_\infty\) (largest row sum) and, through the SVD, \(\lVert A\rVert_2=\sigma_1\), plus the Frobenius norm \(\lVert A\rVert_F=(\sum a_{ij}^2)^{1/2}\). Writing \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\) gives \(\lVert A\rVert_F^2=\sum_i\sigma_i^2\), and hence for a matrix of rank \(r\)

    \[\lVert A\rVert_2\le\lVert A\rVert_F\le\sqrt r\,\lVert A\rVert_2, \qquad \lVert A\rVert_2\le\sqrt{\lVert A\rVert_1\lVert A\rVert_\infty}.\]

    The second inequality holds because \(\sigma_1^2\) is the largest eigenvalue of \(A^\top A\), and every eigenvalue is bounded by any induced norm: \(\sigma_1^2\le\lVert A^\top A\rVert_1\le\lVert A^\top\rVert_1\lVert A\rVert_1=\lVert A\rVert_\infty\lVert A\rVert_1\). On the mtcars design (intercept plus ten predictors) these bounds are far from tight, because disp and hp dominate every norm:

    The least-squares problem

    Given \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\) with \(n\ge p\) and \(y\in\mathbb R^n\), find \(\beta\) minimizing \(f(\beta)=\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2\). Expanding, \(f(\beta)=y^\top y-2\beta^\top X^\top y+\beta^\top X^\top X\beta\), a quadratic with gradient \(2(X^\top X\beta-X^\top y)\) and constant Hessian \(2X^\top X\), which is positive semidefinite because \(v^\top X^\top Xv=\lVert Xv\rVert^2\ge0\). So the minimizers are exactly the solutions of the normal equations

    \[X^\top X\,\hat\beta=X^\top y \iff X^\top(y-X\hat\beta)=0 .\]

    The second form is the geometry. The residual \(r=y-X\hat\beta\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\), so \(X\hat\beta\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space. The minimizer is unique exactly when \(X^\top X\) is invertible, that is, when \(Xv=0\) only for \(v=0\) (full column rank). Three algorithms solve this problem, and they differ in what they do with the matrix.

    Normal equations and the Cholesky factorization

    The direct route forms the \(p\times p\) Gram matrix \(G=X^\top X\) (dm::linalg::gram, \(np^2\) flops, using symmetry) and solves \(G\hat\beta=X^\top y\). \(G\) is symmetric positive definite (SPD) when \(X\) has full rank, and SPD matrices have a Cholesky factorization \(G=LL^\top\) with \(L\) lower triangular and \(\ell_{ii}>0\).

    Existence, by induction on \(p\). Partition \(G=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g^\top\\ g&G_{22}\end{pmatrix}\). Positive definiteness gives \(g_{11}=e_1^\top Ge_1>0\), so set \(\ell_{11}=\sqrt{g_{11}}\) and \(\ell=g/\ell_{11}\). Then

    \[G=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{11}&0\\ \ell&I\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\ 0&S\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\ell_{11}&\ell^\top\\ 0&I\end{pmatrix}, \qquad S=G_{22}-\frac{gg^\top}{g_{11}} .\]

    The Schur complement \(S\) is SPD: for any \(w\ne0\) take \(v=(-g^\top w/g_{11},\,w)\), and then \(w^\top Sw=v^\top Gv>0\). By induction \(S=L_2L_2^\top\), which completes \(L\). Each pivot \(\ell_{ii}^2\) is a Schur complement's corner entry, so the factorization fails (a pivot \(\le0\)) exactly when \(G\) is not positive definite. cholesky() therefore doubles as a definiteness test, and its ok flag reports the outcome. The algorithm costs \(p^3/3\) flops and needs no pivoting: \(\ell_{ij}^2\le\sum_k\ell_{ik}^2=g_{ii}\), so no entry can grow. Two triangular solves \(Lz=X^\top y\), \(L^\top\hat\beta=z\) finish the job. In total the normal equations cost about \(np^2+p^3/3\) flops, the cheapest of the three methods.

    Householder QR

    The orthogonal route never forms \(G\). Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths, so for any orthogonal \(Q\), \(\lVert y-X\beta\rVert=\lVert Q^\top y-Q^\top X\beta\rVert\). If \(X=QR\) with \(R=\begin{pmatrix}R_1\\0\end{pmatrix}\), \(R_1\) upper triangular, and \(Q^\top y=\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}\), then

    \[\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2=\lVert c_1-R_1\beta\rVert_2^2+\lVert c_2\rVert_2^2 ,\]

    and the minimizer solves the triangular system \(R_1\hat\beta=c_1\), with residual norm \(\lVert c_2\rVert\). The factorization is built from Householder reflections \(H=I-2vv^\top\) with \(\lVert v\rVert=1\): \(H\) is symmetric and orthogonal (\(H^2=I-4vv^\top+4v(v^\top v)v^\top=I\)), and it reflects across the hyperplane orthogonal to \(v\). To map a vector \(x\) onto the first axis, \(Hx=\alpha e_1\), the reflection must preserve length, so \(\alpha=\pm\lVert x\rVert\), and \(v\) must be parallel to the difference \(x-\alpha e_1\). The sign is a numerical choice. Taking \(\alpha=-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\lVert x\rVert\) gives \(v\propto x+\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\lVert x\rVert e_1\), whose first entry is a sum of two numbers of the same sign, so nothing cancels even when \(x\) already points nearly along \(e_1\). Step \(j\) of qr() applies such a reflection to rows \(j..n\) of column \(j\), zeroing it below the diagonal without disturbing the columns already finished:

    x = (2.6200, 2.0575), x = 3.3313, R(0,0) from qr() = -3.3313

    Computing \(R\) costs \(2np^2-2p^3/3\) flops, about twice the normal equations when \(n\gg p\). (qr() also accumulates the full \(n\times n\) matrix \(Q\), another \(4n^2p\) flops. That is negligible at \(n=32\) but matters for tall data.) Every reflection is applied exactly in orthogonal form, so errors are not amplified. The computed \(\hat R\) is the exact factor of \(X+\Delta X\) with \(\lVert\Delta X\rVert\le c\,np\,u\lVert X\rVert\), where \(u=2^{-53}\approx1.1\times10^{-16}\) is the unit roundoff. This is backward stability.

    The singular value decomposition

    Every \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\) can be written \(X=U\Sigma V^\top\) with orthonormal columns \(U\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\), orthogonal \(V\in\mathbb R^{p\times p}\), and \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_p\ge0)\). (Take \(V\) and \(\sigma_i^2\) from the eigendecomposition of the symmetric matrix \(X^\top X\), and set \(u_i=Xv_i/\sigma_i\). These are orthonormal because \(u_i^\top u_j=v_i^\top X^\top Xv_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j)=\delta_{ij}\).) Substituting \(\beta=Vw\), \(U^\top y=c\):

    \[\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2=\sum_{i\le r}(c_i-\sigma_iw_i)^2+\text{const}, \qquad \hat\beta=\sum_{i\le r}\frac{u_i^\top y}{\sigma_i}\,v_i=X^{+}y .\]

    When the rank \(r<p\), the coordinates \(w_{r+1},\dots,w_p\) do not affect the residual, and setting them to zero gives the minimizer of smallest norm. That is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse solution. Numerically, singular values below \(\max(n,p)\,\varepsilon\,\sigma_1\), with \(\varepsilon=2u=2^{-52}\) the machine epsilon, are treated as zero (the LAPACK and NumPy convention; SVDDecomposition::rank() and solve() take another cut-off as an argument).

    The new dm::linalg::svd() uses one-sided Jacobi (Hestenes, 1958). It applies plane rotations to pairs of columns of \(X\) until all columns are mutually orthogonal. Then \(XV=W\) with orthogonal columns \(w_j\), so \(\sigma_j=\lVert w_j\rVert\) and \(u_j=w_j/\sigma_j\). For columns \(a_p,a_q\) with \(\alpha=\lVert a_p\rVert^2\), \(\beta=\lVert a_q\rVert^2\) and \(\gamma=a_p^\top a_q\), the rotated pair \(a_p'=ca_p-sa_q\), \(a_q'=sa_p+ca_q\) is orthogonal when

    \[a_p'^\top a_q'=(c^2-s^2)\gamma+cs(\alpha-\beta)=0 \iff t^2+2\zeta t-1=0,\quad t=\frac sc,\quad \zeta=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\gamma} .\]

    The smaller root \(t=\operatorname{sign}(\zeta)/(|\zeta|+\sqrt{1+\zeta^2})\), so \(|t|\le1\) and the rotation angle is at most \(\pi/4\), is the stable choice. Then \(c=1/\sqrt{1+t^2}\) and \(s=ct\). Each rotation is one step of the classical two-sided Jacobi method applied to \(X^\top X\) without forming it. It zeroes the \((p,q)\) entry and lowers the off-diagonal mass \(\sum_{i\ne j}(a_i^\top a_j)^2\) by \(2\gamma^2\), while the total \(\lVert X^\top X\rVert_F\) stays fixed, so cyclic sweeps converge, and the convergence is quadratic in the end. A sweep costs about \(3np^2\) flops (plus \(1.5p^3\) to accumulate \(V\)), and about 6 to 15 sweeps are typical (13 for the \(400\times400\) random matrix timed below). That is slower than bidiagonalization methods. In exchange the algorithm is simple, backward stable, and accurate in the relative sense for small singular values (Demmel and Veselić, 1992), which makes it a good reference solver.

    Conditioning: why \(X^\top X\) is the wrong matrix

    The 2-norm condition number of a full-rank \(X\) is \(\kappa(X)=\sigma_1/\sigma_p\). From the SVD, \(X^\top X=V\Sigma^2V^\top\), which is itself an eigendecomposition, so the singular values of \(X^\top X\) are \(\sigma_i^2\) and

    \[\kappa(X^\top X)=\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_p^2}=\kappa(X)^2 .\]

    For the least-squares problem, perturbation theory (Wedin, 1973) says that if \(X\) and \(y\) are perturbed by relative amounts \(\epsilon\), the solution changes by

    \[\frac{\lVert\delta\hat\beta\rVert}{\lVert\hat\beta\rVert} \lesssim\epsilon\left(\kappa+\kappa^2\tan\theta\right), \qquad \tan\theta=\frac{\lVert r\rVert}{\lVert X\hat\beta\rVert},\]

    where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(y\) and the column space. A backward-stable method (QR, SVD) commits errors equivalent to \(\epsilon\approx u\), so its error obeys this bound: of order \(u\kappa\) for a nearly consistent system, with a \(\kappa^2\) term weighted by the residual. The normal equations instead hand Cholesky the matrix \(X^\top X\). Merely rounding its entries is a relative perturbation of size \(u\) to a matrix with condition number \(\kappa^2\), so the error is of order \(u\kappa^2\) whatever the residual. Once \(\kappa\gtrsim u^{-1/2}\approx10^8\), the normal equations can return no correct digits at all.

    One refinement matters in practice. All three methods are unaffected by scaling the columns of \(X\). Scaling column \(j\) by \(d_j\) only rescales \(\hat\beta_j\), and the rounding errors scale with it. So the \(\kappa\) that governs accuracy is that of the column-equilibrated matrix \(XD^{-1}\), with \(D\) the column norms. By a theorem of van der Sluis, this is within a factor \(\sqrt p\) of the best \(\kappa\) achievable by any column scaling (dm::linalg::equilibrate_columns). A design whose \(\kappa\) comes only from mixing units, cubic inches beside thousands of pounds, is not ill-conditioned in any sense that matters.

    Three things stand out.

    • The *normal equations* track \(u\kappa^2\) closely. At \(d=8\) the prediction is \(u\kappa_{eq}^2\approx0.05\), and the measured error is 0.049: about one correct digit. From \(d=11\), \(\kappa_{eq}^2\) exceeds \(1/u\). The rounded \(X^\top X\) is then no longer positive definite, Cholesky meets a negative pivot, and the method gives up.
    • *QR* stays at or below \(u\kappa\) all the way, about nine correct digits at \(d=8\) and six at \(d=12\). The residual term \(u\kappa^2\tan\theta\) of the Wedin bound never appears, even though \(\tan\theta\approx0.1\): the real response and the consistent one give errors within a factor of ten of each other, usually much closer, with no trend in \(\kappa\). The bound is a worst case over all perturbations of size \(u\), and the structured errors of a Householder sweep do not come close to it on these data.
    • The *SVD* also stays below \(u\kappa\) until \(d=11\). There, \(\sigma_{\min}/\sigma_1\) of the raw design falls below the default cut-off \(32\varepsilon\approx7\times10^{-15}\) (the cut-off is not scale invariant), the SVD treats the design as rank-deficient, and it returns the minimum-norm solution of the truncated problem, a different answer (error near 1). Passing a smaller rcond to SVDDecomposition::solve recovers QR-like accuracy. Whether that is wise is a statistical question: the discarded direction is pure noise amplification.

    The plot is against the equilibrated \(\kappa\). The raw \(\kappa(X_d)\) is larger by up to a factor of 27,000 (at \(d=12\)), because the column \(w^{12}\) is about \(10^{9}\) times longer than the constant column. Against it, the measured slopes are much shallower than 1 and 2. That is the van der Sluis effect at work.

    A rank-deficient design

    Add a column that measures the same thing in other units: displacement in litres, \(\texttt{disp}/61.0237\). The design now has an exact linear dependence (up to the rounding of that one division). Coefficients are no longer identified, so any split of the displacement effect between the two columns fits equally well.

    The rounded quotient leaves \(\sigma_4/\sigma_1=2\times10^{-18}\), below the unit roundoff, so the problem is singular to working precision, and the three methods respond differently. Cholesky meets a non-positive pivot, so the normal equations stop. (With a less exact dependence a tiny positive pivot can slip through instead, and then the method returns large, meaningless coefficients.) QR detects that \(|R_{44}|\) is below its threshold \(\max(n,p)\,\varepsilon\max|R_{ii}|\) and refuses. The SVD drops the null direction and returns the minimum-norm solution. Its residual equals the reduced model's, and its two displacement coefficients satisfy \(b_\texttt{disp}+b_\texttt{disp\_l}/61.0237=\hat b_\texttt{disp}\) (the reduced model's effect per cubic inch), split so that the coefficient vector is as short as possible: \(b_\texttt{disp\_l}/b_\texttt{disp}=1/61.0237\). (QR's threshold used to be an absolute \(10^{-12}\), so whether a design counted as rank-deficient depended on its units. It is now relative to the largest \(|R_{ii}|\).)

    What it costs

    Every dense factorization costs \(O(n^3)\) for an \(n\times n\) matrix, but the constants differ: \(\tfrac13n^3\) flops for Cholesky, \(\tfrac23n^3\) for LU, \(\tfrac43n^3\) for the Householder \(R\) (plus about \(2n^3\) more for the explicit \(Q\)), \(2n^3\) for a product, and several sweeps of about \(4.5n^3\) for one-sided Jacobi.     The slopes sit within 0.1 of 3 (they vary a little between runs), as they should; any excess shows the \(400\times400\) matrices (1.3 MB) starting to spill out of the faster caches. The time ratios follow the flop counts: Cholesky takes about half as long as LU (predicted \(\tfrac12\)), and QR with its explicit \(Q\) about five times as long (predicted \((\tfrac43+2)/\tfrac23=5\)). The one exception is the product, which does three times LU's flops in about the same time, because its inner loop streams through contiguous rows while elimination's access pattern does not. The SVD is about a hundred times slower than Cholesky, a fair price for a reference solution, but it is not a routine solver for large problems. The LU backward error \(\lVert b-A\hat x\rVert/(\lVert A\rVert\lVert\hat x\rVert+\lVert b\rVert)\), of order \(u\), is the backward stability of partial pivoting seen directly.

    Conclusion

    • *Do not solve least squares by the normal equations unless the design is well conditioned.* Forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number. On mtcars polynomials the normal equations lost digits exactly as \(u\kappa^2\) predicts and failed outright past \(\kappa\approx10^8\). Householder QR costs about twice as much and keeps \(u\kappa\). That is why R's lm, and now every part of stats::LM including its standard errors, uses QR.
    • *Measure conditioning after equilibrating columns.* The raw \(\kappa\) of a design conflates units with collinearity. Scaling is free and harmless for every method, so only the equilibrated \(\kappa\) predicts accuracy. Centring then removes the large-mean collinearity with the intercept. For mtcars these two steps take \(\kappa\) from 12,213 to 15.6.
    • *Use the SVD when rank is in doubt.* It exposes the spectrum, returns a sensible minimum-norm answer for a singular design, and doubles as principal components analysis. Its cut-off is a modelling choice, as the \(d\ge11\) polynomials showed. It is also the slowest method by far.
    • *Limits.* These are textbook dense algorithms with plain loops: no blocking, BLAS or threads. They suit the \(p\lesssim\) few hundred problems of classical statistics. qr() forms an explicit \(n\times n\) \(Q\), which wastes \(O(n^2)\) memory on tall data. The Wedin bound's \(\kappa^2\tan\theta\) term, invisible here, can appear for adversarial data or large residuals, so QR is not immune to squaring either.
