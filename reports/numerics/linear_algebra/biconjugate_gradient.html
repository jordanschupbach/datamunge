    The Biconjugate Gradient Method

    Abstract

    The biconjugate gradient method extends conjugate gradients to non-symmetric systems by pairing the Krylov space of \(A\) with a shadow space of \(A^\top\). We derive its recurrences from two-sided Lanczos and show where its two kinds of breakdown come from. We then use it to compute PageRank on the directed 10-nearest-neighbour graph of 1,797 handwritten digits. That system contains a trap: the all-ones vector is an eigenvector of \(A^\top\), so the textbook shadow vector makes BiCG break down at its first step, and BiCGSTAB from \(x_0=0\) fails to converge as damping approaches one. With a random shadow vector BiCG needs 76 matrix products where power iteration needs 146 at \(\alpha=0.85\), and 406 where it needs 24,028 at \(\alpha=0.999\).

    From CG to BiCG

    Conjugate gradient solves \(Ax=b\) for symmetric positive definite \(A\) with short recurrences. Each step needs one product with \(A\) and a few vectors of memory, and the error is minimised in the \(A\)-norm over the Krylov space \(\mathcal K_k(A,r_0)=\operatorname{span}\{r_0,Ar_0,\dots,A^{k-1}r_0\}\). Both properties rest on symmetry. Lanczos's three-term recurrence builds an orthonormal basis of \(\mathcal K_k\) in which \(A\) is tridiagonal. For a non-symmetric \(A\), the orthonormal basis (Arnoldi) gives a full Hessenberg matrix instead. GMRES uses it, but its memory and work grow with every step.

    Two-sided Lanczos

    The biconjugate gradient method (Lanczos 1952, Fletcher 1976) keeps short recurrences by giving up orthogonality. It builds two sequences: residuals \(r_k\in\mathcal K_{k+1}(A,r_0)\) and shadow residuals \(\tilde r_k\in\mathcal K_{k+1}(A^\top,\tilde r_0)\), required to be bi-orthogonal,

    \[\tilde r_i^{\top}r_j=0\quad(i\ne j),\qquad \tilde p_i^{\top}Ap_j=0\quad(i\ne j),\]

    with search directions \(p_k\) and \(\tilde p_k\). Equivalently, \(x_k\in x_0+\mathcal K_k(A,r_0)\) is chosen so that \(r_k\perp\mathcal K_k(A^\top,\tilde r_0)\), a Petrov–Galerkin condition. Because \(\tilde r_i^\top Ar_j=(A^\top\tilde r_i)^\top r_j\) and \(A^\top\tilde r_i\) lies in \(\mathcal K_{i+2}(A^\top,\tilde r_0)\), the matrix \(\tilde R^\top AR\) is tridiagonal, and the recurrences are short, exactly as in CG.

    The recurrences

    Write \(x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kp_k\), so \(r_{k+1}=r_k-\alpha_kAp_k\), and mirror it with \(\tilde r_{k+1}=\tilde r_k-\alpha_kA^\top\tilde p_k\). Requiring \(\tilde p_k^\top r_{k+1}=0\), and using \(\tilde p_k^\top r_k=\tilde r_k^\top r_k\) (the earlier \(\tilde p_i\) are orthogonal to \(r_k\)), gives

    \[\alpha_k=\frac{\tilde r_k^{\top}r_k}{\tilde p_k^{\top}Ap_k}.\]

    New directions \(p_{k+1}=r_{k+1}+\beta_kp_k\) and \(\tilde p_{k+1}=\tilde r_{k+1}+\beta_k\tilde p_k\) must be bi-conjugate to the old ones, and only the last condition is not automatic:

    \[\beta_k=\frac{\tilde r_{k+1}^{\top}r_{k+1}}{\tilde r_k^{\top}r_k}.\]

    Each step costs one product with \(A\) and one with \(A^\top\). In exact arithmetic the residual is zero after at most \(n\) steps, unless the method breaks down first. If \(A\) is symmetric and \(\tilde r_0=r_0\), the two sequences coincide and BiCG is CG. algorithms::biconjugate_gradient takes \(A\) and \(A^\top\) as functions, so a sparse matrix never needs to be stored densely, and it records \(\|r_k\|/\|b\|\) at every step.

    Two kinds of breakdown

    The two divisions can fail with \(A\) perfectly non-singular.

    • *Pivot breakdown*, \(\tilde p_k^\top Ap_k=0\). For \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(b=e_1\), \(p_0=\tilde p_0=e_1\) and \(Ap_0=e_2\), so the first denominator is zero. A is orthogonal, and GMRES solves the system in two steps.
    • *Lanczos (serious) breakdown*, \(\tilde r_k^\top r_k=0\) with \(r_k\ne0\). The extreme case is a shadow space that stops growing, when \(\tilde r_0\) spans an \(A^\top\)-invariant subspace. The PageRank system below is such a case.

    Look-ahead Lanczos steps over the singular steps (Parlett, Taylor and Liu 1985). The simple remedy, used here, is a different shadow vector, usually a random one.

    PageRank on a nearest-neighbour graph

    dm::datasets::digits() holds 1,797 handwritten digits as 64-pixel images. We join every image to its 10 nearest neighbours (geometry::knn_lists), giving a directed graph, since being among my neighbours does not make me one of yours. Let \(P\) be the random walk that moves to a uniformly chosen out-neighbour, so \(P_{uv}=1/10\) for each edge \(u\to v\). PageRank with damping \(\alpha\) is the stationary distribution of the walk that follows \(P\) with probability \(\alpha\) and jumps to a uniformly random image otherwise. It solves

    \[\big(I-\alpha P^{\top}\big)x=\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\mathbf 1,\]

    a sparse, non-symmetric system with 11 non-zeros per column. algorithms::page_rank_system builds \(A=I-\alpha P^\top\), its transpose and the right-hand side.

    Where the eigenvalues lie

    If \(Pv=\mu v\) then \(A^\top v=(1-\alpha\mu)v\), so the spectrum of \(A\) is \(\{1-\alpha\mu\}\). Since \(P\) is stochastic, \(|\mu|\le1\): the eigenvalues of \(A\) lie in the disc of radius \(\alpha\) about 1, at distance at least \(1-\alpha\) from the origin. Power iteration \(x_{k+1}=\alpha P^\top x_k+b\) (the classic PageRank algorithm, Richardson's iteration on this system) shrinks the error by a factor \(\alpha\) per step at worst, needing about \(\log\varepsilon/\log\alpha\) steps: 142 for \(\varepsilon=10^{-10}\) at \(\alpha=0.85\), and 2,292 at \(\alpha=0.99\). A Krylov method instead builds a residual polynomial \(q_k\) with \(q_k(0)=1\) that is small on the whole spectrum. A few eigenvalues near \(1-\alpha\) cost one root each, and the rest are handled at the rate of the smaller disc that contains them. The digits graph has such outliers. Images of one digit mostly point at the same digit, so the walk nearly splits into ten slowly mixing blocks, and \(P\) has about ten eigenvalues close to 1.

    The left eigenvector trap

    Because \(P\) is row-stochastic, \(P\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\), and so \(A^\top\mathbf 1=(1-\alpha)\mathbf 1\). The all-ones vector, which is also the direction of \(b\), is an eigenvector of \(A^\top\). With the textbook choice \(\tilde r_0=r_0=b\), the shadow space \(\mathcal K_k(A^\top,b)\) is the line through \(\mathbf 1\) for every \(k\). Then

    \[\alpha_0=\frac{b^\top b}{b^\top Ab}=\frac{b^\top b}{(1-\alpha)\,b^\top b}=\frac1{1-\alpha},\qquad \tilde r_1=\tilde r_0-\alpha_0A^\top\tilde r_0=\tilde r_0-\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha}\tilde r_0=0 ,\]

    while \(r_1\ne0\): a serious breakdown at the first step. In floating point, \(\tilde r_1\) is rounding noise rather than zero, and an unguarded BiCG carries on with that noise as an accidental random shadow. BiCGSTAB fixes \(\tilde r_0=r_0\) for good. biconjugate_gradient detects a shadow that has collapsed far faster than the residual and reports a breakdown.

    Only 3.5% of the nearest-neighbour edges cross between digits, so the walk on \(P\) is nearly ten separate walks. The spectrum shows it. Twelve eigenvalues have modulus above 0.979, and two equal 1 to four decimals. A stochastic matrix has eigenvalue 1 once for each closed set of states, so the walk has at least two groups of images that it can enter but never leave. The bulk sits in a small cloud around \(-0.1\), well inside the unit disc. Most eigenvalues are complex, since \(P\) is not symmetric, but none is far from the real axis. For \(A=I-\alpha P^\top\), the bulk maps to a cloud near 1 and the cluster near \(\mu=1\) maps to about a dozen eigenvalues near \(1-\alpha\). The power method has to wait for \(\alpha^k\) to shrink. A Krylov method has to spend roughly one step per isolated eigenvalue before converging at the much faster rate of the cloud.

    PageRank itself is dominated by in-degree (correlation 0.90). The share by digit is close to the share of images, except that ones collect more (0.118) and eights less (0.084). The top image has 4.5 times the average PageRank.

    Convergence at \(\alpha=0.85\)

    We now solve the PageRank system at the usual \(\alpha=0.85\) to a relative residual of \(10^{-10}\) with:

    • power iteration;
    • BiCG with the textbook shadow \(\tilde r_0=b\), and with a random positive shadow;
    • BiCGSTAB (van der Vorst 1992, linalg::bicgstab), from \(x_0=0\) and from a random probability vector;
    • full GMRES (linalg::gmres).

    The two library solvers report only their final residual, so their histories are traced by re-running with an iteration cap of \(1,2,3,\dots\). Work is counted in products with \(A\) or \(A^\top\).

    All the converged runs agree with the reference page_rank to \(6\times10^{-13}\) or better, and their recursively updated residuals agree with the true residuals \(\|b-Ax\|/\|b\|\). The ranking follows the theory:

    • *BiCG with \(\tilde r_0=b\)* stops after one step with a breakdown, exactly as derived above. Its iterate is still \(x_1=b/(1-\alpha)\), which has the wrong scale and a residual of 3.3.
    • *BiCG with a random shadow* converges in 39 steps (78 products), about half the work of power iteration. Its residual is not monotone: BiCG minimises nothing, and the peaks are steps where \(\tilde r_k^\top r_k\) happened to be small.
    • *GMRES* is the benchmark, because it minimises \(\|r_k\|\) over the same Krylov space: 36 products, but it stores all 36 basis vectors and the orthogonalisation cost grows with \(k^2\).
    • *BiCGSTAB from \(x_0=0\)* has \(\tilde r_0=r_0=b\), the bad shadow. It does not break down outright, since its smoothing step moves \(r_k\) away from the eigenvector, but it needs 180 products with large jumps. From a random probability vector it needs 40, close to GMRES, and with constant memory.

    As damping approaches one

    The interesting regime is \(\alpha\to1\), where power iteration needs about \(1/(1-\alpha)\) times longer, and the near-unit eigenvalues of \(P\) put eigenvalues of \(A\) close to zero. We count the products needed to reach \(10^{-10}\) for \(\alpha\) from 0.5 to 0.999.

    Power iteration tracks its bound \(\lceil\log\varepsilon/\log\alpha\rceil\) to within 5%, so its work grows like \(1/(1-\alpha)\): a thousand-fold increase from \(\alpha=0.5\) to \(0.999\) costs 700 times more products. The Krylov methods grow far more slowly, roughly as the square root of \(1/(1-\alpha)\): 12 times for BiCG and 11 times for GMRES over the same range, so at \(\alpha=0.999\) BiCG is 59 times cheaper than power iteration. The reason is the polynomial argument above. Pushing \(\alpha\) towards 1 moves the dozen outlying eigenvalues towards zero, but each still costs only about one Krylov step to remove, and the bulk barely moves.

    BiCGSTAB from \(x_0=0\) is the warning. Its cost grows from 24 to 1,514 products by \(\alpha=0.95\), and at \(\alpha=0.99\) and \(0.999\) it produces NaN: with \(\tilde r_0\) along the eigenvector \(\mathbf 1\) of \(A^\top\), its inner products lose all information. Starting from a random probability vector, which changes \(r_0\) and so \(\tilde r_0\), it converges at every \(\alpha\) with work close to BiCG's.

    Summary

    • BiCG is the Petrov–Galerkin method on \(\mathcal K_k(A,r_0)\) against \(\mathcal K_k(A^\top,\tilde r_0)\). Bi-orthogonality makes \(\tilde R^\top AR\) tridiagonal, which gives CG-like two-term recurrences at the price of a product with \(A^\top\) per step and no minimisation property.
    • It can break down with \(A\) non-singular, either when \(\tilde p_k^\top Ap_k=0\) or when \(\tilde r_k^\top r_k=0\). On PageRank the default shadow \(\tilde r_0=b\) is a left eigenvector of \(A\), and BiCG breaks down at its first step, with \(\alpha_0=1/(1-\alpha)\) and \(\tilde r_1=0\) exactly. The same choice makes BiCGSTAB from zero fail as \(\alpha\to1\). A random shadow vector, or a random starting vector for BiCGSTAB, removes the problem.
    • On the digit graph, power iteration needs about \(1/(1-\alpha)\) times more work as damping rises, while BiCG, BiCGSTAB and GMRES grow roughly with its square root, because only a dozen eigenvalues of \(A\) approach zero. GMRES needs the fewest products. BiCG and BiCGSTAB need about 2 to 2.4 times as many, with constant memory.
    • algorithms::biconjugate_gradient is matrix-free, takes an optional shadow vector, and reports breakdowns instead of returning a silently wrong answer. algorithms::page_rank_system builds the sparse PageRank system and its transpose, and geometry::knn_lists builds the nearest-neighbour graph.
